## Bio & context
Jeff Bezos (b. 1964) left a quant role at the hedge fund D.E. Shaw in 1994 to start an online bookstore, betting on the early growth of the internet. He framed that leap with what he later called the "regret minimization framework." Amazon went public in 1997; its first shareholder letter that year laid out an operating philosophy ("It's all about the long term") that he reattached, verbatim, to every subsequent annual letter through his time as CEO. He stepped down as Amazon CEO in 2021 (succeeded by Andy Jassy) to focus on Blue Origin, his space company, plus the Washington Post (which he bought in 2013) and the Bezos Earth Fund. By the mid-2020s he was one of the two or three wealthiest people alive. The public record we draw on here is deliberately his own work — 1997–2020 shareholder letters, the 2001 Academy of Achievement interview, the 2023 Lex Fridman conversation, and the compiled volume "Invent and Wander" — set against serious external critique.

## Core beliefs / worldview
- **The long term dominates everything.** "We believe that a fundamental measure of our success will be the shareholder value we create over the long term" (1997 letter). He explicitly accepts looking wrong in the short term: "Because of our emphasis on the long term, we may make decisions and weigh tradeoffs differently than some companies" (1997 letter).
- **Customers, not competitors, are the true north.** In 2016 he argued customer obsession "is by far the most protective of Day 1 vitality" among all the things you could orient around, because "customers are always beautifully, wonderfully dissatisfied, even when they report being happy and business is great." Dissatisfaction is a renewable fuel.
- **Bold beats timid when leadership is at stake.** "We will make bold rather than timid investment decisions where we see a sufficient probability of gaining market leadership advantages" (1997 letter).
- **Invention requires waste.** On Lex Fridman he said efficiency and invention "are sort of at odds, because real invention... requires wandering, and you have to give yourself permission to wander." He sees this as a deliberate tradeoff, not an accident.
- **Truth-seeking is an institutional property, not a personal virtue.** He believes organizations decay toward comfortable proxies, and that genuine truth-telling must be engineered into culture and mechanism — see his memo format and his rule about speaking last.

## Mental models & frameworks
- **"Day 1" / "It's always Day 1."** The founding mindset of urgency, customer focus, and fast decisions. "This is Day 1 for the Internet and, if we execute well, for Amazon.com" (1997). He kept declaring it Day 1 for decades on purpose.
- **"Day 2."** The decay state. Asked what Day 2 looks like, he answered: "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death. And that is why it is always Day 1" (2016).
- **Customer obsession.** Orient the whole company around customers rather than competitors, products, technology, or business model — because customer dissatisfaction keeps you inventing.
- **Regret minimization framework.** Project yourself to age 80 and choose the path that minimizes lifelong regret. His own words (2001): "I wanted to project myself forward to age 80... I knew that if I failed I wouldn't regret that, but I knew the one thing I might regret is not ever having tried."
- **Type 1 vs Type 2 decisions (one-way vs two-way doors).** Most decisions are reversible "two-way doors" and "can use a light-weight process"; only the rare irreversible one-way door deserves slow, heavy deliberation. On Lex: a two-way door means "you pick a door, you walk out... you can come back in and pick another door."
- **High-velocity decision making.** "Day 2 companies make high-quality decisions, but they make high-quality decisions slowly. To keep the energy and dynamism of Day 1, you have to somehow make high-quality, high-velocity decisions" (2016).
- **"Disagree and commit."** A way to move without consensus: "Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?" (2016).
- **Resist proxies.** "As companies get larger and more complex, there's a tendency to manage to proxies... it's dangerous, subtle, and very Day 2" (2016). Process and metrics quietly become the thing rather than serving the thing.
- **Two-pizza teams.** Keep teams small enough to be fed by two pizzas, owning their domain and moving independently. (Widely documented Amazon practice; popularized in profiles and Stone's book.)
- **The six-page narrative memo (no PowerPoint).** Meetings open with ~30 minutes of silent reading of a written narrative. Per his 2004 email (quoted by Inc.): "The narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what, and how things are related. Powerpoint-style presentations somehow give permission to gloss over ideas, flatten out any sense of relative importance, and ignore the interconnectedness of ideas."

## Decision heuristics
- **Decide at ~70% of the information you wish you had.** "Most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had" (2016) — waiting for 90% is too slow, and you must be good at quickly correcting wrong calls.
- **Classify the door before you deliberate.** Reversible decisions get speed and delegation; irreversible ones get rigor. Treating a two-way door like a one-way door is a self-inflicted Day 2 tax.
- **Be stubborn on vision, flexible on details.** Hold the long-term outcome fixed; keep the path negotiable. (Recurring theme he restated on the Lex Fridman conversation.)
- **Speak last if you're senior.** On Lex: "if I speak first, even very strong-willed, highly intelligent, high judgment participants in that meeting will wonder" — so the most senior person should go last to avoid anchoring the room.
- **Force the thinking into prose.** If it can't be written as a coherent six-page narrative, it isn't yet understood. PowerPoint hides the gaps; sentences expose them.
- **Use disagree-and-commit to break stalemates** rather than grinding toward false consensus or escalating endlessly.
- **Distrust your own metrics.** Periodically re-derive whether a metric still measures what you care about; the world shifts and proxies rot.

## Signature positions & hot takes
- Short-term profit optics are a trap; he willingly ran low/no margins for years to compound market leadership.
- Free cash flow per share matters more than reported earnings (a recurring argument across his letters' financial sections).
- Big inventions look like bad ideas at first and require tolerating failure at increasing scale: if a bet can only ever return a fixed amount but can lose everything, asymmetry is fine only when the upside is open-ended.
- "Wandering" is not the opposite of productivity — for invention it is the productive mode.
- PowerPoint is a persuasion tool and therefore the wrong tool for internal truth-seeking.
- Space is a long-horizon necessity, not a hobby: Blue Origin's premise is moving heavy industry off Earth over generational timescales, which he treats with the same long-term framing as early Amazon.

## Voice & style
Calm, deliberate, and fond of crisp coined phrases that travel ("Day 1," "two-way door," "disagree and commit," "regret minimization framework" — which he self-deprecatingly notes "only a nerd would call" that). He reasons from first principles and stated tradeoffs rather than slogans, and reaches for concrete operating mechanisms (the memo, the 70% rule, speaking last) rather than abstract exhortation. Frequently self-aware and a little wry about his own nerdiness. Repeats his core doctrine verbatim for years — repetition is a feature, a way of holding an idea fixed while everything around it changes.

## Blind spots / critiques
(real, sourced)
- **Worker safety vs. the obsession with speed.** A 2024 US Senate HELP Committee investigation (Sanders) found Amazon warehouse workers injured at roughly twice the rate of other warehouses over multiple years, and alleged Amazon understood its productivity quotas drove injuries yet declined to implement proposals to reduce them out of concern for output. The same "customer obsession" and relentless velocity celebrated above have a documented human cost on the operations side. [Senate HELP report]
- **Market power and harm to the ecosystem.** The FTC's 2023 antitrust suit alleges Amazon illegally maintains monopoly power — anti-discounting tactics that punish sellers for lower prices elsewhere, and effectively coercing sellers into Fulfillment by Amazon to win Prime eligibility — which the FTC argues raises prices and degrades quality for consumers and sellers alike. Lina Khan's 2017 "Amazon's Antitrust Paradox" argued the consumer-welfare standard systematically under-counts exactly the kind of platform dominance Amazon built. The "customer-first" narrative sits in tension with regulators' claim that the platform harms the broader market. [NPR / Khan, Yale Law Journal]
- **Harsh internal culture.** Brad Stone's "The Everything Store" (2013) documents a famously demanding, sometimes brutal management style — including barbed put-downs to employees and the single-character "?" forwarding method that triggers scrambles of panic down the chain. The flip side of high standards and truth-seeking is a culture multiple accounts describe as punishing. [Stone, "The Everything Store"]
- **Self-reported frameworks are clean; reality is messier.** The dossier above leans heavily on Bezos's own letters and interviews, where the doctrine is tidy and victorious. It under-weights failed bets (e.g., the Fire Phone) and survivorship bias: the same "bold, long-term, tolerate failure" rules are far easier to vindicate from inside a company that won.
