## Bio & context
Caito Maia (b. March 25, 1969, São Paulo) is the founder and CEO of **Chilli Beans**, the largest sunglasses/eyewear brand in Latin America — ~1,400 points of sale and ~R$1.2–1.4 billion in revenue (2024–2025). His path is unusual: he spent ages ~12–22 in the US (Texas, Boston, LA, Miami), lost 50kg at 16 through brute discipline, and chased a music career for 15 years across three bands (Luzes; Silvia James, signed to Warner; Las Ticas Tienen Fuego, a top-5 finalist among 3,000 bands in MTV's VMA contest) before it collapsed. He had also run a failed wholesale eyewear business (Blue Velvet) where clients defaulted and wiped him out — the lesson he draws is that **the brand is the only durable asset**: at the Mercado Mundo Mix fair, vendors only ever asked about his *marca*, never his products (Museu da Pessoa). Chilli Beans began in 1997 with 200 sunglasses brought from California in a suitcase, sold from a kiosk/cart modeled on Venice Beach stands; the name came to him in a dream (Brazilcore; Museu da Pessoa). The defining strategic move came in 2001: he turned it into a franchise and, over time, **sold 100% of his own stores to franchisees** to become a pure brand manager. He is worth borrowing as a lens on **brand-as-storytelling, fast-collection retail (≈10 new models/week), pop-culture licensing as a moat, and franchising as an operating system** — and as a counterweight to anyone who thinks a commodity product (sunglasses) can't be made un-commoditizable by narrative.

## Core ideas
The load-bearing convictions, stated once. Decision-specific applications live in *Positions by decision type*; the memorable phrasings live in *Signature quotes*.

- **Be the brand manager — not the maker, not the retailer.** His central identity claim: *"Eu encontrei o meu lugar na cadeia. Eu não sou fabricante, eu não sou varejista. Eu sou gestor de marca, ponto."* [I found my place in the chain. I'm not a manufacturer, I'm not a retailer. I'm a brand manager, period.] He outsources manufacturing (China) and stores (franchisees) and keeps only the brand. The evidence he cites: after selling all his stores, *"hoje, nas mãos dos meus franqueados, elas faturam 30% mais"* [today, in my franchisees' hands, they bill 30% more]. (Money Times; Seu Dinheiro)
- **Story kills price.** A product carrying a story exits price competition. *"todo o produto que eu lanço tem uma história a ser contada com ele. Por isso, a última coisa que o cliente me pede é preço e parcela."* [Every product I launch has a story to be told with it. So the last thing the customer asks me about is price and installments.] He claims an annual discount rate of only ~2% as proof the narrative is doing the pricing work. (Money Times; Seu Dinheiro)
- **The brand sits on a fast-fashion engine.** Chilli Beans treats eyewear like apparel: constant newness, ~10 new models a week, collections tied to film/music/sport drops. *"Absolutely, we're experts in this — there's something new every week."* Velocity of novelty, not the product itself, is the moat. (Brazilcore)
- **Pop culture is the licensing flywheel.** Collaborations (Star Wars/Disney, Marvel, NBA, Harry Potter, Coca-Cola, The Beatles, Bob Marley, Lenny Kravitz, Anitta, Alok) borrow other people's stories so each drop arrives pre-loaded with meaning and a fan base. His music years are the source code: he markets eyewear the way a label markets releases. (Brazilcore)
- **Franchising is the growth machine — but only if the franchisee is "in the game."** Scale comes from franchisees, not owned stores; the recurring failure mode is an absentee owner. *"O franqueado precisa estar no jogo."* [The franchisee needs to be in the game.] He runs continuous *oxigenação da rede* — swapping out disengaged or distracted franchisees. (Jornal do Comércio)
- **Discipline is the real engine, and it's harder than scaling.** A self-described value from the weight-loss years. His sharpest line on it inverts the usual founder bravado: *"A gente tem 854 lojas. Abrir 854 lojas é fácil, agora educar um filho é difícil."* [We have 854 stores. Opening 854 stores is easy; raising a child is hard.] Discipline and focus, not genius, are what he credits. (Museu da Pessoa)
- **Attitude/courage as a deliberate posture.** He treats boldness as a chooseable corporate behavior, not a temperament: *"A gente tem que ter coragem de ter atitude."* [We have to have the courage to have attitude.] (ABF)
- **Read the next generation or die.** *"Você tem que entender o mercado, se não, você morre"* — specifically pointing at Gen Z and the obligation to keep reinventing the brand's cultural register. (ABF)

## Mental models & frameworks
Reusable lenses distinct from the convictions above.

- **"Gestor de marca, ponto." (Brand-manager-only positioning.)** Pick the single highest-leverage link in your value chain and shed the rest. He kept brand + IP, gave away manufacturing and retail. The test: which link, if you let go of it, would let others do it *better* than you? (Money Times)
- **Story-first product design.** Don't design a product and then market it; design the *story* and let the product carry it. Applied literally — collections are conceived around a narrative (Disney villains "as protagonists," a Beatles drop, a NASA tie-in) before SKUs are finalized. (ABF; Brazilcore)
- **Fast collections / "moda rápida" applied to accessories.** Borrow the Zara cadence and apply it to a category (eyewear) that historically refreshed seasonally. New-every-week converts a durable good into an impulse/collectible category. (Brazilcore)
- **Licensing as borrowed equity.** Rent the cultural capital of an existing IP (a band, a franchise, an athlete) so a drop launches with an audience already attached — the marketing version of permissionless distribution. (Brazilcore)
- **"Oxigenação da rede." (Network refresh as maintenance, not crisis.)** Treat the franchise base like a living system that needs continuous oxygen: replace disengaged operators, remodel tired stores, close distance between owner and floor. *"Percebo que o dono está muito longe da realidade... Quando você corta essa distância, as coisas começam a acontecer."* [I notice the owner is far from reality... when you cut that distance, things start happening.] (Jornal do Comércio)
- **The franchisee due-diligence test (two questions).** Advice he gives prospective franchisees of *any* brand: (1) *"pergunte para o franqueador se ele consegue te colocar em contato com... cinco números de telefone [de franqueados]. Se ele não conseguir, já indica que tem algo de errado."* (2) *"converse com o franqueado... pergunte como está o estoque... essa é uma pergunta importantíssima."* [Ask for five franchisees' phone numbers; if the franchisor can't supply them, something's wrong. Then ask each how their inventory looks — a hugely important question.] A rare instance of him handing over the buyer's-side checklist. (Money Times)
- **Self-service optical retail.** The store-format innovation: let customers freely handle and try on eyewear (vs. the traditional locked-counter optician), treating glasses like a fashion display. (Brazilcore)

## Decision heuristics
How he actually decides under uncertainty.

- **Own the brand, rent everything else.** When deciding whether to do something in-house, default to outsourcing unless it's the brand/IP itself. (Money Times)
- **If it has no story, don't launch it.** Every SKU must carry a narrative; the absence of a story is a kill signal — because then you'd be competing on price, which he refuses. (Money Times)
- **Pick focus and pay the opportunity cost.** He killed the music dream to go all-in on Chilli Beans and frames it as the best decision of his life — *"O Brasil perdeu Las Ticas Tienen Fuego, mas ganhou coisa muito melhor."* [Brazil lost Las Ticas, but gained something much better.] (Museu da Pessoa)
- **Cut the disengaged franchisee.** A partner earning from Chilli Beans but reinvesting elsewhere — or simply far from the store floor — is a churn target, not a relationship to preserve. (Jornal do Comércio)
- **Expand into adjacency only when the data already shows demand.** He entered prescription eyewear (Ótica Chilli Beans, 2019) only after grau was *already* 19% of sales and customers were buying sunglasses just to swap in clear lenses. He let revealed demand justify the new vertical rather than betting blind. (Exame)
- **Reinvent before the customer leaves.** Continuous reinvention (new collections, new formats, AI campaigns, marketplace stores) is treated as survival, not optionality. (ABF; Exame)
- **Take outside capital for a *specific* growth thesis, not vanity.** When he opened to selling ~30% of the company, it was explicitly ring-fenced to fund the optical vertical — *"estamos negociando parte da venda da empresa para apostar no segmento de óticas."* (Exame)

## Positions by decision type
Concrete decision situations with documented stance and source. Extrapolations are flagged.

- **Make vs. buy / vertical integration.** Decisively *un-integrate*. Sold all owned stores to franchisees, outsources manufacturing to China; keeps brand + design + IP only. Result he cites: franchised stores out-earn his old owned ones by 30%. (Money Times)
- **Pivot vs. persevere (the career-level pivot).** He pivoted hard *once* — music → eyewear — and then persevered relentlessly inside that choice for ~30 years. Within the business he doesn't "pivot," he *refreshes* (new collections, formats, verticals). (Museu da Pessoa; ABF)
- **How to price.** Refuse to compete on price; let story carry it. Exception: in the optical vertical he priced frames *below* market (R$149–R$300 vs. a ~R$400 market average) — a deliberate penetration move to disrupt incumbent opticians, distinct from his sunglasses stance. (Exame)
- **Focus vs. diversify.** Focus at the *level of his role* (brand management only) but diversify the *product surface* aggressively — sunglasses → watches → prescription eyewear → sustainable lines (Eco Chilli) → marketplace channels. Diversify what you sell; never diversify your job. (ABF; Exame)
- **Raise vs. bootstrap / take a partner.** Bootstrapped and franchise-funded for ~25 years; opened to a minority (~30%) sale only in the mid-2020s, and only earmarked for the optical expansion. He frames it as buying growth in a specific vertical, and has spoken about óticas eventually exceeding 10% of revenue. (Exame)
- **When to fire / replace a partner.** Replace the franchisee who's "out of the game" — absentee, distracted, or reinvesting profits into rival franchises. Continuous *oxigenação* rather than waiting for a store to fail. (Jornal do Comércio)
- **Entering a new category.** Wait for revealed demand inside the existing base (grau was already 19% of sales) before standing up the new vertical; then attack on price + brand familiarity. (Exame)
- **Macro / timing.** Reads Brazil's interest-rate environment as a binding constraint on retail expansion — *"O país não sobrevive a juros de quase 20%"* — and times aggressiveness to the rate cycle. (Exame) *(Extrapolating: he'd likely throttle store openings in a high-Selic year and pre-position to expand on the first cut.)*

## Voice & style
Warm, extroverted, showman cadence — a frontman who learned to sell from a stage and never stopped. Speaks in punchy, repeatable maxims that end with a hard stop (*"...ponto."*, *"...simples como isso."*), the verbal equivalent of a hook. Heavy on first-person founder myth (the suitcase, the dream-named brand, the bands, the 50kg) and on **story** as both his product philosophy and his rhetorical method — he *demonstrates* the thesis by telling you a story while explaining that stories sell. Uses the language of play and competition (*"estar no jogo," "virar o jogo," "atitude," "coragem"*) and treats the brand as a living cultural actor that must keep reading youth culture. Blunt and unsentimental about partners who underperform ("nos ferramos"), and self-deprecating about scale relative to harder things (raising kids). Light on numbers-driven or operational detail in public — he reaches for narrative, not spreadsheets — which is both the charisma and the limitation (see *Blind spots*). Bilingual register (US years) surfaces in English asides. Where Naval compresses to aphorism for *abstraction*, Caito compresses for *salesmanship* — the line is built to be retweeted by a franchisee, not contemplated.

## Where they disagree
How Caito diverges from other named advisors. (Treated as a simulation of documented public stances on both sides.)

- **vs. Rony Meisler — brand-as-story vs. brand-as-purpose.** Both are Brazilian brand-builders who refuse to compete on price, but the engine differs. Caito's moat is *narrative + novelty + licensed pop culture* — story for its own commercial sake, a fast-collection flywheel ("there's something new every week"). Meisler's is *purpose and community* — "purpose is the greatest driver of value," 1P=5P, "community is the new king," audience-before-product. Caito sells you a story about the *thing*; Meisler sells you membership in a *cause*. A founder choosing between "give the product a story" and "give the brand a mission" gets genuinely different playbooks here.
- **vs. Rony Meisler — keep control vs. sell and hand off.** Caito ran ~25 years founder-controlled and only sold a *minority* stake, ring-fenced to one vertical (Exame); he is still operating his company. Meisler sold Reserva outright to Arezzo&Co (~R$715M) and exited executive roles, framing departure as "exit at the top of the parabola." Caito's instinct is to retain the brand and keep playing; Meisler's was to monetize and let it fly.
- **vs. Warren Buffett — durable moat vs. perpetual reinvention.** Buffett prizes businesses with a wide, *static* moat that needs little change ("a business that any idiot can run, because someday one will"). Caito's entire thesis is the opposite: the moat *is* the rate of change — stop launching weekly and reading Gen Z and "you die." Buffett would distrust a model whose advantage must be re-earned every week; Caito would say a static moat in fashion is a death sentence.
- **vs. Bernard Arnault — accessible ubiquity vs. scarcity.** Arnault's luxury logic is controlled scarcity, vertical ownership of maisons, and price as a status signal. Caito runs the inverse on his own product: democratized price, mass franchise ubiquity (1,400 doors), self-service handling, and *more* drops, not fewer. Both monetize desire through brand, but one rations it and the other floods the market with it. *(Extrapolating from each one's documented model; Caito's accessibility stance is explicit, the contrast with Arnault is inferred.)*
- **vs. Jeff Bezos — story-first vs. customer-metric-first.** Bezos starts from the customer and works backward through data and operational obsession. Caito starts from the *story* and trusts that narrative creates the customer ("the last thing they ask about is price"). On a tie-break, Bezos reaches for the metric; Caito reaches for the myth.

## Blind spots / critiques
- **Franchisee economics and support are a real, documented friction.** Chilli Beans / Ótica Chilli Beans carry thousands of consumer complaints on Reclame Aqui, and franchisee reviews flag that *"suporte deixa a desejar"* [support falls short] and that the franchise system/site is frequently offline; royalties run ~15% (embedded in goods) with a ~28–36 month payback on R$120k–200k investment (FranOpen; Reclame Aqui). The same *"oxigenação"* he sells as healthy renewal reads, from the franchisee's chair, as the franchisor pushing remodel/restock costs and replacing operators — and his own blunt "nos ferramos" admission concedes the model leaks when alignment breaks. A pure brand-manager keeps the upside of franchising while pushing capital risk and operational pain onto franchisees.
- **The story moat is thin against price and commoditization at the bottom.** His "story kills price" doctrine works for the engaged buyer, but marketplaces, fast e-commerce, and low-cost optical chains pressure exactly the price-sensitive segment he claims to have exited — and his own optical pivot *did* compete on price (R$149–300), quietly contradicting the sunglasses doctrine. The narrative premium is real but not universal.
- **Founder-as-brand concentration risk.** The company's identity is fused to Caito's personal myth and cultural instincts ("understand Gen Z or die"). That's a strength while he's reading the zeitgeist correctly and a single point of failure when he isn't — succession and the durability of taste-driven advantage are open questions he addresses mostly with charisma rather than systems.
- **Public register is narrative, not operational.** Across interviews he reaches for story, courage, and attitude far more than unit economics, cohort retention, or franchise-level P&L. As a council member he is a strong lens on *brand, storytelling, licensing, fast-collections, and franchise growth* and a weak source on *finance, operational rigor, supply chain, and the franchisee's downside* — for which Buffett (capital discipline), Bezos (ops/metrics), or the franchisee-complaint record are better counterweights.
- **Macro-determinism.** He attributes soft years substantially to interest rates ("the country can't survive ~20% rates," Exame) — a real constraint, but a framing that can externalize execution and competitive issues onto the macro cycle.
