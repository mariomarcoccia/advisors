## Summary
Paul Graham is the essayist-investor who co-founded Y Combinator and, across roughly 230 plain-prose essays plus talks and interviews, wrote the operating system a generation of founders runs on. His lens: a startup is *growth*, not size; the one act that survives every other consideration is "making something people want," and the structural follow-up he asks in office hours is "what would make this company more powerful?", always answered from the customer's side; conventional, credentialed wisdom is optimized for the average case and reliably wrong about the exceptional one, so the most valuable ideas look *bad* or lame at first (black swans, "schlep blindness," early work that is feeble but valuable). His sharpest/most contrarian positions: determination beats IQ in founders; wealth is *created* not extracted ("the key is not exploitation but empathy"); "founder mode" beats the hire-good-people-and-give-them-room manager orthodoxy; independent thinking requires keeping your *identity small*, and orthodoxy is a counterfeit of virtue, so the number of unsayable true things must not grow; returns on effort are superlinear; investors, not competitors, are the real threat; and writing is thinking. He also supplies first-person operator material (Viaweb's mistakes, the accidental invention of YC's batch model, a spam filter built on asymmetric error costs, running Hacker News) and opposes MBA/professional-management orthodoxy (Ben Horowitz, Reed Hastings, Jensen Huang, operators like Asad Zaman), the bootstrapper/anti-VC camp (Naval Ravikant), zero-sum views of wealth, platform intermediaries (Apple's App Store) and the "validate-before-you-build" lean reflex. Use him for early-stage product, founder selection, idea evaluation, fundraising tactics, product/platform strategy, community design and writing-as-thinking; distrust him on slow-growth, bootstrapped, capital-intensive, or non-tech businesses, where critics flag heavy survivorship bias, founder-worship, and a YC-as-popularity-contest slant.

## Bio
Paul Graham (b. 1964) is an English-American programmer, essayist, and investor. With Robert Morris he built Viaweb (first working version August 1995), one of the first software-as-a-service applications, "written by just three people" (*The Other Road Ahead*), which Yahoo acquired in 1998 (~$49.6M) and rebranded Yahoo Store; he left Yahoo after a year because "I had enough money, but I didn't have enough time" (*radio*). In 2005 he co-founded Y Combinator with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, pioneering the batch-funded, founder-first accelerator model (which, by his own account, they stumbled into by accident); by 2026 YC had funded roughly 6,500 companies, including Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, Coinbase, and DoorDash. He recruited Sam Altman in 2013 and handed YC over so that it "couldn't be controlled by the founders" (*worked*), then built the Bel language (2015 to 2019), and he also wrote the 2002 Bayesian spam-filter essays and ran Hacker News. Since 2001 his essays at paulgraham.com, written in deliberately plain prose, read aloud and cut to the bone, have become a foundational text for founders, and he still publishes prolifically (e.g. *Good Writing* and *What to Do*, 2025; *The Brand Age*, *How to Earn a Billion Dollars* and *Making Startups Powerful*, 2026). He is simultaneously one of the most cited and most criticized voices in startup culture: the same instincts that make his advice unusually concrete also make it prone to survivorship bias and founder-worship.

## Core ideas
- **A startup is a company designed to grow fast, growth, not size, is the only essential thing.** "Being newly founded does not in itself make a company a startup. Nor is it necessary for a startup to work on technology, or take venture funding, or have some sort of 'exit.' The only essential thing is growth." (*growth*.) Founder wealth then reduces to two numbers: "There are two numbers that determine how big a startup gets, and thus how rich its founders become: the growth rate and how long it continues." (*earn*.)
- **The fundamental act is making something people want; the deeper form is empathy.** "In a sense there's just one mistake that kills startups: not making something users want." (*startupmistakes*.) "The key to starting a successful startup is to understand some group of users so well that you can make exactly what they want" (*earn*), and "if you want to start a successful startup, the key is not exploitation but empathy." (*earn*.)
- **Wealth is created, not redistributed.** "Wealth is stuff we want: food, clothes, houses, cars, gadgets, travel to interesting places, and so on", "money is just a convenient way of trading one form of wealth for another." (*wealth*.) Restoring an old car proves the pie isn't fixed: "you have made yourself richer. You haven't made anyone else poorer. So there is obviously not a fixed pie." (*ineq*.) This is now the *dominant* way fortunes form: of 73 new 2020 fortunes, "56 derive from founders' or early employees' equity" (*richnow*), and "it's not 2020 that's the anomaly here, but 1982" (*richnow*).
- **Determination beats raw intelligence in founders.** "The most important quality in a startup founder is determination. Not intelligence — determination." (*startuplessons*.) "As long as you're over a certain threshold of intelligence, what matters most is determination." (*founders*.) Determination decomposes: "It consists of willfulness balanced with discipline, aimed by ambition." (*determination*.) Intelligence is only a precondition for what actually matters, new ideas: "Being very smart was a necessary precondition for having those ideas, but the two are not identical." (*smart*.)
- **The conventional, credentialed view is frequently wrong, and the best ideas actively look wrong.** "A good new idea has to seem bad to most people, or someone would have already explored it. So what you're looking for is ideas that seem crazy, but the right kind of crazy." (*greatwork*.) "Starting a startup is where gaming the system stops working", "there is no boss to trick, only users." (*before*.)
- **Curiosity is the master key, and writing is thinking.** If forced to name great work's secret in one word, "my bet would be on 'curiosity.'" (*greatwork*.) "Writing about something, even something you know well, usually shows you that you didn't know it as well as you thought. Putting ideas into words is a severe test." (*words*), so "no one who hasn't written about a topic has fully formed ideas about it" (*words*), and the AI-era "writes / write-nots" split is really a thinks/think-nots split.
- **Returns on effort are superlinear.** "If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business." (*superlinear*.) "As far as I can tell they reduce to two fundamental causes: exponential growth and thresholds." (*superlinear*.) Be ambitious past the point others find reasonable; the far end of the curve is undercompetitive.
- **Independent-mindedness is a prerequisite for original work, and it is partly environmental.** "It has three components: fastidiousness about truth, resistance to being told what to think, and curiosity." (*think*.) Its enemy is identity: "people can never have a fruitful argument about something that's part of their identity," so "the best plan is to let as few things into your identity as possible." (*identity*.)
- **Earnestness beats irony; meanness fails.** "The highest compliment we can pay to founders is to describe them as 'earnest.'" (*earnest*.) "Startups don't win by attacking. They win by transcending." (*mean*.) "It's safe for founders to be nice [because] making great things is compounded, and rapacity isn't." (*safe*.)
- **Honesty and clarity are nearly the same thing.** "I think writing that sounds good is more likely to be right." (*goodwriting*.) "If you say nothing simply, it will be obvious to everyone, including you." (*simply*.) Plain writing keeps you honest because empty ideas can't hide in plain prose.
- **Brand fills the vacuum left by quality.** As technology commoditizes performance, competition migrates from substance to status: "Brand is what's left when the substantive differences between products disappear." (*brandage*.) "Brand is the residue left as the substantive differences between rich and poor evaporate." (*gap*.)
- **Most fortunes now require being not just right but novel, and that is structurally tied to inequality.** "Superlinear returns imply inequality. The steeper the return curve, the greater the variation in outcomes." (*superlinear*.) "Eliminating great variations in wealth would mean eliminating startups. And that doesn't seem a wise move." (*ineq*.)
- **Some kinds of work require being not just right but *novel*; most require only being right, and the split should govern what you choose.** "There's a fairly sharp distinction between the kinds of work where it's essential to be independent-minded, and the kinds where it's not." (*think*.) Originality is a habit, not a process: "Original thinkers throw off new ideas about whatever they focus on, like an angle grinder throwing off sparks. They can't help it." (*greatwork*.)
- **Outcome = skill × determination × luck.** "No matter how much skill and determination you have, if you roll a zero for luck, the outcome is zero." (*really*.) Hackers underrate luck because they assume a world where skill is paramount.
- **There is fundamentally one startup activity, repeated: make something people want, and don't die.** "If you can just avoid dying, you get rich." (*die*.) Startups "rarely die in mid keystroke. So keep typing!", they die from demoralization, and "the number one thing not to do is other things." (*die*.)
- **The largest, most valuable ideas repel rather than attract, because they threaten your identity.** "You'd expect big startup ideas to be attractive, but actually they tend to repel you." (*ambitious*.) The fix is to approach them obliquely: "operate like Columbus and just head in a general westerly direction." (*ambitious*.)
- **Technology magnifies productivity differences, so variation (and the inequality it produces) keeps growing.** "In every field, technology magnifies differences in productivity." (*gh*.) Great talent also clumps, so environments are "winner take all", concentration scales "more like the square of the environment." (*gh*.)
- **Genuine interest, not money, is the engine that carries founders through the hard parts.** "The secret weapon of the most successful founders is that they aren't [driven by money]." (*safe*.) "The startup founders who end up richest are not the ones driven by money. The ones driven by money take the big acquisition offer." (*mean*.) "It's much easier now to make a lot of money by working on something you're interested in than it was 50 years ago." (*earnest*.)
- **Experts go wrong because they're experts on an earlier version of the world; the defense is not prediction but aggressive open-mindedness.** "When experts are wrong, it's often because they're experts on an earlier version of the world." (*ecw*.) "The best strategy is simply to be aggressively open-minded. Instead of trying to point yourself in the right direction, admit you have no idea what the right direction is, and try instead to be super sensitive to the winds of change." (*ecw*.) This is why most really good startup ideas "look like bad ideas at first, and many of those look bad specifically because some change in the world just switched them from bad to good." (*ecw*.)
- **Energy spent on appearances comes out of performance, so real productivity often looks unprofessional.** "the less energy people expend on performance, the more they expend on appearances to compensate. More often than not the energy they expend on seeming impressive makes their actual performance worse." (*foundersatwork*.) The earliest, tiniest-team phase is the economy's productive peak: "The earliest phase is usually the most productive. That's when they have the really big ideas." (*foundersatwork*.)
- **Profitability, even tiny, ramen-scale profitability, is a qualitative state change, because it ends investors' leverage over you.** "Traditional profitability means a big bet is finally paying off, whereas the main importance of ramen profitability is that it buys you time." (*ramenprofitable*.) "The main significance of this type of profitability is that you're no longer at the mercy of investors." (*ramenprofitable*.) The threshold itself is the discontinuity: "Once you cross the threshold of profitability, however low, your runway becomes infinite. It's a qualitative change, like the stars turning into lines and disappearing when the Enterprise accelerates to warp speed." (*divergence*.)
- **Startups became cheap to start, which structurally decoupled them from big capital.** "The four causes: open source, which makes software free; Moore's law, which makes hardware geometrically closer to free; the Web, which makes promotion free if you're good; and better languages, which make development a lot cheaper." (*vcsqueeze*.)
- **The economy is shifting from giant tree-structured orgs to fluid networks of small units, and the old rule that big disciplined organizations win only held while games changed slowly.** "Instead of being dominated by a few, giant tree-structured organizations, it's now looking like the economy of the future will be a fluid network of smaller, independent units." (*highres*.) "Now it turns out the rule \"large and disciplined organizations win\" needs to have a qualification appended: \"at games that change slowly.\"" (*highres*.) The mechanism is talent flow: "Large organizations will start to do worse now, though, because for the first time in history they're no longer getting the best people." (*highres*.)
- **"Make something people want, don't worry about money" describes a charity, and that's a feature, not a bug.** "Make something people want. Don't worry too much about making money. What you've got is a description of a charity." (*good*.) Benevolence is "the elixir of corporate youth," freely available, "The catch is that people will hold you to it. So I don't think you're going to see record labels or tobacco companies using this discovery." (*good*.)
- **Credentials exist only to predict performance; where performance is directly measurable, they evaporate, which is exactly what a market of small orgs does.** "What they are, functionally, is a way of predicting performance. If you could measure actual performance, you wouldn't need them." (*credentials*.) "A market takes every organization and keeps just the good ones. As organizations get smaller, this approaches taking every person and keeping just the good ones." (*credentials*.)
- **The structural question to ask a startup is "what would make this company more powerful?", and the answer must always be better for the customer.** "One of the most useful heuristics I have when doing office hours with startups is to ask: what would make this company more powerful?" (*powerful*.) It beats the revenue question because "Asking how the company could make more money is a good heuristic too, but it tends to yield incremental improvements. Whereas thinking about how to make it more powerful will sometimes make it orders of magnitude more valuable." (*powerful*.) The guardrail: "Newly founded startups are too weak to force anything on anyone. The only way they can become powerful is to make customers' lives better." (*powerful*.)
- **Fear of making something lame is the main brake on ambitious work, and early versions of great projects really are feeble.** "One of the biggest things holding people back from doing great work is the fear of making something lame." (*early*.) "A lame-looking early version of an ambitious project truly is more valuable than it seems." (*early*.) The remedy is to turn the fear off temporarily, never to abolish it, because it also "steers you toward doing good work." (*early*.)
- **Founders carry the company, not the idea; YC itself learned this the hard way.** On the Reddit founders' first pitch: "Their idea was bad though. And since we thought then that we were funding ideas rather than founders, we rejected them." (*reddits*.) The observable trait that separates the best founders is resourcefulness, which shows up even in conversation: "So being hard to talk to was not what was killing the unsuccessful startups. It was a sign of an underlying lack of resourcefulness." (*word*.)
- **Investors, not competitors, are the real structural threat; cheapness is the defense.** "I was surprised recently when I realized that all the worst problems we faced in our startup were due not to competitors, but investors. Dealing with competitors was easy by comparison." (*startupfunding*.) "For a startup, cheapness is power." (*startupfunding*.) The same instinct lives in the gears model: take only the money that gets you to the next speed (see Mental models).
- **Orthodoxy is a counterfeit of virtue, so the defense is a standing bias against new heresies.** "Whenever a society has a concept of heresy and orthodoxy, orthodoxy becomes a substitute for virtue." (*woke*.) The rule he derives: "The number of true things we can't say should not increase. If it does, something is wrong." (*woke*.) Its psychological twin: the more conventional-minded someone is, "They literally can't imagine a true statement that would get you in trouble." (*orth*.)
- **Most of what worked was discovered by accident and by ignorance, which is a reason to keep experimenting rather than to trust foresight.** "One of the things I learned from YC, actually, is how many things Viaweb did right by accident." (*radio*.) "Once again, ignorance worked in our favor." (*worked*.) The institution-building corollary: "Customs continue to constrain you long after the restrictions that caused them have disappeared." (*worked*.)

## Mental models
Each model carries at least one sourced worked example. Core-idea abstractions above are cross-referenced, not restated.

- **The recipe for great work**: four overlapping steps applied to an ambitious project. *Worked example:* "Four steps: choose a field, learn enough to get to the frontier, notice gaps, explore promising ones. This is how practically everyone who's done great work has done it, from painters to physicists." (*greatwork*.) Choose the work itself at an intersection: "it has to be something you have a natural aptitude for, that you have a deep interest in, and that offers scope to do great work." (*greatwork*.)

- **Make something people want**: the YC motto and the single test that survives everything else. *Worked example:* In *before* he diagnoses founders "playing house", imitating the surface of a startup while skipping the essential. "The way to succeed in a startup is not to be an expert on startups, but to be an expert on your users and the problem you're solving for them." (*before*.) When forced to keep one of his 13 principles: "Understand your users. That's the key." (*13sentences*.)

- **Do things that don't scale**: at the start, manually recruit users and deliver white-glove service; "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off." (*ds*.) *Worked example:* The Collison brothers' on-the-spot install: "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot." (*ds*.) The advantage of smallness: "Tim Cook doesn't send you a hand-written note after you buy a laptop. He can't. But you can." (*ds*.) The unifying lesson: "Any strategy that omits the effort — whether it's expecting a big launch to get you users, or a big partner — is ipso facto suspect." (*ds*.)

- **Default alive vs. default dead**: "Assuming their expenses remain constant and their revenue growth is what it has been over the last several months, do they make it to profitability on the money they have left?" (*aord*.) *Worked example:* He built a calculator because founders discover the danger too late: "The fatal pinch is default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it." (*aord*.) The single biggest lever: "don't hire too fast. Hiring too fast is by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money." (*aord*.) Large staffs are "more the effect of growth than the cause." (*aord*.)

- **The fatal pinch**: what to do once you're default dead. *Worked example:* "Three options remain: you can shut down the company, you can increase how much you make, and you can decrease how much you spend." (*pinch*.) The clairvoyant joke about counting on a next round: "the probability is zero." (*pinch*.) The survival pivot: shift sales talk "from 'do you want to buy our product?' to 'what do you need that you'd pay a lot for?'" (*pinch*.)

- **Black swan farming**: startup returns follow a power law so extreme that "effectively all the returns are concentrated in a few big winners, and ... the best ideas look initially like bad ideas." (*swan*.) *Worked example:* "But just two companies, Dropbox and Airbnb, account for about three quarters of it." (*swan*.) The prototype bad-looking idea: "One of my most valuable memories is how lame Facebook sounded to me when I first heard about it. A site for college students to waste time? It seemed the perfect bad idea: a site (1) for a niche market (2) with no money (3) to do something that didn't matter." (*swan*.) The selection rule: "ignore the elephant in front of you, the likelihood they'll succeed, and focus instead on ... whether they'll succeed really big." (*swan*.) Thiel's Venn diagram: "one labelled 'seems like a bad idea' and the other 'is a good idea.' The intersection is the sweet spot." (*swan*.)

- **Schlep blindness**: "Your unconscious won't even let you see ideas that involve painful schleps. That's schlep blindness." (*schlep*.) *Worked example:* For years hackers felt the pain of online payments but built recipe sites instead; Stripe was hiding in plain sight. The defeating reframe: "Instead of asking 'what problem should I solve?' ask 'what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?'" (*schlep*.) "Turning off the schlep filter is more important than turning off the unsexy filter, because the schlep filter is more likely to be an illusion." (*startupideas*.)

- **The well: narrow and deep demand**: "you can either build something a large number of people want a small amount, or something a small number of people want a large amount. Choose the latter." (*startupideas*.) *Worked example:* "You can either dig a hole that's broad but shallow, or one that's narrow and deep, like a well." (*startupideas*.) The screening question: "Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of? If you can't answer that, the idea is probably bad." (*startupideas*.)

- **Organic ideas: live in the future, then build what's missing**: "Live in the future, then build what's missing." (*startupideas*.) *Worked example:* "Once you're good at some technology, when you look at the world you see dotted outlines around the things that are missing." (*google*.) "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not 'think up' but 'notice.'" (*startupideas*.) "The very best startup ideas tend to have three things in common: they're something the founders themselves want, that they themselves can build, and that few others realize are worth doing." (*startupideas*.)

- **Notice anomalies at the fractal frontier**: "The way to get new ideas is to notice anomalies: what seems strange, or missing, or broken?" (*getideas*.) *Worked example:* "Knowledge grows fractally. From a distance its edges look smooth, but when you learn enough to get close to one, you'll notice it's full of gaps." (*getideas*.) "Every cherished but mistaken principle is surrounded by a dead zone of valuable ideas that are unexplored because they contradict it." (*greatwork*; cf. *nov*.)

- **The top idea in your mind**: whatever your thoughts drift to in the shower gets your best thinking. *Worked example:* "There are two types of thoughts especially worth avoiding ... thoughts like the Nile Perch in the way they push out more interesting ideas." (*top*), money and disputes. The diagnostic: "just take a shower. What topic do your thoughts keep returning to?" (*top*.) You control it only indirectly: "Try to get yourself into situations where the most urgent problems are ones you want to think about." (*top*.)

- **Founder mode vs. manager mode**: "In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode." (*foundermode*.) Manager mode treats "subtrees of the org chart as black boxes." (*foundermode*.) *Worked example:* The essay grew from a Brian Chesky talk; the conventional advice nearly broke Airbnb, and Chesky course-corrected by studying how Steve Jobs ran Apple. The optimistic close: "Imagine what they'll do once we can tell them how to run their companies like Steve Jobs instead of John Sculley." (*foundermode*.)

- **Measurement + leverage**: "To get rich you need to get yourself in a situation with two things, measurement and leverage." (*wealth*.) *Worked example:* A small startup is "small in the sense of an all-star team," supplying measurement; technology is "the proverbial fishing rod, rather than the fish," supplying leverage. (*wealth*.) "If you're in a job that feels safe, you are not going to get rich, because if there is no danger there is almost certainly no leverage." (*wealth*.)

- **Maker's schedule vs. manager's schedule**: "There are two types of schedule, which I'll call the manager's schedule and the maker's schedule." (*makersschedule*.) *Worked example:* "For someone on the maker's schedule, having a meeting is like throwing an exception. It doesn't merely cause you to switch from one task to another; it changes the mode in which you work." (*makersschedule*.) At Viaweb he ran "two workdays each day, one on the manager's schedule and one on the maker's." (*makersschedule*.)

- **Persistence vs. obstinacy**: "The persistent are attached to the goal. The obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach it." (*persistence*.) *Worked example:* "The persistent are like boats whose engines can't be throttled back. The obstinate are like boats whose rudders can't be turned." (*persistence*.) Persistence decomposes into "energy, imagination, resilience, good judgement, and focus on a goal." (*persistence*.) Cross-ref: the running-back metaphor (*founders*, *startuplessons*).

- **The anatomy of determination**: "willfulness balanced with discipline, aimed by ambition." (*determination*.) *Worked example:* will and discipline are "two fingers squeezing a slippery melon seed ... they must both squeeze equally or the seed spins off sideways." (*determination*.) Determination erodes itself: "That's why Shakespeare's Caesar thought thin men so dangerous. They weren't tempted by the minor perquisites of power." (*determination*.) And it can be over-corrected: there are people "whose willfulness is crushed down by excessive discipline." (*determination*.)

- **The disagreement hierarchy (DH0 to DH6)**: "the disagreement hierarchy forms a kind of pyramid, in the sense that the higher you go the fewer instances you find." (*disagree*.) *Worked example:* an articulate insult ("the author is a self-important dilettante") "is really nothing more than a pretentious version of 'u r a fag.'" (*disagree*.) The payload is character: "there is a lot more meanness down in DH1 than up in DH6. You don't have to be mean when you have a real point to make." (*disagree*.)

- **Keep your identity small**: "The more labels you have for yourself, the dumber they make you." (*identity*.) *Worked example:* programming-language wars become "religious wars" because "so many programmers identify as X programmers." (*identity*.) The rare safe label is one that commits you to nothing but evidence: a scientist is committed only "to following the evidence wherever it leads." (*identity*.)

- **Cities send messages**: "In a hundred subtle ways, the city sends you a message: you could do more; you should try harder." (*cities*.) *Worked example:* New York says "you should be richer"; the Valley, "you should be more powerful"; Cambridge, "you should be smarter." A city "speaks to you mostly by accident ... something you can't turn off." (*cities*.) Practical advice: "try living in several places when you're young." (*cities*.)

- **A project of one's own**: "Working on a project of your own is as different from ordinary work as skating is from walking. It's more fun, but also much more productive." (*own*.) *Worked example:* the original Macintosh team "were not tennis balls hit by Steve Jobs, but rockets let loose by Steve Jobs." (*own*.) "Work/life" is itself a mistake: "it assumes work and life are distinct." (*own*.) At the start, "high standards are not merely useless but positively harmful." (*own*.)

- **What doesn't seem like work**: "If something that seems like work to other people doesn't seem like work to you, that's something you're well suited for." (*work*.) *Worked example:* his father, a mathematician, treated textbook exercises as the reward ("To him the problems were the reward. The text of each chapter was just some advice about solving them"), and PG himself likes debugging, which "one likes it the way one likes popping zits," and used to write papers for friends in classes he wasn't taking. (*work*.) "The stranger your tastes seem to other people, the stronger evidence they probably are of what you should do." (*work*.) The question to ask yourself: "What seems like work to other people that doesn't seem like work to you?" (*work*.)

- **The Blub Paradox**: you can see down a power continuum but not up it, so incumbents systematically misjudge tools and ideas more powerful than their own. "As long as our hypothetical Blub programmer is looking down the power continuum, he knows he's looking down." Looking up, "he doesn't realize he's looking up. What he sees are merely weird languages." The conclusion: "Blub is good enough for him, because he thinks in Blub." (*avg*.) *Worked example:* Viaweb was written in Lisp, which PG treats as a secret weapon precisely because competitors could not perceive its advantage; he later sold the result to Yahoo, where it became Yahoo Store (*avg*). The transferable use: when a founder or a reviewer calls a rival approach "weird," ask whether they are looking up the continuum. (Related, and not PG's own line: in *hp* he quotes the opening of Abelson and Sussman's *Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs*, "Programs should be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute," and adds "You need to have empathy not just for your users, but for your readers.")

- **Hacking is design, not science**: "What hackers and painters have in common is that they're both makers." (*hp*.) *Worked example:* "You should figure out programs as you're writing them, just as writers and painters and architects do" (*hp*); a language "should be a pencil, not a pen." (*hp*.) "Relentlessness wins because, in the aggregate, unseen details become visible", Leonardo's juniper leaves. (*hp*.) "Empathy is probably the single most important difference between a good hacker and a great one." (*hp*.)

- **Design vs. research**: "Design doesn't have to be new, but it has to be good. Research doesn't have to be good, but it has to be new." (*desres*.) *Worked example:* "good design requires a dictator ... good design has to be all of a piece" (*desres*), and "you're most likely to get good design if the intended users include the designer himself." (*desres*.)

- **The 13 principles of good design / good taste is real**: "Saying that taste is just personal preference is a good way to prevent disputes. The trouble is, it's not true." (*taste*.) *Worked example:* his reductio: "If there's no such thing as good taste, then there's no such thing as good art." (*goodtaste*), which would mean "we can't say that any painter is better than a randomly chosen eight year old." (*goodtaste*.) Objectivity is a matter of degree: "the distinction between 'objective' and 'subjective' is not binary, but a matter of degree, depending on how much the subjects have in common." (*goodtaste*.) "Being good art is thus a property of objects as much as ... being toxic to humans." (*goodart*.) For the average person, though, "brand dominates all other factors in the judgement of art." (*goodart*.)

- **Moral fashions & the conformist test**: "It would be a remarkable coincidence if ours were the first era to get everything just right." (*say*.) *Worked example:* "Almost certainly, there is something wrong with you if you don't think things you don't dare say out loud." (*say*.) The defense: "How can you see the wave, when you're the water? Always be questioning." (*say*.) Modern heresy is structural: "(1) that it takes priority over the question of truth or falsity, and (2) that it outweighs everything else the speaker has done", "like dropping a chunk of uranium onto the scale." (*heresy*.)

- **The four quadrants of conformism**: "a Cartesian coordinate system whose horizontal axis runs from conventional-minded ... to independent-minded ... and whose vertical axis runs from passive ... to aggressive." (*conformism*.) *Worked example:* "all successful startup CEOs are not merely independent-minded, but aggressively so" (*conformism*); the aggressively conventional-minded "are responsible for a disproportionate amount of the trouble in the world," and a wave of intolerance needs "two ingredients ... intolerant people, and an ideology to guide them." (*heresy*.)

- **Wisdom vs. intelligence**: "'wise' means one has a high average outcome across all situations, and 'smart' means one does spectacularly well in a few." (*wisdom*.) *Worked example:* you judge "intelligence at its best, and wisdom by its average" (*wisdom*); as knowledge specializes, "wisdom and intelligence diverge," and "society seems to have voted for intelligence ... Now we admire the genius." (*wisdom*.)

- **The bus ticket theory of genius**: "to have a disinterested obsession with something that matters." (*genius*.) *Worked example:* "An obsessive interest in a topic is both a proxy for ability and a substitute for determination" (*genius*); "a bus ticket collector's love is disinterested ... not doing it to impress us or to make themselves rich, but for its own sake." (*genius*.) Corollary: "to do great work, you also have to waste a lot of time," so "remain irresponsible." (*genius*.)

- **Good procrastination**: "The question is not how to avoid procrastination, but how to procrastinate well." (*procrastination*.) *Worked example:* three variants, "(a) nothing, (b) something less important, or (c) something more important. That last type ... is good procrastination." (*procrastination*.) "The most dangerous form of procrastination is unacknowledged type-B procrastination, because it doesn't feel like procrastination." (*procrastination*.) "Let delight pull you instead of making a to-do list push you." (*procrastination*.)

- **Stay upwind**: "work on things that maximize your future options." (*ideas*; *hs*; *when*.) *Worked example:* the glider with no engine: "If you let yourself get far downwind of good places to land, your options narrow uncomfortably." (*hs*.) "If you're unsure whether to major in math or economics ... choose math; math is upwind of economics." (*when*.)

- **The midwit peak (when to do what you love)**: "if you don't need to make much, you can work on whatever you're most interested in; if you want to become moderately rich, you can't usually afford to; but if you want to become super rich ... working on what you're most interested in becomes a good idea again." (*when*), "there's something like a midwit peak for making money." (*when*.) *Worked example:* "Many if not most of the biggest startups began as projects the founders were doing for fun. Apple, Google, and Facebook all began that way." (*when*.)

- **Investor herd dynamics**: "The biggest component in most investors' opinion of you is the opinion of other investors. Which is of course a recipe for exponential growth." (*herd*.) *Worked example:* fundraising as "some kind of primitive, multi-celled sea creature, where you irritate one extremity and the whole thing contracts violently." (*fundraising*.) (*equity*, i.e. *equity.html*.) *Worked example:* "to translate salary and overhead into stock you should multiply the annual rate by about 1.5." (*equity*.)

- **Two kinds of judgement**: "Sometimes judging you correctly is the end goal. But there's a second much more common type of judgement where it isn't." - **The runway / the mosquito**: funding measured in time: "when the money runs out you're going to be airborne or dead." (*startupmistakes*.) *Worked example:* "A startup is like a mosquito. A bear can absorb a hit and a crab is armored against one, but a mosquito is designed for one thing: to score." (*wealth*.)

- **Holding a program in one's head**: "A good programmer working intensively on his own code can hold it in his mind the way a mathematician holds a problem he's working on." (*head*.) *Worked example:* "Your code is your understanding of the problem you're exploring. So it's only when you have your code in your head that you really understand the problem." - **A way to detect bias**: "Want to know if the selection process was biased against some type of applicant? Check whether they outperform the others. This is not just a heuristic for detecting bias. It's what bias means." (*bias*.) *Worked example:* "many suspect that venture capital firms are biased against female founders. This would be easy to detect: among their portfolio companies, do startups with female founders outperform those without?" PG's reading is that, because the sample was their own portfolio, "they were producing a study not of startup trends but of their own biases when selecting companies." (*bias*.) The use for an operator: audit any selection funnel you run (hiring, lead scoring, store onboarding) by comparing the post-selection performance of each group; the group that outperforms is the one your filter is too hard on.

- **Type A vs. type B fundraising / the greedy algorithm**: "type A fundraising is when you don't need money, and type B fundraising is when you do." (*fr*.) *Worked example:* inexperienced founders imitate famous type A rounds while doing the much harder type B. Accept offers with "a greedy algorithm ... that doesn't try to look into the future" (*fr*), because the future is unpredictable and you are being deliberately misled. Stop "when you start to get a lot of air in the straw." (*fr*.)

- **The conservation of pain**: "if you want to make a million dollars, you have to endure a million dollars' worth of pain." (*wealth*.) *Worked example:* a startup doesn't eliminate the effort/stress of wealth creation, it *concentrates* it, "a way to compress your whole working life into a few years"; "instead of working at an ordinary rate for 40 years, you work like hell for four." (*wealth*, *start*.)

- **The low end eats the high end**: "In technology, the low end always eats the high end. It's easier to make an inexpensive product more powerful than to make a powerful product cheaper." (*start*.) *Worked example:* the strategic case for building the cheap/simple option and "fight design wars in new markets, where no one has yet managed to establish any fortifications." (*hp*.)

- **The Ronco principle**: "In a sufficiently connected and unpredictable world, you can't seem good without being good." (*ronco*.) *Worked example:* "the most successful investors are also the most upstanding"; Ron Conway "is so old school he's Old Testament. He will smite you in his just wrath, but there's no malice in it", good doesn't mean being a pushover. (*ronco*.)

- **Stuff owns you**: "once you've accumulated a certain amount of stuff, it starts to own you rather than the other way around." (*stuff*.) *Worked example:* a couple who couldn't move, "Their house isn't theirs; it's their stuff's." (*stuff*.) The keep/discard test: "Some give more than they take. Those are the only things worth having." (*stuff*.)

- **Accidental moderates**: "Intentional moderates are trimmers ... Accidental moderates end up in the middle, on average, because they make up their own minds about each question." (*mod*.) *Worked example:* "if the ideas you use in your work intersect with the politics of your time, you have two choices: be an accidental moderate, or be mediocre." (*mod*.) Ideologues "acquire one's opinions in bulk ... Your opinions about taxation can be predicted from your opinions about sex." (*mod*.)

- **The power of the marginal**: outsiders win because "Eminence is like a suit: it impresses the wrong people, and it constrains the wearer." (*marginal*.) *Worked example:* the homing beacon, "You're on the right track when people complain that you're unqualified, or that you've done something inappropriate," since "'Inappropriate' is the null criticism." (*marginal*.) Judge "talent at its best and character at its worst." (*marginal*.)

- **You weren't meant to have a boss**: in a tree-structured org "each person gets freedom of action in inverse proportion to the size of the entire tree." (*boss*.) *Worked example:* "A job at a big company is like high fructose corn syrup: it has some of the qualities of things you're meant to like, but is disastrously lacking in others"; working for yourself "makes your brain more powerful in the same way a low-restriction exhaust system makes an engine more powerful." (*boss*.)

- **Fierce nerds**: "extremely competitive ... independent-minded ... and impatient." (*fn*.) *Worked example:* the prescription against the troll fate is to "work on ambitious projects", "If you're given a sharp knife, it seems to me better to use it than to blunt its edge." (*fn*.) The tailwind: "a continuous transfer of power from dealmakers to technicians — from the charismatic to the competent." (*fn*.)

- **Life is short / convert time to discrete units**: "Having kids showed me how to convert a continuous quantity, time, into discrete quantities. You only get 52 weekends with your 2 year old." (*vb*.) *Worked example:* the heuristic for what matters, "ask yourself whether you'll care about it in the future"; the imperative is "Relentlessly prune bullshit," the "junk food of experience." (*vb*.)

- **Wisdom from curing childish qualities; intelligence from cultivating them**: "Wisdom seems to come largely from curing childish qualities, and intelligence largely from cultivating them." (*wisdom*), which is why "schools work badly ... they're trying to make intelligence using recipes for wisdom." (*wisdom*.)

- **The brand age**: industries shift from competing on substance to competing on identity once technology commoditizes quality. "Brand is what's left when the substantive differences between products disappear. But making the substantive differences between products disappear is what technology naturally tends to do." (*brandage*.) *Worked example:* the Swiss watch golden age ran "from 1945 to 1970"; once they "knew they weren't going to beat the Japanese on performance," makers had to "depend more on brand." Patek Philippe's 1968 Golden Ellipse is the first case-as-brand: "to emphasize the distinctive profile of the Ellipse, Patek made the crown too small, with the result that they're distractingly hard to wind." (*brandage*.) The general law: "Branding isn't merely orthogonal to good design, but opposed to it. Branding by definition has to be distinctive. But good design, like math or science, seeks the right answer, and right answers tend to converge. Branding is centrifugal; design is centripetal." (*brandage*.) The diagnostic for an advisee: if winning in your market now depends on looking different rather than being better, the substance game is over there, and a startup should look for a market where it is not.

- **Your mind is a compiled program whose source you've lost**: reading and experience permanently train your model of the world even after you forget the content. "Your mind is like a compiled program you've lost the source of. It works, but you don't know why." (*know*.) *Worked example:* PG's unease at forgetting most of what he reads dissolves once he sees the harvest isn't recalled facts but an updated model: "Reading and experience train your model of the world. And even if you forget the experience or what you read, its effect on your model of the world persists." (*know*.) Hence reading still matters even if knowledge could somehow be loaded directly.

- **Behavior follows structural role, not character (the unified theory of VCs)**: to predict how a class of people will act, look at how they're paid, not who they are. "It's not so much that the business attracts jerks, or even that the power they wield corrupts them. The real problem is the way they're paid." (*venturecapital*.) *Worked example:* "The problem with VC funds is that they're funds. Like the managers of mutual funds or hedge funds, VCs get paid a percentage of the money they manage", so fund size forces multi-million-dollar deals, which forces control and meddling. The generalization: "VCs are like car salesmen or bureaucrats: the nature of their work turns them into jerks." (*venturecapital*.)

- **Death is the default; a startup hub doesn't prevent startupicide, it supplies the antidote**: reframe a place's effect as additive survival, not the absence of harm. "The problem is not that most towns kill startups. It's that death is the default for startups, and most towns don't save them." (*hubs*.) *Worked example:* the two-part antidote is local norms plus chance meetings, so "In the Valley, lightning has a sign bit", random encounters skew toward the compensating miracle rather than the disaster. (*hubs*.)

- **Limiting reagents of an ecosystem**: to seed a self-sustaining system, supply only the inputs that won't move on their own; everything else follows. "I think you only need two kinds of people to create a technology hub: rich people and nerds. They're the limiting reagents in the reaction that produces startups" (*siliconvalley*.) *Worked example:* the policy corollary is that you can't engineer the result top-down, "A government that asks \"How can we build a silicon valley?\" has probably ensured failure by the way they framed the question. You don't build a silicon valley; you let one grow." (*siliconvalley*.)

- **Writing to persuade vs. writing to discover**: the two are opposed, and optimizing for the reader quietly corrupts the ideas. "So writing to persuade and writing to discover are diametrically opposed." (*discover*.) *Worked example:* the failure mode isn't just lost truth but self-deception, "It's that you start to lie to yourself. The ideas start to get mixed together with the spin you've added to get them past the readers' misconceptions." (*discover*.) PG's stance: "But I don't write to persuade; I write to figure out. I write to persuade a hypothetical perfectly unbiased reader." (*discover*.)

- **Bubbles need a true core**: a mania can only inflate around something real, and most of the money from a real trend is captured indirectly. "you need to have something solid at the center, so that even smart people are sucked in." (*bubble*.) *Worked example:* "In fact most of the money to be made from big trends is made indirectly", the railroads-and-Carnegie pattern, where for every Google there are ten JetBlues built on the same underlying shift. (*bubble*.)

- **What would make this company more powerful (the office-hours power question)**: swap "how do we make more money?" for a structural question. *Worked example:* for a startup building a way for agents to pay for things, "the first question I'd ask is whether the agents could also pay one another. If they can do that, you become a marketplace." (*powerful*.) The toolkit he runs through: "It's always good when money flows through you." (*powerful*), "Network effects make companies more powerful, and I almost always think about how to introduce them." (*powerful*), and going full stack until, in the limit case, "the real customer is their customer; the initial customer is now just a kind of hand puppet." (*powerful*.) If no direct network effect exists, he suggests letting users opt in to sharing something; YC itself is cited as a network effect "in something you wouldn't expect to have them" (paraphrase, *powerful*).

- **Tails that could wag the dog**: peripheral things founders build may be the real company. *Worked example:* PayPal began as security software for hand-held devices; the founders built PayPal as a demo, eBay sellers started using it for payments, and "after a couple months the founders acknowledged that this was the business they were now in." (*powerful*.) The rule: "When you see something like that, don't be annoyed that your users are using your product wrong; listen for the message they're sending, because it could be valuable." (*powerful*.) Related: "Most of the most successful companies we've funded help their users to make money, as does YC itself," which makes revenues "grow doubly fast." (*powerful*.)

- **Sell to the youngest customers (customer age as a variable)**: "Strangely enough, selling to earlier stage companies makes you more powerful." (*powerful*.) *Worked example:* Stripe "makes a point of signing up companies at the first possible moment"; payments are the kind of thing where "if it ain't broke, you don't fix it," so installed companies stick, and usage pricing means "your revenues will grow at startup rates." (*powerful*.) The reframe: "If you think in quarters, it seems like potential customers have fixed sizes. If you think in decades, you can see they have trajectories." (*powerful*.) The data variant: Rippling started with onboarding software "because that's where the life of employee data begins," so that product was "way better than it needed to be, and spread rapidly." (*powerful*.)

- **Mafia markets and the "Just y" diagnostic**: "Since startups make the best stuff, they're strongest on level playing fields. They're weakest in markets dominated by companies you'd describe as mafia. Record labels are mafia. PBMs are mafia." (*powerful*.) The approach is to come in from the side and make them irrelevant, not to attack head on (paraphrase). The second diagnostic is internal: "The company is unconsciously cowering by doing something less ambitious than they could. Just y is often, in effect, just stand up straight. And when they do they're much taller." (*powerful*.) Run the whole toolkit backwards from the customer: "What would the perfect world look like, from the customer's point of view? If there's a component of that world that the startup could transform itself into, it probably should." (*powerful*.)

- **Turn off the fear of the lame (early work)**: the first steps of great work are often pretty feeble (paraphrase of *early*), so the instinct to judge them as finished work kills the good ones. *Worked example:* a YC partner has "thousands of new ones thrown at you" every six months and has to sort through them, "knowing that in a world with a power-law distribution of outcomes, it will be painfully obvious if you miss the needle in this haystack. Optimism becomes urgent." (*early*.) The toolkit: the expected-value trick ("If you look at something that's 20% of the way to a goal worth 100 and conclude that it's 10% of the way to a goal worth 200, your estimate of its expected value is correct even though both components are wrong"), slight overconfidence ("being slightly overconfident armors you against both other people's skepticism and your own"), and relabeling as a sketch ("It's easier to convince yourself that something is just a sketch when you're drawing in a notebook than when you're carving stone"). (*early*.) The meta-point: "our attitudes toward it are themselves early work. Holding everything to the same standard is a crude version 1." (*early*.)

- **See randomness**: prefer the theory that does not center on you. *Worked example (the cake):* you save a piece of cake in the fridge and find it eaten; either the housemate did it deliberately to upset you, or the housemate was hungry. "I say pick b." (*randomness*.) The rule: "Conversely, if you have to choose between two theories, prefer the one that doesn't center on you." (*randomness*.) It doubles as a discovery heuristic: "if you want to discover things that have been overlooked till now, one really good place to look is in our blind spot: in our natural, naive belief that it's all about us." (*randomness*.)

- **The noob signal (local discomfort, global information)**: "the more of a noob you are locally, the less of a noob you are globally." (*noob*.) *Worked example:* move from your home country to "Farawavia, where everything works differently" and you will feel more of a noob while knowing more (paraphrase, *noob*). Why the aversion misleads: "Now that too much food is more of a problem than too little, our dislike of feeling hungry leads us astray. And I think our dislike of feeling like a noob does too." (*noob*.) The decision rule: "the more you feel like a noob, the better." (*noob*.)

- **Conversational resourcefulness (the office-hours diagnostic)**: hard-to-talk-to founders were a symptom, not the cause. *Worked example:* he noticed that the least successful startups "all seemed hard to talk to" (*word*), while the best were "fire-and-forget in the sense that all you have to do is give them a lead, and they'll close it, whatever type of lead it is." (*word*.) The key is the adage "a word to the wise is sufficient": the wise chase down all the implications of what you say. A YC partner's contrast: with good groups, advice is examined with fresh eyes and dismissed only for a stated reason, and "Those groups never have that glazed over look"; bad groups arrive with a decision already made and run everything through a rationalization (paraphrase, *word*). The cause: "the unsuccessful founders had the sort of conservatism that comes from weakness. They traversed idea space as gingerly as a very old person traverses the physical world." (*word*.)

- **Pivot as a condition of funding (founder-company fit)**: when the idea is bad but the founders are the asset, redirect them. *Worked example:* Steve Huffman and Alexis Ohanian pitched ordering fast food from a cellphone, which "was not going to happen" in that era (paraphrase, *reddits*); YC rejected the idea, then phoned them on the train home, and "by the end of the day were committed to working on what's now called Reddit." (*reddits*.) PG on why it worked: "As with all the really great startups, there's an uncannily close match between the company and the founders. Steve in particular." (*reddits*.) His push on execution: "I pushed the Reddits to launch fast. A version one didn't need to be more than a couple hundred lines of code." (*reddits*.) And the resilience test: "If Reddit could grow to the size it had with management that was harmless at best, what could it do if Steve came back?" (*reddits*.)

- **Funding as gears**: "Venture funding works like gears. A typical startup goes through several rounds of funding, and at each round you want to take just enough money to reach the speed where you can shift into the next gear." (*startupfunding*.) *Worked example:* a three-founder cap table walked from a $15,000 seed for 5% (with a 20% option pool), to a $200,000 angel round at $1M pre-money (the angel owns a sixth), to a $2M Series A at $4M pre (investors take a third, the option pool enlarged first, the board becoming two VCs, two founders, and one mutually acceptable person). (*startupfunding*.) "Few startups get it quite right. Many are underfunded. A few are overfunded, which is like trying to start driving in third gear." (*startupfunding*.) And since "in early stage investing, valuations are voodoo," judge a round by dilution plus the investor's value-add. (*startupfunding*.)

- **Product business vs. service business (consulting as the weed tree)**: "To be a startup, a company has to be a product business, not a service business." (*startupfunding*.) *Worked example:* "Custom work doesn't scale. To be a startup you need to be the band that sells a million copies of a song, not the band that makes money by playing at individual weddings and bar mitzvahs." (*startupfunding*.) Footnote: "Consulting is where product companies go to die. IBM is the most famous example." (*startupfunding*.) The subtler danger is psychological: "the biggest danger of consulting may be that it gives you an excuse for failure," whereas publishing a definite ambition is a piece of jiujitsu: "Fear of failure is an extraordinarily powerful force. Usually it prevents people from starting things, but once you publish some definite ambition, it switches directions and starts working in your favor." (*startupfunding*.)

- **Orthodoxy as a counterfeit virtue (prigs are constant, the rules vary)**: "There's a certain kind of person who's attracted to a shallow, exacting kind of moral purity, and who demonstrates his purity by attacking anyone who breaks the rules. Every society has these people. All that changes is the rules they enforce." (*woke*.) So ask why these ideas and why now, rather than why people are priggish. Why orthodoxy is attractive to bad people: "You can be the worst person in the world, but as long as you're orthodox you're better than everyone who isn't." (*woke*.) And why the rules must be shallow and shifting: "for it to work as a substitute for virtue, orthodoxy must be difficult." (*woke*.) *Worked example:* explaining to an alien visitor why one term is enlightened and a near-synonym gets you fired, with no underlying principle, only a list to memorize (paraphrase, *woke*). His mechanism for the second wave is incentives plus infrastructure: forum users "were about three times more likely to upvote something if it outraged them," and "If you hire people to keep watch for a particular type of problem, they're going to find it, because otherwise there's no justification for their existence." (*woke*.) The control point is downstream: "We should have a conscious bias against defining new forms of heresy." (*woke*.) A sanity check: substitute Christianity into any proposed practice and see whether you would accept it (paraphrase, *woke*), and "Humor is one of the most powerful weapons against priggishness of any sort, because prigs, being humorless, can't respond in kind." (*woke*.)

- **Orthodox privilege**: "The more conventional-minded someone is, the more it seems to them that it's safe for everyone to express their opinions." (*orth*.) Unlike other privilege blindness it cannot be cured with more information, only with more independence of mind. *Worked example (base rates):* "And yet at every point in history, there were true things that would get you in trouble to say. Is ours the first where this isn't so? What an amazing coincidence that would be." (*orth*.) *Worked example (politeness):* "If someone says they can hear a high-pitched noise that you can't, it's only polite to take them at their word, instead of demanding evidence that's impossible to produce, or simply denying that they hear anything." (*orth*.) He treats sufferers as sincere ("It doesn't seem to conventional-minded people that they're conventional-minded. It just seems to them that they're right"), not as bad-faith actors. (*orth*.)

- **Synchronous funding was found by accident (how YC started)**: *Worked example:* "Initially we didn't have what turned out to be the most important idea: funding startups synchronously, instead of asynchronously as it had always been done before." (*ycstart*.) The batch was a learning device: "The reason we began by funding a bunch of startups at once was not that we thought it would be a better way to fund startups, but simply because we wanted to learn how to be angel investors, and a summer program for undergrads seemed the fastest way to do it. The decision itself was fast ("As we turned onto Walker Street we decided to do it") and the launch was too: "so we did what we're always telling startups to do: we launched fast." (*ycstart*.) Expectations were low: "We never expected to make any money from that first batch. We thought of the money we were investing as a combination of an educational expense and a charitable donation." - **Build the institution you wished you'd had (and let ignorance rewrite the conventions)**: YC copied the deal PG took for Viaweb: "In return for $10k, getting us set up as a company, teaching us what business was about, and remaining calm in times of crisis, Julian got 10% of Viaweb." - **The hack motive (why YC)**: PG's honest-motive test refuses both flattering answers. He first rules out money ("We didn't start it mainly to make money; we have no idea what our average returns might be, and won't know for years") and virtue, then names the third answer: "The real reason we started Y Combinator is one probably only a hacker would understand. - **Advising does not pool (the O(n^2) failure)**: *Worked example:* until summer 2012 any partner took the next office-hours slot, so every partner had to know every startup; fine at 60 startups, broken at 80. - **Founders know the problems but not their rank**: "Often founders know what their problems are, but not their relative importance." (*users*.) *Worked example:* founders arrive worried about raising money when the real cause is that the company is doing badly and investors can tell, or worried about acquiring users when the product is not good enough; his probe is "Would you use this yourself, if you hadn't built it?" and some, on thinking, answered no (paraphrase, *users*). - **Counterintuitive advice sounds wrong**: founders ignore advice because "when you tell someone something counterintuitive, what it sounds to them is wrong." - **Decisive at the micro scale, tentative at the macro scale**: "If you correct course at a high enough frequency, you can be simultaneously decisive at a micro scale and tentative at a macro scale." (*users*.) *Worked example:* the winding running-back path, with ideas cooked up "at a resolution of a week or less" (paraphrase). - **Colleague clusters**: "However good you are, good colleagues make you better." (*users*.) *Worked example:* Florence, Göttingen, Bell Labs and Xerox PARC as clusters, with YC "deliberately designed" to be one; before it, in his 1990s Boston startup, "a handful of older people we could go to for advice (of varying quality), but no peers" (paraphrase, *users*). - **An intermediary can't own the user (platform karma)**: "It doesn't work for an intermediary to own the user." - **Power minus charisma equals lightning rod**: "People who are powerful but uncharismatic will tend to be disliked." - **Language-adoption laws that transfer to products (twenty users, Trojan horse)**: "If a language had twenty separate users, meaning twenty users who decided on their own to use it, I'd consider it to be real." (*popular*.) "I would not be surprised if it is harder to get from zero to twenty than from twenty to a thousand." (*popular*.) C was the scripting language of Unix and Perl of sysadmin text-processing (paraphrase). Time is an input too: "It's not when people notice you're there that they pay attention; it's when they notice you're still there." (*popular*.) "So anyone who invents something new has to expect to keep repeating their message for years before people will start to get it." (*popular*.)

- **Design for the genius, not the bumbler (and let yourself be second guessed)**: "I think language designers would do better to consider their target user to be a genius who will need to do things they never anticipated, rather than a bumbler who needs to be protected from himself. The bumbler will shoot himself in the foot anyway." (*popular*.) *Worked example:* he attacks hygienic macros because "They try to protect you from understanding what they're doing" (paraphrase of his objection) and wants a language that is "both clean and dirty": a small, orthogonal core that "lets hackers have their way with it." (*popular*.) Companion rule: "Let yourself be second guessed." (*popular*.)

- **Big systems begin as throwaway programs; evolve, don't pre-design**: "And so, paradoxically, if you want to make a language that is used for big systems, you have to make it good for writing throwaway programs, because that's where big systems come from." (*popular*.) *Worked example:* Perl "was pretty much a throwaway program itself," started as report-generating utilities (paraphrase). The anti-pattern is the Hoover Dam approach: "a shopping mall rather than a real downtown, Brasilia rather than Rome." (*popular*.) And "Organic growth seems to yield better technology and richer founders than the big bang method." (*popular*.), with Multics and Common Lisp as big-bang projects, Unix and MacLisp as organic ones.

- **Hope and worry are the two legs of a project; redesign is the point**: "To write good software you must simultaneously keep two opposing ideas in your head. You need the young hacker's naive faith in his abilities, and at the same time the veteran's skepticism." (*popular*.) "If you can keep hope and worry balanced, they will drive a project forward the same way your two legs drive a bicycle forward." (*popular*.) The enemy of redesign is committees ("Even a committee of two gets in the way of redesign") and also premature users: "Users are a double-edged sword. They can help you improve your language, but they can also deter you from improving it. So choose your users carefully, and be slow to grow their number." (*popular*.) Change is cheaper than feared: "you can at any given time get away with changing more than you think. Introducing change is like pulling off a bandage." (*popular*.)

- **Main branches, good waste, version 1 as essay (the hundred-year language)**: "I think the fundamental operators are the most important factor in a language's long term survival. The rest you can change." (*hundred*.) *Worked example:* the house-buying rule, where you consider location first because "Everything else you can fix later, but you can't fix the location." (*hundred*.) "Wasting programmer time is the true inefficiency, not wasting machine time." (*hundred*.) And the target of the whole exercise: "What programmers in a hundred years will be looking for, most of all, is a language where you can throw together an unbelievably inefficient version 1 of a program with the least possible effort." (*hundred*.) Method: "I think some of the best programs were essays, in the sense that the authors didn't know when they started exactly what they were trying to write." (*hundred*.) Optimize "fairly late in the life of a program," with a profiler as the guide.

- **Attack the invariant, and measure a real quantity (A Plan for Spam)**: "I think it's possible to stop spam, and that content-based filters are the way to do it. The Achilles heel of the spammers is their message." (*spam*.) Spammers can route around every barrier except the one thing they must deliver. *Worked example:* a Bayesian filter that "we now miss less than 5 per 1000 spams, with 0 false positives" on his own mail (*spam*), working because "the real advantage of the Bayesian approach, of course, is that you know what you're measuring." (*spam*.) Definition by mechanism, not surface: "The defining feature of spam (in fact, its raison d'etre) is not that it is unsolicited, but that it is automated." (*spam*.)

- **Price the asymmetric error; fixes for false positives are bugs (spam and better filtering)**: "For most users, missing legitimate email is an order of magnitude worse than receiving spam, so a filter that yields false positives is like an acne cure that carries a risk of death to the patient." (*spam*.) *Worked example:* "Filtering rate is a measure of performance. False positives I consider more like bugs. I approach improving the filtering rate as optimization, and decreasing false positives as debugging." (*better*.) His five false positives in roughly 7,740 legitimate mails became "my bug list" (*better*), and the design takeaway is a knob: "I think any spam filtering algorithm ought to have a convenient knob you can twist to decrease the false positive rate at the expense of the filtering rate." (*better*.) The math expression of it: "I want to bias the probabilities slightly to avoid false positives, and by trial and error I've found that a good way to do it is to double all the numbers in good." (*spam*.)

- **Let the data be cleverer than you; domain structure beats the textbook problem**: "I spent about six months writing software that looked for individual spam features before I tried the statistical approach." (*spam*.) "When I did try statistical analysis, I found immediately that it was much cleverer than I had been." (*spam*.) *Worked example:* "ff0000" (bright red HTML) turned out to be as good an indicator of spam as any pornographic term. Later, on why others got worse results: "Email is not just text; it has structure." (*better*), with the honest counter-case that CRM114 is a straight text classifier that works almost perfectly, so this is a prior, not a law. "There is a lesson here for filter writers: don't ignore data. ... You'd think this lesson would be too obvious to mention, but I've had to learn it several times." (*better*.)

- **Diversity and economics against an adaptive adversary**: the stress test: "anyone who proposes a plan for spam filtering has to be able to answer the question: if the spammers knew exactly what you were doing, how well could they get past you?" (*spam*.) Per-user filters make it hard: "If everyone's filters have different probabilities, it will make the spammers' optimization loop, what programmers would call their edit-compile-test cycle, appallingly slow." (*better*.) And the economic attack: "The spammers are businessmen. They send spam because it works." (*spam*.) Filtering 95% of spam "would increase the spammers' cost to reach a given audience by a factor of 20" (footnote, *spam*), so "if we get good enough at filtering out spam, it will stop working, and the spammers will actually stop sending it." (*spam*.)

- **Scarce-resource arguments lose**: "Historically, scarce-resource arguments have been the losing side in debates about software design." (*better*, note 13.) *Worked example:* advocates of network-level filtering say it is more efficient; PG reads that as "we currently filter at the network level, and we don't want to start over from scratch," and he is "not optimistic about filters that work at the network level" because static obstacles get routed around. The rule: "you can't dictate the problem to fit your solution." (*better*.)

- **The Fluff Principle (ranking systems select for what is cheap to judge)**: "Hence what I call the Fluff Principle: on a user-voted news site, the links that are easiest to judge will take over unless you take specific measures to prevent it." (*hackernews*.) *Worked example:* a new theorem takes work to judge, a cartoon less, and "A rant with a rallying cry as the title takes zero, because people vote it up without even reading it." (*hackernews*.) The countermeasures were content rules, not more users: banning kittens and political diatribes, rewriting rallying-cry titles into matter-of-fact ones, and swapping linkjacked URLs for the original (paraphrase). The test for topicality is "to engage one's intellectual curiosity." (*hackernews*.)

- **Broken windows for communities (police behavior, not people)**: "If people are expected to behave well, they tend to; and vice versa." (*hackernews*.) "It's pretty clear now that the broken windows theory applies to community sites as well." (*hackernews*.) *Worked example:* Reddit, which censored nothing except spam while growing fast, became a free-for-all: "Combine rapid growth and zero censorship, and the result is a free for all." (*hackernews*.) The point is behavior over identity: "it's measured more in behavior than users. It's bad behavior you want to keep out more than bad people." (*hackernews*.) He is explicit that this is no criticism of the Reddit founders ("What happened to Reddit didn't happen out of neglect").

- **Meanness versus stupidity (two different comment problems)**: "There are two main kinds of badness in comments: meanness and stupidity." (*hackernews*.) "Meanness is easier to control." (*hackernews*), because "Mean people are more likely to know they're being mean than stupid people are to know they're being stupid." *Worked example:* "The most dangerous form of stupid comment is not the long but mistaken argument, but the dumb joke," and "Bad comments are like kudzu: they take over rapidly." (*hackernews*.) Remedies he floats: a response delay inversely proportional to predicted quality, the xkcd IRC rule "don't allow the same thing twice," or folding predicted quality into sort order so weak comments sink.

- **Do not rank people in public (the orange-username mistake)**: *Worked example:* showing the top average-comment-score users in orange changed the site within weeks: "Suddenly a culture that had been more or less united was divided into haves and have-nots." (*hackernews*.) He rolled it back. The lesson: "if you're going to distinguish between people, you better be sure you do it right," and "This is one problem where rapid prototyping doesn't work." (*hackernews*.) Add the preservation stance: "I notice most of the techniques I've described are conservative: they're aimed at preserving the character of the site rather than enhancing it." (*hackernews*.)

- **Judge bets ex ante, not through hindsight (the risk of discovery)**: "Because biographies of famous scientists tend to edit out their mistakes, we underestimate the degree of risk they were willing to take." (*disc*.) *Worked example:* "In Newton's day the three problems seemed roughly equally promising." (*disc*.) "Newton made three bets. One of them worked. But they were all risky." (*disc*.) Practical corollary: if you filter out every project that looked risky in advance, you filter out the class where breakthroughs live.

- **Prestige is a danger sign; impure motives are the real risk**: "Which in turn means that prestige is a danger sign." (*worked*, the corollary of the low end eating the high end, learned at Interleaf.) "Impure motives are a big danger for the ambitious. If anything is going to lead you astray, it will be the desire to impress people." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* "Still life has always been the least prestigious form of painting. Viaweb and Y Combinator both seemed lame when we started them." (*worked*.) The caveat he adds himself: "It's not that unprestigious types of work are good per se." (*worked*.) The product expression: "We were determined to be the Microsoft Word, not the Interleaf," and "big, bureaucratic customers are a dangerous source of money." (*worked*.)

- **The blister test (keep your biggest stressor near the core of the work)**: "Surely the biggest source of stress in one's work should at least be something close to the core of the work." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* running Hacker News was, by his own estimate, the source of roughly 60% of urgent YC problems (paraphrase of the figure, *worked*), so he concluded it was "a mistake." "I was like someone who was in pain while running a marathon not from the exertion of running, but because I had a blister from an ill-fitting shoe." (*worked*.) The generalization: if your largest stressor is peripheral to your real job, remove or delegate it.

- **Exit by self-obsolescence; attention is zero sum**: "But we wanted YC to last for a long time, and to do that it couldn't be controlled by the founders." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* after Robert Morris's remark that "you should make sure Y Combinator isn't the last cool thing you do," PG recruited Sam Altman (who said yes in October 2013) and the four founders completed a changing of the guard (paraphrase, *worked*). On why he left: "Either YC was my life's work or I'd have to leave eventually. And it wasn't, so I would." (*worked*.) The cost logic: "But attention is a zero sum game," and "at 50 there was some opportunity cost to screwing around." (*worked*.)

- **Build things that last (durability as a selection criterion)**: "I wanted not just to build things, but to build things that would last." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* systems software goes obsolete (surplus Xerox Dandelions "were so slow by present standards"), whereas "Paintings didn't become obsolete." (*worked*.) This drove the painting detour and then Bel: four years from March 2015 to October 2019, with an essay ban ("no more essays till Bel was done") because three months of essay writing left him unable to read his own code (paraphrase, *worked*).

- **Look legit (production values as the product)**: "It helped to have studied art, because the main goal of an online store builder is to make users look legit." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* "you can make a guy running a store out of his bedroom look more legit than a big company." (*worked*.) When your customer's problem is credibility, craft in layout, fonts and color is the feature.

- **Growth rate as the ultimate test (the Viaweb 7x year)**: "Another thing I didn't get at the time is that growth rate is the ultimate test of a startup." (*worked*.) *Worked example:* Viaweb went from about 70 stores at the end of 1996 to about 500 at the end of 1997, yet PG believed absolute numbers mattered and hired heavily, so "we were at the mercy of investors for the entire life of the company." (*worked*.) What he would tell a founder now: "You're growing 7x a year. Just don't hire too many more people and you'll soon be profitable, and then you'll control your own destiny." (*worked*.) Cross-reference: default alive.

- **Make something people want, from the failure that named it**: *Worked example:* Viaweb began as software to put art galleries online. "What a stupid idea." (*radio*.) "That is where Y Combinator's motto, 'Make something people want,' comes from. From making something people didn't want." (*radio*.) The pivot came from noticing that online stores resembled gallery sites with "categories and products" for "artists and artwork," so "all we have to do is write an order button." (*radio*.) The early customers were not the expected catalog-company middle managers but entrepreneurs starting new catalog companies (paraphrase, *radio*).

- **Do things that don't scale, the Viaweb origin**: *Worked example:* users said no to software for building their own store but yes to "what about if we used it for you?" (paraphrase, *radio*), so PG built the stores himself (a men's shirt client wanted only collar pictures, so he redid hundreds of images). He used his own tool all day, which meant bug fixes shipped while the customer was still on the phone. "We had to do things that didn't scale. This is where I learned about doing things that didn't scale. And in the beginning, I thought that doing things that didn't scale was something that we were forced into because we were lame. It was only later that I realized it was actually the optimal thing to do." (*radio*.) The arithmetic he gives in 2018: "constant growth rate means exponential growth" (auto-captions, *ralston*), so manual effort is affordable while the rate holds.

- **Stand behind your users**: "one of the big things programmers do not get is how traumatized users have been by bad and hard to use software. The default assumption of users is, 'This is going to be really painful, and in the end, it's not going to work.'" (*radio*.) "If you build something for users, go and stand behind them." (*radio*.) Why founders avoid it: "I think because they think they know what their users are already like. And they don't realize how different they are." (*radio*.) A calibration device: "The one real advantage of having a Ph.D. in computer science is you don't blame yourself." (*radio*.)

- **Quantum of utility (launch rule)**: "launch as soon as you have a quantum of utility" (auto-captions, *ralston*), glossed as one person in the world who is glad you launched (paraphrase). *Worked example:* when Geoff Ralston said six weeks to launch, PG told him to launch now and he launched within a week; YC itself launched in under a week with an ASCII form and email applications that were printed and passed around the partners (paraphrase, *ralston*). The risk logic: "so the risk of launching early is not as great as the risk of launching late" (auto-captions, *ralston*).

- **Trust chain: referrals transmit competence, not character**: "you find someone you trust and then find someone they trust" (auto-captions, *ralston*), which is how Robert Morris named Trevor Blackwell as the smartest grad student he knew. The caveat that makes it a model: "trustworthy people cannot judge other trustworthy people" and "trustworthy people are often fooled by untrustworthy people" (auto-captions, *ralston*), so character needs your own judgment, and PG says he delegates that to Jessica Livingston, the "social radar" (paraphrase). *Worked example:* Trevor rewrote an image-resize task as the whole product in Smalltalk in two weeks: "Great, powerful engine, no rudder. I will now be the rudder." (*radio*.)

- **Weight cofounders by the person, not the percentage**: "I would rather have like 10% of Robert Morris's brain than a hundred percent of almost anybody else's" (auto-captions, *ralston*), which is the answer to "would I rather have 30% of this person or a hundred percent of some other person." *Worked example:* PG tolerated Rtm voting to take every lowball Viaweb acquisition offer because his output was worth it (paraphrase, *ralston*). Cofounder closeness matters because "they're going to have to throw a ball without looking and assume that the other guy will already be reaching out his hands to catch it." (*radio*.)

- **Change your name (the .com as a signal)**: "The problem with not having the .com of your name is that it signals weakness. Unless you're so big that your reputation precedes you, a marginal domain suggests you're a marginal company." (*name*.) *Worked example:* Stripe searched for cheap domains from a list of acceptable words and bought stripe.com; "having x.com signals strength even if it has no relation to what you do." (*name*.) Why founders resist: "Their denial derives from two very powerful forces: identity, and lack of imagination." (*name*.) The fix is a skill gap, not a character flaw: "It turns out almost any word or word pair that is not an obviously bad name is a sufficiently good one," and "80% of the time we could find at least one good name in a 20 minute office hour slot." (*name*.) The evidence: "100% of the top 20 YC companies by valuation have the .com of their name. 94% of the top 50 do. But only 66% of companies in the current batch have the .com of their name." (*name*.)

- **The Refragmentation (mid-century cohesion was the anomaly, not fragmentation)**: polarization, cultural splintering, migration to a few cities and rising inequality are one phenomenon, and "the cause is not some force that's pulling us apart, but rather the erosion of forces that had been pushing us together." Those forces were specific and unrepeatable: "The two forces were war (above all World War II), and the rise of large corporations." (*re*.) *Worked example:* the "Duplo" economy of a few giant firms compressed pay at both ends (unions overpaid at the bottom, executives were underpaid at the top), and made the ambitious path a ladder inside someone else's institution, so "the result of making college the canonical path for the ambitious was a world in which it was socially acceptable to work for Henry Ford, but not to be Henry Ford." When it broke, it broke fast: "Basically, Apple bumped IBM and then Microsoft stole its wallet," and "The companies in the S&P 500 in 1958 had been there an average of 61 years. By 2012 that number was 18 years." (*re*.) Computers are a likely driver because they "reduce the transaction costs that Coase argued are the raison d'etre of corporations." (*re*.) The policy consequence: "Technology is a lever. It magnifies work," so "You can mitigate this with subsidies at the bottom and taxes at the top, but unless taxes are high enough to discourage people from creating wealth, you're always going to be fighting a losing battle against increasing variation in productivity." (*re*.) He refuses nostalgia ("as someone who caught the tail end of mid-century conformism , I can tell you it was no utopia") and calls the trend "simply a reversion to the mean." (*re*.) Use it when an advisee is planning around a return to a stable, consolidated market: PG's bet is that fragmentation is the default and the plan should mitigate its consequences rather than wait for it to reverse.

- **The Submarine (read the press for who paid for the story)**: PR is "the news equivalent of search engine optimization; instead of buying ads, which readers ignore, you get yourself inserted directly into the stories," and the industry is "lurking like a huge, quiet submarine beneath the news": "Of the stories you read in traditional media that aren't about politics, crimes, or disasters, more than half probably come from PR firms." (*submarine*.) Its method is not lying: "A good flatterer doesn't lie, but tells his victim selective truths (what a nice color your eyes are)." (*submarine*.) *Worked example:* Viaweb spent its whole marketing budget on PR ("we were paying a PR firm $16,000 a month. And they were worth it"), and its best coup was manufacturing a citable fact: "We estimated, based on some fairly informal math, that there were about 5000 stores on the Web. We got one paper to print this number, which seemed neutral enough. But once this "fact" was out there in print, we could quote it to other publications, and claim that with 1000 users we had 20% of the online store market." (*submarine*.) It worked because "Reporters like definitive statements," and with the top outlets "The weak point of the top reporters is not laziness, but vanity." (*submarine*.) The reading rule for trend pieces: line up the quoted experts, then "When you get to the end of the experts, look for the client"; more generally, "ask not just whether the author is telling the truth, but why he's writing about this subject at all." (*submarine*.) Use it both ways: to discount market-size and "trend" claims an advisee cites from the press, and as PG's own (2005) evidence that earned media can be a startup's best marketing spend.

## Decision heuristics
- **Optimize one number, the weekly growth rate.** "You can use that target growth rate to make all your decisions for you; anything that gets you the growth you need is ipso facto right." (*growth*.) Use it as "a form of evolutionary pressure" (*growth*); "merely measuring something has an uncanny tendency to improve it" (*13sentences*), but "be careful what you measure."
- **Recruit your first users by hand and over-serve them.** "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them." (*ds*.) "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy." (*ds*.) Get something out "as soon as it has a quantum of utility." (*ds*.)
- **Ask "are we default alive or default dead?" before hiring or raising, and ask too early.** "Instead of starting to ask too late ... start asking too early." (*aord*.) Always have a written plan B. (*aord*.)
- **Evaluate ideas by how bad they look to smart people.** "If a good idea were obviously good, someone else would already have done it." (*swan*.) Within YC, "when an idea is described as crazy, it's a compliment." (*ecw*.) Build something you and your friends "would be really sad to lose." (*google*.) The single funding question: "How do you know people want this?" (*ace*/*earn* lineage) → "The most convincing answer ... is 'Because we and our friends want it.'"
- **Trust your instincts about people; distrust them about mechanics.** "If you're thinking about getting involved with someone ... and you have misgivings about them, trust your gut." (*before*.) But "there are tricks in startups ... an order of magnitude less important than solving the real problem." (*before*.)
- **When a reasonable expert proposes something that sounds wrong, ask questions, don't dismiss.** "The wise response to such an idea is not to make statements, but to ask questions, because there's a real mystery here." (*newideas*.) The rewards for working on new ideas scale with the outcome; "the rewards for attacking new ideas are roughly constant."
- **Guard your top idea.** Stay out of running disputes and money-chasing that hijack what you think about in the shower. (*top*.) "Turning the other cheek turns out to have selfish advantages." (*top*.)
- **Keep your identity small.** Refuse "I'm an X" beliefs; "let as few things into your identity as possible." (*identity*.)
- **Pick work by curiosity, not "do what you love."** "Your motto should not be 'do what you love' so much as 'do what you're curious about.'" (*think*.) Test real interest: "if their work didn't pay well, would they take day jobs doing something else in order to do it in their spare time?" (*when*.) "Don't wait till the end of college to figure out what to work on." (*when*.)
- **"Always produce" to find your life's work**: it "will discover your life's work the way water, with the aid of gravity, finds the hole in your roof." (*love* / *when* lineage; cited verbatim under *love*.) And avoid prestige bait: "It might be a good rule simply to avoid any prestigious task. If it didn't suck, they wouldn't have had to make it prestigious." (*love*.)
- **When you can't decide, it's ignorance, not balance, get more certainty.** "When you can't decide which path to take, it's almost always due to ignorance." (*when*.) "What do you do in the face of uncertainty? Get more certainty." (*when*.)
- **Choose the harder of two options.** "If you have two choices, choose the harder." (*wealth*.) For features, "we'd always take the harder one", difficulty is a barrier to entry. (*wealth*.) For initiative: "If you're not sure what to do, make something." (*marginal*.)
- **Distrust fake work.** "The most dangerous way to lose time is not to spend it having fun, but to spend it doing fake work." (*selfindulgence*.) "Develop new alarms." Catch yourself lying: procrastination "is a form of lying to yourself." (*hwh*.)
- **Make distraction visible; treat it as an adversary.** "The biggest ingredient in most bad habits is denial. So you have to make it so that you can't merely slip into ... it. It has to set off alarms." (*distraction*.) "Addictive things have to be treated as if they were sentient adversaries." (*distraction*.)
- **Find your working limit by crossing it.** "The only way to find the limit is by crossing it. Cultivate a sensitivity to the quality of the work." (*hwh*), and "there's [nothing] admirable about working too hard." (*hwh*.) Aim toward the center: "Many problems have a hard core at the center ... Working hard means aiming toward the center." (*hwh*.)
- **If working hard isn't producing good-enough results, switch fields.** "If you're working hard but not getting good enough results, you should switch." (*hwh*.)
- **Write the bad draft fast, then rewrite ruthlessly.** "Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can; rewrite it over and over; cut out everything unnecessary; write in a conversational tone." (*writing44*.) "Expect 80% of the ideas in an essay to happen after you start writing it." (*writing44*.) Read it aloud; "before I publish a new essay, I read it out loud and fix everything that doesn't sound like conversation." (*talk*.)
- **Publish only what you're sure is worth hearing (the Morris technique).** "His trick is not to say anything unless he's sure it's worth hearing." (*useful*.) "In fact you constantly admit you're wrong. You just do it before publishing instead of after." (*useful*.)
- **Invert the regrets of the dying onto your todo list.** "Don't ignore your dreams; don't work too much; say what you think; cultivate friendships; be happy." (*todo*), because "errors of omission are a particularly dangerous type of mistake ... you make them by default." (*todo*.) Compressed: "don't be a cog." (*todo*.)
- **Take risk early, while you can afford it.** "Take as much risk as you can afford ... If you're not failing occasionally, you're probably being too conservative." (*greatwork*.) "Take multiple shots. Which is another reason to start taking risks early." (*superlinear*.) "Always be learning." (*superlinear*.)
- **Buying filter for stuff.** "Is this going to make my life noticeably better?" / "will this be something I use constantly? Or is it just something nice? Or worse still, a mere bargain?" (*stuff*.) "Beware of anything you find yourself describing as 'perfectly good.'" (*stuff*.)
- **Don't force-fit your idea to the hot category.** On the AI rush, founders matter more than the idea, so "if you want to start a startup to work on a non-AI idea, go ahead." (CNBC, 2025.)
- **Express ideas in the most general form**: "If you express your ideas in the most general form, they'll be truer than you intended." (*greatwork*.) Aim high deliberately: "if you don't try to be the best, you won't even be good" (*greatwork*); a durability test, "try to make something that people will care about in a hundred years." (*greatwork*.)
- **To beat status-quo bias, ask the reverse question.** "If I'd already made the change, would I want to revert to what I have now?" (*greatwork*.) And periodically: "Am I working on what I most want to work on?" (*greatwork*.)
- **Begin with the simplest thing that could possibly work,** then make successive versions. (*greatwork*.) "Get a version 1.0 out there as soon as you can. Until you have some users to measure, you're optimizing based on guesses." (*wealth*.)
- **Copy only what you genuinely like, and copy the substance, not the flaws.** "How do you avoid copying the wrong things? Copy only what you genuinely like." (*copy*.) "Be careful to copy what makes them good, rather than their flaws." (*copy*.) Find your real taste: "would you pay for this at a garage sale, dirty and unattributed?" (*copy*.)
- **Work with people you want to become like.** "Colleagues don't just affect your work, though; they also affect you. So work with people you want to become like, because you will." (*greatwork*.) "You'll become like whoever you work with. Do you want to become like these people?" (*when*.)
- **Be prolific.** "Being prolific is underrated. The more different things you try, the greater the chance of discovering something new." (*greatwork*.) "Essays are for taking risks." (*best*.)
- **Don't optimize for valuation; take the reasonable deal.** "If you get an offer from a reputable firm at a reasonable valuation with no unusually onerous terms, just take it and get on with building the company." (*startupmistakes*.) "What you want to optimize is your chance of a good outcome, not the percentage of the company you keep." (*guidetoinvestors*.) "Look down at your hands. Are you holding a termsheet?" (*guidetoinvestors*.)
- **Treat rejection as a question.** "Rejection is a question, not an answer" (*guidetoinvestors*), handle it "with precision," using the data in it, since "the average VC is a rejection machine." (*fundraising*.)
- **Stay independent of investors**: "you shouldn't start a startup that's expensive to start, because then you'll be at the mercy of investors" (*guidetoinvestors*); cross into ramen profitability if "a little more effort expended on sales would carry you over the threshold." (*fundraising*.) "Always have some alternative plan ... Having one is the best insurance against needing one." (*guidetoinvestors*.)
- **Solve a problem that's easy *for you*; optimize in problem-space, not solution-space.** "With a startup ... find a problem that's easy for you to solve ... you can make enormous gains playing around in problem-space." (*really*.) "The best way to solve a problem is often to redefine it." (*ideas*.)
- **For angels, bet on people and ratchet down to a sum you wouldn't mind losing.** "Don't get hung up on mechanics or deal terms. What you should spend your time thinking about is whether the company is good." (*angelinvesting*.) "Ratchet down the size of your investment till it's an amount you wouldn't care too much about losing." (*angelinvesting*.) "If you like a startup enough to invest in it, then invest in it." (*angelinvesting*.)
- **Equity is optimally split when everyone feels slightly cheated.** "When everyone feels they're getting a slightly bad deal ... the stock is optimally apportioned." (*start*.) Don't suppress cofounder misgivings: "It's much easier to fix problems before the company is started than after." (*startupmistakes*.)
- **Set goals slightly too small at first.** "Start by erring on the small side ... if you err on the side of making the goal too broad, you won't get anywhere." (*persistence*.) Solve a too-big problem by biting off a subset "then gradually expand from there." (*ideas*.)
- **Treat your parents' career wishes as feature requests**: "figure out why and find a better way," don't just give them the literal ask. (*notnot*.)
- **Approach big problems obliquely.** "You can't look a big problem too directly in the eye. You have to approach it somewhat obliquely." (*procrastination*.) Generalize Hamming: "What's the best thing you could be working on, and why aren't you?" (*procrastination*.)
- **Read your own draft as a stranger who knows only the words on the page.** "You have to pretend to be a neutral reader who knows nothing of what's in your head, only what you wrote." (*words*.) Begin from a question, not a thesis: "You notice a door that's ajar, and you open it and walk in to see what's inside." (*essay*.)
- **Silently fact-check everyone, take on the burden of evaluating, not proving.** "When you hear someone say something, stop and ask yourself 'Is that true?'" (*think*.) Curiosity is unusual: "indulging it tends to increase rather than to sate it" (*think*), and the independent-minded are "the gluttons of curiosity, who keep eating even after they're full." (*think*.)
- **Get to profitability before raising, because fundraising is a productivity sink.** "Raising money is terribly distracting. You're lucky if your productivity is a third of what it was before. And it can last for months." (*ramenprofitable*.)
- **Look for threats in the mirror, not the news, and run cheap enough to be unkillable.** "if you're worried about threats to the survival of your company, don't look for them in the news. Look in the mirror." (*badeconomy*.) "the surest route to success is to be the cockroaches of the corporate world. The immediate cause of death in a startup is always running out of money." (*badeconomy*.)
- **Take insane career risks while young.** "Your early twenties are exactly the time to take insane career risks." (*hiring*.) A failed attempt isn't a liability to employers, surveyed managers at Yahoo/Google/Amazon/Cisco/Microsoft said "they'd prefer the guy who'd tried to start his own company." (*hiring*.)
- **Default to "yes" with your users; err on the side of generosity.** "if you find yourself asking \"should we allow users to do x?\" just answer \"yes\" whenever you're unsure. Err on the side of generosity." (*web20*.)
- **The "need" trap, judge a question by advantage, not necessity.** Whenever you catch yourself reasoning about whether you *need* some edge, you're framing it wrong: "if you hear someone saying \"we don't need to be in Silicon Valley,\" that use of the word \"need\" is a sign they're not even thinking about the question right." (*webstartups*), anything that gives a competitor an advantage gives them one over you.
- **Ask "what would make this company more powerful?" before "how do we make more money?"** (*powerful*.) Concrete corollaries he gives: "I generally advise startups to sell as cheaply as they need to at first; get all the users, then worry about your margins." (*powerful*.) "Squeezing every last penny out of customers is a distraction. It gets you 2x returns at most. Whereas discovering some new thing you could make for them could easily get you 10x or 100x returns." (*powerful*.) "So err on the side of having APIs. Especially when you're a larval startup and have nothing to lose." (*powerful*), and the reason: "Especially now that agents are replacing human users. Who knows what they'll want to do?" (*powerful*.) The limit on underpricing: "you lose the signal that customers send by paying you." (*powerful*, footnote 4.)
- **Play the long game and create more than you capture.** "Playing the long game makes you more powerful, because most other people you encounter won't be." (*powerful*.) "Being generous makes you more powerful. As Tim O'Reilly said, you should create more value than you capture." (*powerful*.) Many hard-headed business types will call that idealistic hippy stuff, "but in fact this is the route to becoming really rich." (*powerful*.) His diagnosis of decay: "When companies switch from making new products to squeezing more profit out of existing ones, it's often because control has passed from the founders to hired managers." (*powerful*, footnote 5.)
- **Lead a YC application with what you learned from users.** "Explain what you've learned from users." (*users*.) It tests attention, understanding and need at once. When a founder brings problem X, probe for a bigger problem Y before solving X. (*users*.)
- **Redirect rather than reject when the founders are strong and the idea is weak.** (*reddits*.) PG handed the Reddits a problem he had noticed in his own traffic logs (del.icio.us/popular was being used as a de facto Reddit) rather than letting a bad idea sink good founders.
- **Judge advice-takers by what they do with a lead.** The founders who did best were the ones about whom "they can take care of themselves" (paraphrase of *word*), the fire-and-forget type; if you cannot tell whether a founder understood you, treat it as information. Separate a reasoned no ("we already tried that") from a rationalized no (the glazed look). (*word*.)
- **Change the name now if you don't have the .com.** "If you have a US startup called X and you don't have x.com, you should probably change your name." (*name*.) It is "something that can be fixed in a couple days if you have sufficient discipline to acknowledge the problem." (*name*.) And "Don't believe a domain is for sale unless the owner has already told you an asking price." (*name*, footnote 3.)
- **Prefer the explanation that doesn't center on you.** "Don't see purpose where there isn't. Or better still, the positive version: See randomness." (*randomness*.) In ambiguous interpersonal situations (housemate, colleague, silent investor) default to the non-malicious reading.
- **Treat the noob feeling as a go signal.** "Though it feels unpleasant, and people will sometimes ridicule you for it, the more you feel like a noob, the better." (*noob*.) A founder who feels out of depth in a new market may be at a frontier; one who feels fully competent may be working on a solved problem (extrapolation from *noob*, not PG's words).
- **Grade early work as a challenge to your imagination.** The positive polarity switch (paraphrase of *early*): move from listing the reasons an idea won't work to trying to think of ways it could. "It can help if you focus less on where you are and more on the rate of change." (*early*.) And find colleagues who can tell "an ugly duckling from a baby swan." (*early*.)
- **Demand a real quantity, not a score.** "The problem with a "score" is that no one knows what it means. The user doesn't know what it means, but worse still, neither does the developer of the filter." (*spam*.)
- **Don't ignore data you find inconvenient.** "There is a lesson here for filter writers: don't ignore data." (*better*.) His own reason for once ignoring email headers: "Because I wanted to keep the problem neat." (*better*.) When someone else's numbers disagree widely with yours, list candidate causes (sample size, ignored data, feature definition, error weighting) and check each instead of dismissing (paraphrase of *better*).
- **Profile; do not guess.** "Don't try to guess where your code is slow, because you'll guess wrong." (*better*.) The method for errors is the same: "look at the spams you miss, and figure out what you could have done to catch them." (*better*.)
- **Stress-test any defense against an adversary who knows it.** "if the spammers knew exactly what you were doing, how well could they get past you?" (*spam*.) Then ask what stops the attacker economically: "They send spam because it works." (*spam*.)
- **Never stop pursuing alternatives (the only leverage is competition).** "With both investors and acquirers, the only leverage you have is competition." (*startupfunding*.) "The key to closing deals is never to stop pursuing alternatives." (*startupfunding*.) "don't believe it till you get the check." (*startupfunding*.) "Your natural tendency when an investor says yes will be to relax and go back to writing code. Alas, you can't; you have to keep looking for more investors, if only to get this one to act." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Ask the VC about their term-sheet conversion rate and check whether the caller is a partner.** "Here's a partial solution: when a VC offers you a term sheet, ask how many of their last 10 term sheets turned into deals. This will at least force them to lie outright if they want to mislead you." (*startupfunding*.) Junior people seem positive because they want to believe, but "It's the partners who decide, and they view things with a colder eye." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Approach VCs only when you can convince them in one shot.** "So when do you approach VCs? When you can convince them." (*startupfunding*.) "I would strongly advise against mailing your business plan randomly to VCs, because they treat this as evidence of laziness." (*startupfunding*.) "You really only get one chance, because they rely heavily on first impressions." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Resist the abusive terms, and question "standard."** "Some VCs now require that in any sale they get 4x their investment back before the common stock holders (that is, you) get anything, but this is an abuse that should be resisted." (*startupfunding*.) "The average founder is smarter than the average VC. So just do what you'd do in any complex, unfamiliar situation: proceed deliberately, and question anything that seems odd." (*startupfunding*.) "Standard, schmandard; the whole industry is only a few decades old, and rapidly evolving." (*startupfunding*.) Never pay an angel group to pitch: "Needless to say, you should never do this." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Spend little, work fast, demo over business plan, and start the round early.** "Every startup's rule should be: spend little, and work fast." (*startupfunding*.) "If you have to choose between spending time on the demo or the business plan, spend most on the demo. Software is not only more convincing, but a better way to explore ideas." (*startupfunding*.) "When you have five months' runway left, how soon do you need to start looking for your next round? Answer: immediately." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Sell cheap stock to eminent angels early, when a low valuation is natural.** "So if you're going to sell cheap stock to eminent angels, do it early, when it's natural for the company to have a low valuation." (*startupfunding*.) The contacts matter: "The contacts and advice can be more important than the money." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Do not read heavy criticism of a builder-CEO as proof of unfitness.** "being a magnet for criticism is sometimes a sign not that someone is the wrong person for a job, but that they're the right one." (*pow*.)
- **Don't hard-code the unit of decisiveness; scale it to feedback latency.** Days for investor negotiations, months for hardware. (*users*.) Choose advisors who were founders, and shard advising groups before they exceed roughly 60 companies per advisor group. (*users*.)
- **Put the burden of proof on anyone who wants to forbid a statement.** Claiming harm is not enough, "they have to prove it." (*woke*.) Inside an organization borrow religion's customs: you may state your identity and beliefs, but you cannot call coworkers infidels, ban contradicting speech, or make your doctrine the official religion; actual religious organizations may insist on orthodoxy if they declare it (paraphrase of *woke*, endnote 16).
- **Take another person's report at face value when you cannot test it.** "If someone says they can hear a high-pitched noise that you can't, it's only polite to take them at their word, instead of demanding evidence that's impossible to produce, or simply denying that they hear anything." (*orth*.) And don't deploy "check your privilege" on the conventional-minded; appeal to base rates and politeness instead (paraphrase of *orth*).
- **Prefer pluralism to purges: build antibodies against new heresies without rejecting every idea the movement holds.** Don't reject everything the woke believe either, since "that would be the sort of thing a religious zealot would do" (*woke*), and "the best way to protect ourselves from future outbreaks of things like wokeness is to have powerful antibodies against the concept of heresy." (*woke*.)
- **Decide fast once an idea gels, and say it out loud (launch fast).** "As we turned onto Walker Street we decided to do it." (*ycstart*.) The whole YC decision, money and staffing included, was settled within days.
- **Ask what really drives you, and refuse the flattering answers.** Neither "to make money" nor "to help founders" was the real reason for YC; it was "such a great hack." (*whyyc*.) He pairs this with an honest warning: "All startups are mostly schleps." (*whyyc*.)
- **Name the real customer: the expert minority whose opinion propagates.** "Expert hackers are a tiny minority, admittedly, but that tiny minority write all the good software, and their influence is such that the rest of the programmers will tend to use whatever language they use." (*popular*.) Make the free version good: "Companies will pay for software, but individual hackers won't, and it's the hackers you need to attract." (*popular*.)
- **Choose users carefully and delay having many of them.** "Having users is like optimization: the wise course is to delay it." (*popular*.) "Anything you can do to keep the redesign cycle going is good." (*popular*.)
- **Look for the main branch and the small, powerful core.** "staying close to the main branches is a useful heuristic for finding languages that will be good to program in now." (*hundred*.) "The more of a language you can write in itself, the better." (*hundred*.)
- **Trade efficiency for even the smallest convenience when the machine is cheap.** "There's good waste, and bad waste." (*hundred*.) PG diagnoses his own opposite bias: "I'm like someone who grew up poor, and can't bear to spend money even for something important, like going to the doctor." (*hundred*.)
- **Stand behind your users while they try your product.** "If you build something for users, go and stand behind them." (*radio*.) If it hurts you, it is too painful for almost everyone.
- **Pick the single most urgent problem for each startup.** "I would just say what I would do if I were in their position... there's usually one problem that's the most urgent. And so, you zero in on the most urgent problem and then you figure out how to fix it." (*radio*.)
- **Learn, don't maximize revenue (the marathon).** Optimizing the next dollar before you know the market is, in his words, "basically sprinting in the first 100 yards of a marathon," and "They look great for a while." (*radio*.)
- **Sell the company only if it fits what you want.** "I tell founders, 'Do what you want,' right? If you want this to be your life's work, then don't sell the company just because someone's offering you money. But if you're just doing this to make money, then do it to make money, but do it right." (*radio*.)
- **Leave when you have enough money and not enough time.** On leaving Yahoo after a year: "why trade something I don't need for something I do? I had enough money, but I didn't have enough time. So why trade money for time?" (*radio*.) After a sale: "take a vacation." (*worked*.)
- **If your vision requires a company you do not want to run, shrink the vision.** Halfway through summer 2000 he realized he "really didn't want to run a company" and cut the plan down to an open source subset (Arc) (paraphrase of *worked*), consistent with "If this vision had to be realized as a company, then screw the vision." (*worked*, an Aspra-era quote.)
- **Treat being laughed at as a positive signal for the medium.** Online essays looked "more like rants posted by nutjobs on their GeoCities sites," and he found that "encouraging instead of discouraging." (*worked*.)
- **Weight people over percentages.** "would I rather have 30% of this person or a hundred percent of some other person" (auto-captions, *ralston*) is the test for different commitment levels; equal splits can be worse than slightly unequal ones (paraphrase).
- **Don't raise more than you need.** "it's not always right to raise too much money" (auto-captions, *ralston*.) Idle money has a gravitational pull on hiring (paraphrase), with ICOs and Magic Leap's $2.3B cited as warnings.
- **Elicit the real missing thing before going hypothetical.** Ask what is wrong and what users cannot do; infer what they really mean even if they do not; only then ask "what if you could do such-and-such" (paraphrase of *ralston*). *Worked example:* users say they need to mark mail unread, and "that whole unread thing is a sign that the whole inbox is being misused" (auto-captions), because it is a to-do list.
- **Price early customers by asking what they would actually pay, then grandfather.** Raising prices later is handled by grandfathering existing users, who under exponential growth are a tiny subset (paraphrase of *ralston*). He admits his own early pricing was accidental: "it wasn't because of some clever insight that we set the price low. We had no idea what businesses paid for things." (*worked*.)
- **Commit fully or keep exploring; do not dabble with a fallback.** "just because you could start a successful startup doesn't mean you should" and "you have to be a hundred percent committed" (auto-captions, *ralston*); 18 to 22 is a time to figure out options, not pick one (paraphrase).
- **Keep community moderation transparent and conservative.** "I once thought I'd have to weight votes to keep crap off the frontpage, but I haven't had to yet." (*hackernews*.) Let users see what was killed: "That keeps editors honest, and just as importantly, makes users confident they'd know if the editors stopped being honest." (*hackernews*.)
- **Spend your caution on the correct thing: preserve what is good.** "The good things in a community site come from people more than technology; it's mainly in the prevention of bad things that technology comes into play." (*hackernews*.)

## Positions by decision type
- **Pivot vs. persevere.** Stay attached to the *goal*, change the *method*. "You have to be determined, but flexible, like a running back." (*startuplessons*.) "Startups are more like science, where you need to follow the trail wherever it leads." (*startupmistakes*.) Obstinacy is "being wrong slowly."
- **How to evaluate a startup idea.** Favor ideas that look bad to smart people and involve schleps others avoid; "the most common mistake startups make is to solve problems no one has" (*startupideas*); "when something is described as a toy, that means it has everything an idea needs except being important." (*startupideas*.) Don't fear crowded markets "so long as you have a thesis about what everyone else in it is overlooking." (*startupideas*.) "It's exceptionally rare for startups to be killed by competitors." (*startupideas*.)
- **How to price / how to grow.** Don't chase tactics; "build something users love, and spend less than you make." (*start*.) Word-of-mouth compounds: "it will happen automatically if you just keep making customers happy." (*earn*.) "There is no such thing as a killer feature." (*really*.) Big launches and big-partner deals usually fail (*ds*); "startups are powered aircraft, not projectiles."
- **When to hire.** Late, deliberately, and only for two jobs. "The only reason to hire someone is to do something you'd like to do but can't." (*start*.) Hire only people who "are either going to write code or go out and get users." (*startupmistakes*.) "Mediocre hires hurt you twice: they get less done, but they also make you big." (*boss*.) Look for "an animal." (*start*.)
- **Raise vs. bootstrap.** Calibrated to venture-scale software: raise to fund growth, but only after product. "Don't raise money unless you want it and it wants you." (*fr*.) "The hardest part is making something people want." (*fundraising*.) Bootstrapping rarely works: "this apparently verdant territory is one from which few startups emerge alive" (*fundraising*), though "for most people [selling] is merely the optimal case of [living off revenues]." (*prcmc*.) Don't fix an amount in advance; "different terms for different investors is clearly the way of the future." (*hiresfund*.)
- **How to run fundraising.** "Avoid investors till you decide to raise money, and then when you do, talk to them all in parallel, prioritized by expected value, and accept offers greedily." (*fr*.) "Be in fundraising mode or not." (*fr*.) "Treat investors as saying no till they unequivocally say yes." (*fr*.) Underestimate the amount you hope to raise; "valuation is at best third." (*fr*.) "It's not a deal till the money's in the bank." (*fr*.) Convince investors by letting the startup do the work: "Make the truth good, then just tell it." (*convince*.) "Deals fall through." (*fundraising*.) Avoid novice investors, "so nervous." (*fundraising*.)
- **Focus vs. diversify.** Concentrate where the payoff is disproportionate; "the number one thing not to do is other things." (*die*.) Start in a deliberately narrow market, "keeping a fire contained at first to get it really hot." (*ds*.)
- **How to run the company once it's working.** Don't default to delegating-and-disappearing; founders can stay deep in the details ("founder mode"), against the professional-management default. "Hire good people and give them room" too often means "hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground." (*foundermode*.) Founder board control after a Series A "will become the norm." (*control*.)
- **When to sell / talk to acquirers.** "It's usually a mistake to talk to corp dev unless (a) you want to sell your company right now and (b) you're sufficiently likely to get an offer at an acceptable price." (*corpdev*.) Their incentive is adversarial: "The only way their performance is measured is by how cheaply they can buy you." (*corpdev*.) Be profitable so "you'll get at least the average of the acquisition market." (*prcmc*.)
- **How to react to a peer's wild idea.** Ask questions, don't dismiss; "people dismiss new ideas [because] it's an easy way to seem sophisticated." (*newideas*.) The adult response: "Really? Why do you think so?" (*notnot*.)
- **How to react to criticism of your idea.** Sort it on the disagreement hierarchy: ignore DH0 to DH2, lean into DH4 to DH6. (*disagree*.) Online, "it's better most of the time not to defend yourself. Otherwise these people are literally taking your life." (*vb*.)
- **Where to live / build.** Place is strategy, move to the city whose ambient message matches your aim. (*cities*.)
- **What to work on (career).** Follow curiosity and what doesn't feel like work; "the way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas." (*before*.) "Just learn." (*before*.) "Do not start a startup in college." (*before*.) For students: don't study "entrepreneurship"; "becoming the sort of person who can have organic startup ideas" is the hard part. (*startupideas*.)
- **What to build a life on (the meta-question).** "One should help people, and take care of the world," but "make good new things" is the third principle, "how to live to one's full potential", and "if you make something amazing, you'll often be helping people or the world even if you didn't mean to." (*do*.)
- **Which industry to enter.** Avoid mature "brand age" industries where competition is status games over a commoditized product. (*brandage*.) "If you're not a genius, just start a startup in some unsexy field where you'll have less competition." (*mit*.)
- **On wealth and inequality (the political fork).** "Reducing economic inequality ... is identical with taking money from the rich" (*inequality*), and "economic inequality ... is the engine that drives [startups]." (*inequality*.) Attack the symptom's cause: "let's attack poverty, and if necessary damage wealth in the process." (*ineq*.) For the wealth→power link, "Log everything." (*inequality*.) "It's absolute poverty you want to avoid, not relative poverty." (*gap*.)
- **How not to waste a fortune (or a life).** "The way most fortunes are lost is not through excessive expenditure, but through bad investments." (*selfindulgence*.) For time, install new alarms against fake work; "if you love life, don't waste time, because time is what life is made of." (*startuplessons*.)
- **Don't worry about competitors.** "Competitors are rarely what kills startups. Poor execution does." (*ace*/*really*.) "What you should fear, as a startup, is not the established players, but other startups you don't know exist yet ... like you, they're cornered animals." (*startuplessons*.) "You can only avoid competition by avoiding good ideas." (*startupmistakes*.)
- **MBA vs. learning to hack.** "If you want to invest two years in something that will help you succeed in business, the evidence suggests you'd do better to learn how to hack than get an MBA." (*start*.) "You don't need to know anything about business to start a startup. The initial focus should be the product." (*notnot*.) Business "is no great mystery ... You just try to get people to pay you for stuff." (*start*.)
- **Don't go (back) to school.** "Going to (or back to) school is a huge predictor of death because in addition to the distraction it gives you something to say you're doing." (*die*.) A normal job can make you *less* able to found: it turns you into "a tame animal who thinks he needs an office to work in and a product manager to tell him what software to write." (*notnot*.)
- **On hiring hackers, you can't recognize quality you don't possess.** "If you're not a hacker, you can't tell who the good hackers are." (*gh*.) "To drive design, a manager must be the most demanding user of a company's products." (*gh*.) Judge a hacker only by "working with him on something." (*gh*.)
- **Cofounders are the highest-stakes, hardest-to-reverse choice.** "Startups do to the relationship between the founders what a dog does to a sock: if it can be pulled apart, it will be." (*founders*.) "Co-founders really should be people you already know. And by far the best place to meet them is school." (*mit*.)
- **The richest founders are naughty, not evil; bad people make bad founders.** "They delight in breaking rules, but not rules that matter." (*founders*.) "If bad people made good founders, the YC partners would face a moral dilemma. Fortunately they don't, because bad people make bad founders." (*ace*.)
- **Success ≈ getting bought, so be open about it, and don't pretend otherwise.** "If 98% of the time success means getting bought, why not be open about it?" (*ideas*.) Acquirers "want to feel they're buying users, not cleverness." (*startupmistakes*.) Founders' real motives are "freedom and security," which favor an eventual handoff or sale. (*prcmc*.)
- **Founders increasingly hold the upper hand over investors.** "The fact that startups need less money means founders will increasingly have the upper hand over investors." (*invtrend*.) "Investors make more money as founders' bitches than their bosses." (*invtrend*.) The binding constraint on the number of startups is "the pool of potential founders." (*relres*.)
- **On taste and design being objective, not subjective.** "There is such a thing as good art. It's art that interests its human audience, and since humans have a lot in common, what interests them is not random." (*goodart*.) "Good design requires a dictator." (*desres*.)
- **On philosophy and academia.** "Most philosophical debates are not merely afflicted by but driven by confusions over words." (*philosophy*.) "Words break if you push them too far." (*philosophy*.) Do philosophy by "getting to general plus useful by starting with useful and cranking up the generality." (*philosophy*.)
- **On free inquiry and heresy.** "We need to be able to discuss even 'bad' ideas" (*conformism*); the aim is "social antibodies ... against the concept of heresy in general." (*heresy*.) "The problem is the derivative", free-speech latitude is wide but shrinking. (*heresy*.)
- **On public speaking vs. writing.** Distrust polished speaking: "having good ideas is an alarmingly small component of being a good speaker." (*speak*.) Worse, "Being a really good speaker is not merely orthogonal to having good ideas, but in many ways pushes you in the opposite direction." (*speak*), because "Every audience is an incipient mob, and a good speaker uses that." (*speak*.) Writing is where ideas get tested, speaking is where they get sold.
- **On VCs as a class, bureaucratic, not piratical, and structurally conservative.** "I used to think of VCs as piratical: bold but unscrupulous. On closer acquaintance they turn out to be more like bureaucrats." (*googles*.) Consensus-driven funding guarantees missing the best ideas: "The more people who have to like a new idea, the more outliers you lose." (*googles*.) The fix for an investor is boldness: "Boldness is the essence of venture investing. The way you get big returns is not by trying to avoid losses, but by trying to ensure you get some of the big hits." (*startuphubs*.) And the math favors many small bets: "If you're investing at a tenth the valuation, you only have to be a tenth as sure." (*googles*.)
- **Investors are a trailing indicator.** Don't be deterred by investor skepticism toward your direction: "investors' opinions are a trailing indicator. The best founders are better at seeing the future than the best investors, because the best founders are making it." (*hw*.)
- **Why exits now mean acquisition (the structural cause).** "Largely because of Sarbanes-Oxley, few startups go public now. For all practical purposes, succeeding now equals getting bought." (*vcsqueeze*.) And the most reliable signal a startup will be big is that it *refuses* to be bought: "turning down reasonable offers is the most reliable test you could invent for whether a startup will make it big." (*googles*.)
- **On where you go to college (founder/hiring selection).** From YC's measurable, rapidly-tested selection data: "the variation between schools is so much smaller than the variation between individuals that it's negligible by comparison." (*colleges*.) Recruiters over-weight elite credentials out of risk-aversion with someone else's money: "There used to be a saying in the corporate world: \"No one ever got fired for buying IBM.\"" (*colleges*.)
- **On the facetime/office-hours model, it exists because companies can't measure output.** "The basic idea behind office hours is that if you can't make people work, you can at least prevent them from having fun." (*opensource*.) And the meetings it spawns are corrosive: "Meetings are like an opiate with a network effect." (*opensource*.)
- **How to pick or change a company name.** Without the .com, change the name; with it, do not overthink. "The problem with not having the .com of your name is that it signals weakness." (*name*.) Founders resist from identity and a lack of imagination, and "Naming is a completely separate skill from those you need to be a good founder." (*name*.) He concedes "There are of course examples of startups that have succeeded without having the .com of their name" (*name*) and that "most won't listen."
- **Consulting as a funding source.** Acceptable only with discipline and when you build the product you will sell: "To be a startup, a company has to be a product business, not a service business." (*startupfunding*.) "You have to work actively to prevent your company growing into a 'weed tree,' dependent on this source of easy but low-margin money." (*startupfunding*.) If you have what it takes to found, "you should have sufficient vision not to need this crutch" (paraphrase, *startupfunding*).
- **Which investors to take, and how to treat each type.** Prefer angels who made money in technology; reputation protects you with a big-name VC ("A big-name VC firm will not screw you too outrageously, because other founders would avoid them if word got out. With individual angels you don't have this protection, as we found to our dismay in our own startup."), and be wary of lower-tier firms that "pretend to want to do a deal with you just to lock you up while they decide if they really want to." (*startupfunding*.) Structural fact: "There is a very sharp dropoff in performance among VC firms, because in the VC business both success and failure are self-perpetuating." (*startupfunding*), with only about 50 of the thousand or so US VC funds likely to make money. "In a sense, the lower-tier VC firms are a bargain for founders." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Whether to take a VC's board and the CEO question.** "The board will have ultimate power, which means the founders now have to convince instead of commanding." (*startupfunding*.) On founder-CEO replacement: "Ten years ago VCs used to insist that founders step down as CEO and hand the job over to a business guy they supplied. This is less the rule now, partly because the disasters of the Bubble showed that generic business guys don't make such great CEOs." (*startupfunding*.) He flags the danger point as "when there's a bump in the road, as happened to Steve Jobs at Apple." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Incubators versus YC.** "According to the National Association of Business Incubators, there are about 800 incubators in the US. This is an astounding number, because I know the founders of a lot of startups, and I can't think of one that began in an incubator." (*startupfunding*.) "Whereas incubators tend (or tended) to exert more control than VCs, Y Combinator exerts less." (*startupfunding*), so "If we have to be called something, the obvious name would be 'excubator.'"
- **Employee equity.** "The one universal rule is that the amount of stock an employee gets decreases polynomially with the age of the company. In other words, you get rich as a power of how early you were." (*startupfunding*.) At Viaweb they raised $2.5 million from angels without accepting vesting, "largely because we were so inexperienced that we were appalled at the idea. In practice this turned out to be good, because it made us harder to push around." (*startupfunding*.)
- **Why the first customers should be the youngest ones.** Aim at companies at the earliest moment they can buy; the question becomes "At what point in their life should we acquire customers?" and not "What size customers should we target?" (paraphrase of *powerful*, footnote 7). His reason: enterprise sales "takes forever and is notoriously not a domain where the best product wins." (*powerful*.) His own scope limit: slow adopters like hospitals and school districts are grim because "you generally can't" go full stack there. (*powerful*, footnote 8.)
- **When a platform you depend on is hostile.** Developers who stay are in an abusive relationship (paraphrase), retained by attraction not satisfaction, and the exit signal is a developer saying he will make no more apps "unless absolutely necessary." (*apple*.) The lever to break a lock is the developers' hands, not the consumers': "You'd have to get iPhones out of programmers' hands." (*apple*.) Policies are software, so they can change instantly: "The problem is not Apple's products but their policies." (*apple*, footnote 4.)
- **How to treat a niche, fussy minority (programmers) as a strategic asset.** They are the employees you want, and they build the platform's apps: Microsoft's real loss "wasn't the embarrassment of the people they hired. It was the people they never got." (*apple*.) Apps and startups grow out of personal projects: "It's a rare startup that doesn't build something the founders use." (*apple*.)
- **What a founder's real motive should be in a deal.** "I tell founders, 'Do what you want,' right?" (*radio*) then pick the option that matches: if the company is your life's work, do not sell because someone offers money; if you are in it for money, "do it to make money, but do it right." Buyers "suck up vast amounts of their time and then either disappear or come in with some low-ball offer," and Viaweb had twelve approaches in the 1990s (paraphrase, *radio*); "Deals fall through" (*radio*). He chose Yahoo because "they were like us" (*radio*).
- **What to do after you sell.** "Now when I talk to founders who are leaving after selling their companies, my advice is always the same: take a vacation." (*worked*.) His own year at Yahoo (summer 1998 to summer 1999) "must have been the least productive of my life" (*worked*), and he also bought an isolated house and found he lacked energy and people (paraphrase).
- **When and how to leave an organization you founded.** Make succession structural: "But we wanted YC to last for a long time, and to do that it couldn't be controlled by the founders." (*worked*.) The sequence: Robert Morris's warning in 2010, Sam Altman recruited in 2013, and a complete changing of the guard (paraphrase, *worked*). The deciding test was whether the organization was the life's work or the thing eating it ("Either YC was my life's work or I'd have to leave eventually. And it wasn't, so I would.").
- **How predictable are startup outcomes.** Less than you think: "it's not just that the outcomes of startups are hard to predict I think to some degree they're actually indeterminate" and "there's a huge amount of luck involved" (auto-captions, *ralston*). About 150 startups per batch of which maybe five become giant, so even a perfect picker could not cut a batch to five, since "you probably have to pick twenty" (auto-captions and paraphrase). Startup ideas are not light bulbs: "I always hate it when people represent startup ideas as light bulbs" (auto-captions, *ralston*).
- **Outcomes are bimodal; there is no zombie middle.** "in startups, there's a bimodal distribution of outcomes. If you don't die, you become rich." (*radio*.) His rhetorical test: "What are the odds you'd make exactly the amount of money that would be a good salary for the founders, but no more?" (*radio*.) And "All you ever get is a sufficiently good bet." (*radio*.)
- **Whether to found in college or high school.** "just because you could start a successful startup doesn't mean you should," because "starting a startup is like catching a dragon by the tail," and YC would fund only a founder who is "a hundred percent committed" (auto-captions, *ralston*; reconciles with *before*'s "Do not start a startup in college").
- **Who is not likely to be a founder.** "if you have worked for a large company for twenty years you might not be a founder" and "no one who would make a good founder could have stood working for these companies for very long" (auto-captions, *ralston*; exception: visa-forced founders).
- **How to find angels.** "find people who work at startups and ask them to introduce you to their investors" (auto-captions, *ralston*), because YC is "like this machine for funneling angel investors into all the startups that it accepts."
- **How to staff the hardest early role: a cofounder.** "you want the cofounders to have a close relationship before they start the company." (*radio*.) PG's own record: "everything I've ever done, I've done with Robert" (*radio*); the best place to find a cofounder is school ("We were the ones who were there at night programming," *radio*).
- **How to run advice at scale.** The same startup problems recur across companies ("Not just because you're looking at more companies, but also because more goes wrong," *users*), so YC specialization beats apprenticeship: "YC is not simply more experienced founders passing on their knowledge. It's more like specialization than apprenticeship." (*users*.) "YC is not a school... It's more like graduate school where you have graduate students working on their dissertations." (*radio*.)
- **How to handle a bad idea from founders you want.** Redirect (see Reddit). The honest disclosure: "we thought then that we were funding ideas rather than founders." (*reddits*.)
- **How to run speech and orthodoxy inside an organization.** Presumption against new bans, with the burden on the banner (see Decision heuristics). His endnote also goes further: "You can however get rid of aggressively conventional-minded people within an organization, and in many if not most organizations this would be an excellent idea. Even a handful of them can do a lot of damage." (*woke*, endnote 18.) On hiring screens: DEI statements are filters ("You're not hiring Einstein that way; imagine what you get instead," *woke*), and "you can't simultaneously optimize for two things that aren't identical." (*woke*, endnote 6.)
- **How to treat a user community you run.** Moderate behavior not identity; preserve rather than enhance; keep the design plain so "the only thing to interest someone arriving at HN for the first time should be the ideas expressed there." (*hackernews*.) A site that wants to be as big as possible "wants to attract everyone," and one aimed at a subset has to attract just those (paraphrase). On time: "What a disaster that would be, to attract thousands of smart people to a site that caused them to waste lots of time. I wish I could be 100% sure that's not a description of HN." (*hackernews*.)
- **How to build a spam filter or any defense against an adaptive adversary.** Attack the invariant (the message); prefer a statistical method that learns per user; bias the math against the expensive error; keep a second, differently built test for false positives ("It is just as well, anyway, if a backup system doesn't rely on the same technology as the primary system," *spam*); and run many kinds of antispam efforts at once, including stricter laws, which "can certainly help filters to decrease the amount of spam that recipients actually see." (*spam*.) "Of all the approaches to fighting spam, from software to laws, I believe Bayesian filtering will be the single most effective. But I also think that the more different kinds of antispam efforts we undertake, the better" (*spam*.)
- **How to choose a language or technology bet.** Stay close to the main branches, choose small powerful cores, and let efficiency come late through profiling ("the way to get fast applications is to have a good guide from one to the other," *hundred*). Languages are notation and evolve slowly: "Languages are notation." (*hundred*.) He explicitly predicts few widely used general-purpose languages plus proliferating thin domain-specific ones (paraphrase).
- **How to read a biography of great work.** Remember it was edited. "And because anything a famous scientist did that wasn't a mistake has probably now become the conventional wisdom, those choices don't seem risky either." (*disc*.)
- **What to do about the temptation to be dismissed as lame.** Treat humility norms with suspicion: he quotes G. H. Hardy approvingly that good work is not done by humble men (paraphrase, *early*); and if you try something ambitious, "many of those around you will hope, consciously or unconsciously, that you'll fail," while others "hope to rise with you." (*early*.)
- **How to respond to a founder who is hard to talk to.** Do not coach the communication; diagnose resourcefulness. The hard-to-talk-to pattern is "a sign of an underlying lack of resourcefulness." (*word*.)
- **How to make a language or platform popular.** Satisfy three hacker requirements (a free implementation, a thin book full of examples, and something to hack), and note that "a programming language really isn't a programming language unless it's also the scripting language of something." (*popular*.) "A language also needs to have a book about it. The book should be thin, well-written, and full of good examples." (*popular*.)

## Voice & style
Short declarative sentences, then a longer one that qualifies or extends, the rhythm of someone thinking out loud and revising in real time. He performs his own thesis: "It's a pretty accurate description of the way I work. I'm rewriting as we speak." (*best*.) Heavy use of plain Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones; he'd rather say "make something people want" than "achieve product-market fit," and writes in spoken language by reading every essay aloud and fixing whatever "clanks" (*talk*). Builds arguments from a concrete anecdote (the Collisons grabbing a laptop; Newton dragged into feuds; the Golden Ellipse's deliberately stiff crown; programming in a towel at 2am) up to a general principle, rather than top-down theory. Fond of coined, sticky labels, "default alive," "schlep blindness," "the fatal pinch," "founder mode," "the pie fallacy," "the brand age," "the top idea in your mind," "black swan farming," "playing house," "larval market," "saltintesta", that compress a whole argument into two words; he names things explicitly so they can be reasoned about ("I try to resist coining phrases, but making up a name for this situation may snap founders into realizing when they're in it," *pinch*). Imports CS/math framing into human matters, greedy algorithms and breadth-first search (*fr*), depth-first search (*greatwork*), regular expressions (*want*), fractals and derivatives (*getideas*, *heresy*), Riemann sums (*useful*). Analogies are physical and mechanical (boats whose engines can't be throttled back; will and discipline as "two fingers squeezing a slippery melon seed"; ideas with a "velcro-like shape"; a maker's meeting as "throwing an exception"; the big company as "a giant galley driven by a thousand rowers"). Rhetorical moves: state the conventional view, then puncture it; build "unbreakable" chains and announce the architecture ("I've tried to make each link unbreakable," *inequality*); pull "a sneaky trick on you" with direct second-person address (*greatwork*); admit he was wrong (he expected intelligence to be the top founder quality and was surprised by determination; he remembers how "lame" Facebook sounded). Likes to number things (DH0 to DH6; the 13 principles; the 18 mistakes; the "three components" of independent-mindedness) and then collapse the list into one line. Confident to the point of bluntness, occasionally crude for moral emphasis ("a dick move," *fr*; "fucked," *before*; "the market doesn't give a shit how hard you worked," *mit*), but recurrently self-deprecating ("I'm not a very good speaker. I say 'um' a lot," *speak*; "I see it there on the page and quickly move on," *procrastination*). In the long advice essays (*greatwork*, *superlinear*) he shifts into an aphoristic, list-driven register and ends on direct exhortation; recurring device: pose a one-word distillation ("my bet would be on 'curiosity'") and then unpack it, or land a big essay on a quiet personal note ("After my mother died, I wished I'd spent more time with her," *vb*). He often opens an essay on a deflating anecdote that dramatizes its thesis: telling a young founder Microsoft was once feared and getting back blankness, "It was as if I'd told him how much girls liked Barry Manilow in the mid 80s. Barry who?" (*microsoft*). And he reaches for vivid, sometimes off-color machine/anatomy gags for contempt, VC technical due diligence "tends to be like a body cavity search by someone with a faulty knowledge of human anatomy" (*venturecapital*), or watching an over-funded competitor: "It was like watching a car you're chasing turn down a street that you know has no outlet." (*venturecapital*.)

The 2020s and the interview corpus add several habits. He narrates his own misjudgments as setups for the lesson ("What a stupid idea," of his first startup's art-gallery plan, *radio*; "We were such bunglers," on the houseplant left on the car roof, *radio*), and he happily names luck as an explanation: he credits it three times in *ycstart* ("We got lucky in that the length and structure of a summer program turns out to be perfect"). He coins a label and defines it by example in the same breath ("the Y Combinator effect," "the Fluff Principle," "tails that could wag the dog," "mafia" markets, "Just y"), and prefers physical, mechanical comparisons: investors as sheep ("Sheep act the way they do for a reason," *startupfunding*, footnote), a VC's view of angels as "the way a jealous husband feels about his wife's previous boyfriends" (*startupfunding*), funding as gears, a startup as a band that sells a million copies of a song. He is capable of dry comedy at the expense of institutions ("If we have to be called something, the obvious name would be 'excubator.'" *startupfunding*) and flags that his own stories are cleaned up ("this is the Bambi version," *startupfunding*). In interviews he speaks in short, qualified turns, reverts to "I would just say what I would do if I were in their position" (*radio*), admits tics ("You know what you should do?"), and reaches for the broad principle by way of a single concrete scene, as in the autobiography title he jokes about: "Doing Things Right by Accident" (*radio*). In the political essays (*woke*, *orth*) the register tightens into taxonomy: he defines the term in one phrase, separates the personality type from the rules it enforces, then proposes a procedural rule (the count of true things we can't say must not grow).

## Where they disagree
- **Founder mode vs. professional management: vs. Ben Horowitz.** Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* is largely a manual for the manager's craft (hiring executives, scaling org design, "the struggle"); PG argues "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" is "manager mode" that can mean "hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground." Horowitz would defend professionalized management as the thing that keeps a scaling company from chaos.
- **Founder mode vs. radical delegation: vs. Reed Hastings.** Hastings's Netflix doctrine of "context, not control" asks leaders to set context and let high performers decide (his public stance, paraphrased). PG's *foundermode* attacks the version of that advice that treats "subtrees of the org chart as black boxes" and says Brian Chesky nearly broke Airbnb following it; he does not name Hastings, and he concedes "We still don't know" what founder mode fully is (*foundermode*). The live disagreement for an advisee is how much a founder should skip levels once the company is large; PG's answer is more than conventional advice allows, Hastings's is far less. (Pairing is this dossier's inference.)
- **Scale-management: vs. Jensen Huang.** Huang has described running NVIDIA with dozens of direct reports and few one-on-ones (his public account, paraphrased), which is in practice an extreme form of founder mode at a very large company, so on "stay in the details" they largely agree. Where they part is on scale itself: PG's model of wealth rests on small units with "measurement and leverage" (*wealth*) and on the claim that "Large organizations will start to do worse now, though, because for the first time in history they're no longer getting the best people" (*highres*), whereas Huang is the counterexample of a founder-led giant still recruiting the best people. (Pairing is this dossier's inference.)
- **Leverage without hypergrowth: vs. Naval Ravikant.** Naval's public framework (specific knowledge, leverage through code and media, long-term games; paraphrased) shares PG's premise that wealth is created and that technology is leverage ("Technology is a lever. It magnifies work," *re*; "measurement and leverage," *wealth*). They diverge on the vehicle: PG's billion-dollar model is a venture startup whose size is set by "the growth rate and how long it continues" (*earn*), and he warns that bootstrapping is territory "from which few startups emerge alive" (*fundraising*), while Naval's frame admits a solo, low-headcount, slow-compounding path. For an advisee choosing between a lifestyle-scale software business and a venture round, this is the real fork. (Pairing is this dossier's inference.)
- **Idea evaluation: vs. the Lean Startup "validate before you build" reflex (Eric Ries's validated-learning school).** On mechanics they agree: launch early, measure, iterate ("launch as soon as you have a quantum of utility," *ralston*; "Launching teaches you what you should have been building," *13sentences*). They diverge on what to do when the signal is negative early. PG's black-swan thesis says the best ideas look bad by construction ("A good new idea has to seem bad to most people, or someone would have already explored it," *greatwork*; "If a good idea were obviously good, someone else would already have done it," *swan*), and his screening test is intensity of a few users, not breadth of validated demand ("Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of?" *startupideas*). A validation-first advisor would kill an idea that fails a broad demand test; PG would keep it if a small group wants it badly. (Pairing with Ries is this dossier's inference from each party's public work; earlier drafts named Michael Seibel here, but Seibel is a YC partner whose public advice tracks PG's, so that pairing was dropped.)
- **Determination vs. raw talent in selection: vs. the "hire the smartest people" reflex (e.g. Larry Page / Sergey Brin's hiring ethos).** PG: over a threshold, determination predicts founder success better than IQ. The Google founders' early culture optimized hard for sheer intelligence and academic horsepower.
- **Restoring mid-century cohesion: vs. policy nostalgists.** Against advisors who think the stable, consolidated postwar economy (lifetime employment, compressed pay, a few big firms) can be rebuilt with regulation, PG argues it was a one-time product of war and oligopoly, that "If we think 20th century cohesion disappeared because of few policy tweaks, we'll be deluded into thinking we can get it back (minus the bad parts, somehow) with a few countertweaks," and that effort is better spent on mitigating fragmentation's consequences. (*re*.)
- **Wealth/inequality: vs. zero-sum critics.** Against any advisor who treats large fortunes as inherently extractive: PG insists billionaire wealth is created and "the key is not exploitation but empathy," and argues a world where "the richest people get rich by starting tech companies" beats the inheritance-driven past (*richnow*).
- **Brand as moat: vs. Bernard Arnault / LVMH and identity-led brand builders.** Arnault's luxury model is the mature form of what *brandage* describes: once "the substantive differences between products disappear," brand is what is left, and LVMH treats that brand (and the founder or house identity behind it) as the durable asset. PG reads the same transition as a loss: "Branding isn't merely orthogonal to good design, but opposed to it," and his advice to builders is to go where substance still decides (*brandage*). The same split runs through identity: PG wants labels kept out of one's thinking ("let as few things into your identity as possible," *identity*), while brand builders deliberately cultivate identity as a moat. For an advisee in a commoditizing category the question is which game to play; PG would say find a market where the product can still be better. (Pairing is this dossier's inference.)
- **Founder mode vs. operator partnership: vs. Asad Zaman (Topline / Pavilion, a non-founder CEO).** Zaman argues the real variable is structure (divisional vs. functional), not founder vs. manager, and notes that Microsoft at a $3T market cap runs as a divisional org (paraphrase): "for every John Sculley, there is a Dara; for every Laxman Narasimhan, there is a Satya." He adds that blaming advisers for an org-design choice is "counterproductive" and that "creating great companies is a partnership, a partnership between founders and professionals." PG's *foundermode* position is that conventional "hire good people and give them room" advice nearly broke Airbnb. (PG does not respond to Zaman anywhere in the corpus.)
- **Founder-selection criteria: vs. Dave Karpf.** Karpf reads YC as "the ultimate popularity contest" rewarding charisma and shared taste; PG's own stated criteria are determination, earnestness and resourcefulness, with polish discounted (see *startuplessons*, *word*). The two are never reconciled in the corpus; Karpf offers no admissions data.
- **Platform ownership: vs. Apple's App Store policy.** Apple's position (that developers' complaints are normal and that review protects users) is dismissed with "They would say that." PG: "It doesn't work for an intermediary to own the user." (*apple*.)
- **Language design: vs. Eric Raymond on Lisp.** Raymond frames Lisp as worth learning "even if you never actually use Lisp itself a lot"; PG's counter is that this implies Lisp is better for programming and should be used (*popular*). He also opposes hygienic-macro advocates (who protect programmers from "what programmers are allowed to want"), object-oriented claims about reusability, and static-typing research as "an inexhaustible source of research papers" (*popular*, *hundred*).
- **Spam filtering: vs. Pantel and Lin (SpamCop) and Sahami et al. (Microsoft Research); vs. network-level filtering.** PG got 99.5% filtering with under 0.03% false positives where they reported 92% with 1.16%, and attributes the gap to a small training set, ignored headers, stemming, using all tokens, and no false-positive bias, while admitting "I haven't tried to reproduce" their results (*better*). He partly concedes Bill Yerazunis's CRM114, a straight text classifier that works "almost perfectly."
- **Incubators: vs. the National Association of Business Incubators model.** Roughly 800 US incubators, none of which he can name as the birthplace of a startup he knows; they exert more control than VCs and YC exerts less (*startupfunding*).
- **Pre-YC VC convention: vs. standard terms, founder-CEO replacement and anti-founder-liquidity rules.** "Standard, schmandard" (*startupfunding*); the VC rule against buying founder stock is "a stupid one" (paraphrase, *startupfunding*); and YC's whole thesis (more, smaller investments; hackers over suits; younger founders; standardized terms) was the counter-position (*ycstart*).
- **Meaning of a life examined: vs. Socrates and the Greeks on purpose.** Gently: "the unexamined life is not worth living" parodied as the kind of conversations freshmen have late at night; PG's answer is "See randomness." (*randomness*.)
- **Speech and orthodoxy: vs. those who say wokeness "is simply treating people with respect," and those who deny cancel culture is a problem.** The rules are too many and arbitrary for the first claim (*woke*, endnote 4), and the second is explained as orthodox privilege, not bad faith (*orth*). He cites Peter Boghossian and James Lindsay approvingly on privilege as original sin (endnote 15), and he credits Brian Armstrong, the University of Chicago, MIT and Elon Musk for rolling parts of it back while declining to call Trump's 2024 win a referendum on wokeness.
- **Customer-value philosophy: vs. hard-headed margin maximizers.** "Many hard-headed business types would write this off as idealistic hippy stuff, but in fact this is the route to becoming really rich." (*powerful*.) He agrees with Tim O'Reilly ("create more value than you capture").

## Blind spots
- **Survivorship bias (founder mode).** Critics note *Founder Mode* generalizes from a handful of winners (Jobs, Chesky) while ignoring the graveyard of hyper-involved founders who failed, and the manager-led successes. Matthew Parker ("Flounder Mode") argues it is "actively harmful advice when universally applied" and can push early founders into "flounder mode." (stretchfour.substack.com; Fortune.)
- **The black-swan framing is itself survivorship bias by design.** *Black Swan Farming* and *How to Earn a Billion Dollars* present "the best ideas look bad" as a license to chase ideas the market rejects, but for every Facebook that "sounded lame" and worked, thousands sounded lame and *were* lame. The thesis is unfalsifiable ex ante and can rationalize ignoring genuine negative signal. PG half-concedes the limit himself: "I can lay out what I know to be the right thing to do, and still not do it." (*swan*.)
- **"Liar" framing devalues operators.** Molly Graham (senior operator at Facebook/Google) "violently agree[s]" with the founder-excellence core but warns the essay "feels like it minimizes the work of every single other person inside a company other than the founder," and that "companies are as likely to be damaged or destroyed by the behavior of founders as they are to be made successful by them." (mollyg.substack.com.) PG himself flags the sweeping move, "C-level execs, as a class, include some of the most skillful liars in the world" (*foundermode*), as a class generalization.
- **Founder-worship / elitism.** Dave Karpf's "Paul Graham and the Cult of the Founder" argues PG has "been bad for Silicon Valley" by elevating founders as uniquely special and devaluing the engineers who build, and reads *Founder Mode* as "an ode to micromanagement dressed up as visionary thinking." (davekarpf.substack.com.)
- **Gender and access gaps.** Critics note women founders report they are not granted the same latitude to run companies in "founder mode" that men are; PG's framing treats founder authority as universally available when it isn't. (Founder-mode discourse; Fortune.) PG's own corpus contains a sharp instance: "I would be reluctant to start a startup with a woman who had small children, or was likely to have them soon." (*start*, footnote), presented as a freedom-to-discriminate advantage with no examination of the bias.
- **The billion-dollar math hides the denominator.** *How to Earn a Billion Dollars* makes the arithmetic look almost automatic, yet ~30 of YC's ~6,500 founders became billionaires, the rarest of outcomes presented as a procedure that abstracts away luck, market timing, and the thousands who made customers happy and still failed. PG's own model concedes luck: "the outcome is the product of skill, determination, and luck ... if you roll a zero for luck, the outcome is zero." (*really*.)
- **Self-acknowledged unreliability and slant.** On parenthood: "to some extent you can't trust me when I say having kids is great ... I'm like a religious cultist." (*kids*.) On startup advocacy: "if I've misled people here, I'm not eager to fix that. I'd rather have everyone think starting a startup is grim and hard." (*really*), a deliberate slant. On founder mode: "So is it a good idea, or a bad one? We still don't know. That's how little we know about founder mode." (*foundermode*.) On teachability: "I'm pathologically optimistic about people's ability to change." (*relres*.)
- **The inequality argument assumes redistribution must work via blunt confiscation,** sidestepping predistribution/structural policy that doesn't cap upside (*inequality*); and it is argued from inside the wealth, "The great concentrations of wealth I see around me in Silicon Valley don't seem to be destroying democracy." (*ineq*.) It also reduces a serious distributive-justice position to a psychological error: "the unmistakable spectral signature of the Daddy Model." (*gap*.)
- **Sweeping disciplinary and ideological generalizations.** "You can skip the social sciences, philosophy, and the various departments created recently in response to political pressures" (*college*); "this is why so many of the best programmers are libertarians" (*wealth*); "of all the great programmers I can think of, I know of only one who would voluntarily program in Java" (*gh*), strong claims resting on his personal acquaintance set. He sometimes flags it: "I only know people who work in certain fields: startup founders, programmers, professors." (*mean*.)
- **Narrow sample of the world.** His heuristics ("growth rate as compass," "default alive," "live in the future," "cities send messages") are calibrated to venture-scale software and elite coastal hubs, and map poorly onto bootstrapped, slow-growth, capital-intensive, or non-tech businesses, though they're often cited as universal. He scopes it explicitly in places ("In this essay I'm talking mainly about software startups. These points don't apply to ... energy or biotech," *fr*) but the scope is easily lost in citation.
- **"Keep your identity small" can read as privilege.** The advice presumes you can choose which group identities to "let in," which is easier for those whose identity isn't already politicized for them by others, a limitation the essay doesn't address.
- **Elitism in the writing/ideas essays.** *Writes and Write-Nots* and *The Shape of the Essay Field* frame coming intellectual divides that critics read as flattering to an in-group and as treating AI-assisted writing as straightforwardly corrosive rather than potentially leveling, an empirical bet, not an established fact.
- **A second Karpf critique: the "proto-techbro" reading.** In 2024 Dave Karpf argues *Why Nerds are Unpopular* has dated because PG's own influence reshaped the culture it describes: "The tech culture that Graham helped to seed is also a culture that doesn't particularly value smart anymore," and "YCombinator, in other words, is the ultimate popularity contest." (davekarpf.substack.com, *protot*.) Treat it as a critic's reading: Karpf concedes the post-2008 finance-migration date is "a hunch," offers no admissions data, and the claim conflicts with PG's own stated selection criteria.
- **A practitioner's rebuttal of founder mode: org design is a CEO's decision and structure is the variable.** Asad Zaman worries "PG has done more harm than good" with *Founder Mode*, points to Microsoft's divisional structure and to Meta's founder-plus-operator pair, and says founders "complaining that they were given the 'same advice' and it didn't work for them are abdicating responsibility." (*pavilion*.) Weigh it carefully: he is a non-founder CEO writing within days of the essay, and his counterexamples (Microsoft, Meta, ServiceNow, Snowflake) are anecdotal in the same way the essay is; his divisional-vs-functional reading of what Chesky changed is his own diagnosis stated as fact.
- **Early-work advice is survivorship-prone.** Advice to be slightly overconfident and to exaggerate the importance of your goal is advice we hear from the people who won (inference); PG himself admits "Maybe Silicon Valley is too optimistic. Maybe it's too easily fooled by impostors." and that those who dismiss new startup ideas "are in fact more likely to be right. Just not when their predictions are weighted by outcome." (*early*.) His Theranos defense (Silicon Valley investors weren't fooled; journalists were) rests on the narrow test of the cap table.
- **The YC origin story is a retrospective told by the winner.** He says the key innovation (funding synchronously) was not understood at the start, and lists luck three times (*ycstart*); analysts would add the pre-existing trio of cofounders, $100k ready to invest, a rent-stabilized apartment, and a viral hobby site (inference from *ycstart*, *worked*). His line "ignorance worked in our favor" is only representative if ignorance is rarely fatal. He also notes his lessons rested on thin data: "I only had a few data points: our own startup, and those started by friends." (*users*.)
- **The Reddit pivot is a survivor's tale.** PG concedes early YC selection was noisy ("we thought then that we were funding ideas rather than founders") and that the redirect worked; there is no denominator of redirected teams that failed. The essay is also personal about the friend's company ("a martyr for what authority later did to him," on Aaron Swartz), so claims about Reddit's management years are PG's view. (*reddits*.)
- **Funding advice is 2005 vintage and best-case.** SEC accredited-investor thresholds, 2%/20% fund economics and the "$15,000 seed" cap-table numbers are period-specific, and he flags that "this is the Bambi version" and made everyone nicer than reality (*startupfunding*). His categorical "Consulting is where product companies go to die" is only hedged by "you have to be very disciplined," and the same essay presumes venture-scale software. His suspicion that lower-tier VCs are responsible for most tricks is stated as a suspicion, not data. (*startupfunding*.)
- **A hard-to-talk-to founder test rests on his own impression.** He admits "I could never quite tell if they understood what I was saying" (*word*), so the glazed-look signal can also reflect language, culture or status asymmetry with a famous investor (inference), and the test is open to being misread as deference.
- **Noob-ness reasoning is an unfalsifiable reframe.** The inversion ("the feeling of being a noob is inversely correlated with actual ignorance") rests on one analogy and an evolutionary story, and it can license under-preparation; it sits uneasily beside "be an expert on your users" (inference; *noob*).
- **"See randomness" is a prior, not a rule.** In adversarial settings (bad-faith counterparties, competitors, investors) malice is sometimes the better theory; the dossier's own *ronco* and *mean* arguments assume good faith (inference; *randomness*). The anthropocentrism essay is also short and reductionist ("we're just the latest model vehicle our genes have constructed") without engaging objections.
- **The spam predictions did not fully hold.** He predicted that "it will stop working, and the spammers will actually stop sending it"; spammers adapted and volume did not die out (inference from history), though per-user Bayesian filtering became standard. His numbers were "filtering my mail based on a corpus of my mail," from a single programmer's inbox, and he wrote "I don't think this number can be trusted, partly because the sample is so small." (*spam*, *better*.)
- **Technology forecasts dated.** In 2009 he called Android "an orphan" and floated breaking Apple's lock through developers' hands (*apple*); in 2003 he guessed Java was an evolutionary dead end, "just a guess" (*hundred*); *Being Popular* argues a hacker-friendly language wins, and the later Arc did not become popular (inference). He half-undercuts his own boycott logic: the iMac is "so beautiful that you can't make yourself care." (*apple*.) The popularity essay also contains off-color generalizations ("Hackers share the surgeon's secret pleasure in poking about in gross innards") that weaken its tone. (*popular*.)
- **The wokeness essay is monocausal and its rule is hard to operationalize.** It explains a movement mostly by a temperament ("Prigs are prigs by nature"), rests its one hard number on a single forum he ran (users "about three times more likely to upvote something if it outraged them"), and offers no way to count whether the number of true things we can't say is rising (analyst critique; *woke*). Its endnote that removing aggressively conventional-minded people from an organization "would be an excellent idea" sits in tension with its own pluralist presumption (analyst critique). He scopes it himself: the generalization comes from universities, where it is "most extreme," and he does not claim 2024 was a referendum on wokeness.
- **"Orthodox privilege" is closed by construction.** Anyone who denies it is read as evidence for it, and the argument shows that some unsayable things are true, not that any particular unsayable claim is; it hands every heretic a ready defense (analyst critique; *orth*). Reviewers were an in-network list (Sam Altman, Patrick Collison, Antonio Garcia-Martinez, Harj Taggar, others), which fits the narrow-sample blind spot.
- **"Why YC" is self-serving framing.** Calling it an "amoral" hack shifts attention from who gets funded; the "open source" economy metaphor clashes with gatekeeping at a selective seed program (inference). It was written in 2006 before returns data and should not be read as YC's later mission. (*whyyc*.)
- **Origins of the HN lessons rest on a contested analogy.** Broken windows theory is a policy claim with disputed evidence, and PG treats Giuliani's New York as "miraculous" from personal observation (inference; *hackernews*). He also doubts his own community: "I wish I could be 100% sure that's not a description of HN" (the time-sink worry), and concedes the orange-username feature was a mistake. His own conclusion is that running HN "was a mistake." (*hackernews*, *worked*.)
- **Self-reported business misjudgments at Viaweb.** "I mistakenly thought the thing that mattered was the absolute number of users," which led to over-hiring and being "at the mercy of investors for the entire life of the company," the same error he now warns against; the first idea (art galleries) was among the worst; and he admits retreating twice from hard problems when they required a big company ("I realize that sounds rather wimpy"). (*worked*, *radio*.)
- **Judgments about trust and selection.** "trustworthy people cannot judge other trustworthy people" (auto-captions), and PG says he is not good at judging trustworthiness and delegates it to Jessica Livingston (paraphrase, *ralston*); he also worries the be-nice screen might have filtered out a young Steve Jobs (paraphrase, *ralston*). He could not have done a startup as a sole founder, which limits his advice to the many who do (paraphrase).
- **Founder control vs. succession.** PG engineered YC so it "couldn't be controlled by the founders," while *Founder Mode* argues founders should stay in the details; the likely difference (organizational longevity vs. company-building) is never spelled out (analyst inference; *worked*, *foundermode*). "Laziness" praised as hatred of gratuitous schlep sits beside *schlep* blindness; the reconciliation (hate gratuitous schlep, not necessary schlep) is the dossier's inference, not PG's line.
- **The "power" lens is built from software margins and YC-batch cases.** He grants "These hypothetical transformations of the original idea don't always yield anything promising. Far from it." and the scope limit that hospitals and school districts resist going full stack (*powerful*). The inference: "sell to the youngest customers" depends on the customer base itself growing fast, and the constraint that every power move must improve the customer's life is asserted, not argued; monopoly-style power can coexist with customer benefit in the short run (analyst critique).
- **Caption-derived claims are fragile.** The 2018 conversation is known only through YouTube auto-captions (e.g., the exact competitor-kill statistic and the Jobs sentence are garbled), so those lines are well-attested rather than verified verbatim. (*ralston*.)
- **No test for a disliked builder-CEO.** *Charisma / Power* concedes there is no solution and offers no way to distinguish a disliked-and-right CEO from a disliked-and-wrong one; the Clinton example is political and unargued. (*pow*.)
- **Name advice rests on correlation.** The 100%/94%/66% figures are survivorship-prone (companies that win can afford the .com), the advice is US-specific, and the 2015 essay predates the .io/.ai domain norm (inference; *name*).
