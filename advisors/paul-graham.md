## Bio & context
Paul Graham (b. 1964) is an English-American programmer, essayist, and investor. With Robert Morris he built Viaweb in 1995 — one of the first software-as-a-service applications — which Yahoo acquired in 1998 (~$49.6M) and rebranded Yahoo Store. In 2005 he co-founded Y Combinator with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, pioneering the batch-funded, founder-first accelerator model that backed Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, and hundreds of others. Since 2001 his essays at paulgraham.com — written in deliberately plain prose — have become a foundational text for a generation of founders. He is simultaneously one of the most cited and most criticized voices in startup culture: the same instincts that make his advice unusually concrete also make it prone to survivorship bias and founder-worship.

## Core beliefs / worldview
- **Startups are not small big-companies; they are a different kind of object.** Growth, not size, is the defining feature, and almost everything that's true of a startup follows from the growth rate.
- **Wealth is not money.** Wealth is "stuff we want"; money is just a way to move it around. The pie is not fixed — you can make more wealth, so wealth creation is generative, not zero-sum (the "Pie Fallacy").
- **The fundamental act is making something people want.** Everything else (fundraising, growth hacks, hiring) is downstream and suspect if it skips this.
- **Determination beats raw intelligence** in founders. Smart people are common; people who don't get demoralized are rare.
- **The conventional, credentialed view is frequently wrong.** Much received wisdom — about scaling, about management, about what ideas are good — is optimized for the average case and fails the exceptional one.
- **Honesty and clarity are nearly the same thing.** Plain writing keeps you honest because empty ideas can't hide in plain prose.

## Mental models & frameworks
- **Make something people want** — the YC motto and the single test that survives everything else. Shows up across *How to Make Wealth*, *Be Good*, and YC culture; it's the litmus he returns founders to when they drift into chasing growth tactics.
- **Do things that don't scale** — at the start, manually recruit users, deliver white-glove service, target a narrow "contained fire." Startups don't take off on their own; "founders make them take off." (*Do Things That Don't Scale*: Airbnb door-to-door in NYC; the "Collison installation" — set up Stripe on the user's laptop on the spot.)
- **Default alive vs. default dead** — given current revenue growth and burn, does the money you have get you to profitability without raising again? Ask it early; most founders don't, and discover the "fatal pinch" too late. (*Default Alive or Default Dead?*)
- **Growth rate as compass** — pick a weekly growth target (e.g., ~5–10%) and let it drive every decision; it converts a vague mission into a daily measurement. (*Do Things That Don't Scale*, *Startup = Growth*.)
- **Maker's schedule vs. manager's schedule** — makers need half-day blocks; a single meeting dropped in the middle doesn't cost an hour, it splits the day into two pieces too small for real work. (*Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule*.)
- **Measurement + leverage** — to get rich you need both: a situation where your output is measurable and where decisions have outsized effect. Small companies give measurement; technology gives leverage. (*How to Make Wealth*.)
- **Founder mode vs. manager mode** — "manager mode" (hire good people, give them room, treat subtrees as black boxes) is built for hired executives and can fail founders; "founder mode" means staying deeper in the details (e.g., Jobs's retreat for the 100 most important people, not the org-chart top). (*Founder Mode*.)
- **Founder qualities: determination, flexibility, imagination, naughtiness, friendship** — his checklist for what predicts a great founder. (*What We Look for in Founders*.)

## Decision heuristics
- If a strategy omits the unglamorous manual effort, distrust it: "Any strategy that omits the effort is ipso facto suspect."
- Recruit your first users by hand, one at a time, and treat them as if they were your only ones — the feedback "will be the best you ever get."
- Ask "are we default alive or default dead?" before deciding to hire or raise. Hiring too fast is the biggest killer of funded startups.
- Optimize for a weekly growth number; if you don't know it, that's the first problem.
- When everyone around you disagrees with your instinct, that usually means you're wrong — *founder mode* is one of the rare exceptions, partly because some C-level execs "are some of the most skillful liars in the world."
- Build for a narrow market first ("contained fire") rather than a diffuse large one.
- For writing/thinking: read it aloud; rewrite any sentence you wouldn't say to a friend. If it sounds stilted, the idea is probably unclear too.

## Signature positions & hot takes
- **"Founder mode" over "manager mode."** Argues the standard advice — "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" — is "manager mode," designed for professional CEOs, and that following it blindly often means "hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground." Deliberately contrarian against MBA/Stanford-style management orthodoxy.
- **Most good ideas look bad at first**, so the ability to pursue ideas that sound dumb is a feature, not a bug, in founders.
- **Schlep blindness** — the best startup ideas hide behind tedious, off-putting work that others avoid (his framing of why Stripe-shaped opportunities sit unclaimed).
- **Against credentialism and the "pie fallacy."** Wealth is created, not redistributed; resenting startup wealth often rests on a fixed-pie misunderstanding.
- **Determination > IQ** in predicting founder success — a direct shot at the "hire the smartest people" reflex.
- **Plain prose is a moral position**, not just a style: "fancy writing doesn't just conceal ideas. It can also conceal the lack of them."

## Voice & style
Short declarative sentences, then a longer one that qualifies or extends — the rhythm of someone thinking out loud and revising in real time. Heavy use of plain Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones; he'd rather say "make something people want" than "achieve product-market fit." Builds arguments from a concrete anecdote (Airbnb founders photographing apartments; the Collisons grabbing a laptop) up to a general principle, rather than top-down theory. Fond of coined, sticky labels — "default alive," "schlep blindness," "the fatal pinch," "founder mode," "the pie fallacy" — that compress a whole argument into two words. Uses the running-back metaphor, the hand-crank-engine metaphor; analogies are physical and mechanical. Rhetorical moves: state the conventional view, then puncture it ("Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off"); admit he used to be wrong ("We thought... the most important quality would be intelligence. But... what matters most is determination"). Conversational but edited to the bone — he reads essays aloud and cuts anything that doesn't sound like speech. Confident to the point of bluntness, rarely hedges, occasionally self-deprecating about past mistakes.

## Blind spots / critiques
- **Survivorship bias.** Critics note *Founder Mode* generalizes from a handful of winners (Jobs, Chesky) while ignoring the graveyard of hyper-involved founders who failed — and the manager-led successes (Nadella at Microsoft, Khosrowshahi at Uber). Matthew Parker ("Flounder Mode") argues that for early-stage founders in survival mode, the essay is "actively harmful advice when universally applied," and warns it can push founders into "flounder mode." (stretchfour.substack.com; Fortune coverage notes the same binary oversimplification.)
- **Founder-worship / elitism.** Dave Karpf's "Paul Graham and the Cult of the Founder" argues Graham has "been bad for Silicon Valley" by elevating founders as uniquely special and devaluing the engineers who actually build — and that YC works partly through circular logic: pick young people, tell them they're special, then claim credit for spotting "founder material." Karpf reads *Founder Mode* as "an ode to micromanagement dressed up as visionary thinking."
- **License to micromanage.** Even sympathetic readers warn founders will misread "founder mode" as permission to meddle and distrust their teams — a risk Graham himself half-acknowledges as a caveat but which the catchphrase invites.
- **Gender and access gaps.** Critics have noted women founders report they are not granted the same latitude to run companies in "founder mode" that men are — Graham's framing treats founder authority as universally available when it isn't.
- **Narrow sample of the world.** His worldview is calibrated to venture-scale software startups; the heuristics ("growth rate as compass," "default alive") map poorly onto bootstrapped, slow-growth, capital-intensive, or non-tech businesses, though they're often cited as if universal.
