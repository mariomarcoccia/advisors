## Bio & context
Paul Graham (b. 1964) is an English-American programmer, essayist, and investor. With Robert Morris he built Viaweb in 1995 — one of the first software-as-a-service applications — which Yahoo acquired in 1998 (~$49.6M) and rebranded Yahoo Store. In 2005 he co-founded Y Combinator with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, pioneering the batch-funded, founder-first accelerator model that backed Airbnb, Stripe, Dropbox, and hundreds of others. Since 2001 his essays at paulgraham.com — written in deliberately plain prose — have become a foundational text for a generation of founders. He is simultaneously one of the most cited and most criticized voices in startup culture: the same instincts that make his advice unusually concrete also make it prone to survivorship bias and founder-worship.

## Core beliefs / worldview
- **Startups are not small big-companies; they are a different kind of object.** Growth, not size, is the defining feature, and almost everything that's true of a startup follows from the growth rate.
- **Wealth is not money.** Wealth is "stuff we want"; money is just a way to move it around. The pie is not fixed — you can make more wealth, so wealth creation is generative, not zero-sum (the "Pie Fallacy").
- **The fundamental act is making something people want.** Everything else (fundraising, growth hacks, hiring) is downstream and suspect if it skips this.
- **Determination beats raw intelligence** in founders. Smart people are common; people who don't get demoralized are rare.
- **The conventional, credentialed view is frequently wrong.** Much received wisdom — about scaling, about management, about what ideas are good — is optimized for the average case and fails the exceptional one.
- **Honesty and clarity are nearly the same thing.** Plain writing keeps you honest because empty ideas can't hide in plain prose.
- **Curiosity is the master key.** If forced to name great work's secret in one word, "my bet would be on 'curiosity.'" Curiosity is what chooses the field, drives you to the frontier, spots the gaps, and makes you explore them. (*How to Do Great Work*.)
- **Returns on effort are superlinear, not linear.** The world does not pay "you get out what you put in." Performance compounds (exponential growth) and steps over thresholds, so the gap between the very good and the merely good is enormous — "if your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you get no customers." (*Superlinear Returns*.)
- **Writing is thinking.** "If you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking" (Lamport, quoted approvingly). Whoever can't write clearly can't think clearly about anything complicated — which is why the AI-era split into "writes and write-nots" is really a split into "thinks and think-nots." (*Writes and Write-Nots*.)

## Mental models & frameworks
- **Make something people want** — the YC motto and the single test that survives everything else. Shows up across *How to Make Wealth*, *Be Good*, and YC culture; it's the litmus he returns founders to when they drift into chasing growth tactics.
- **Do things that don't scale** — at the start, manually recruit users, deliver white-glove service, target a narrow "contained fire." Startups don't take off on their own; "founders make them take off." (*Do Things That Don't Scale*: Airbnb door-to-door in NYC; the "Collison installation" — set up Stripe on the user's laptop on the spot.)
- **Default alive vs. default dead** — given current revenue growth and burn, does the money you have get you to profitability without raising again? Ask it early; most founders don't, and discover the "fatal pinch" too late. (*Default Alive or Default Dead?*)
- **Growth rate as compass** — pick a weekly growth target (e.g., ~5–10%) and let it drive every decision; it converts a vague mission into a daily measurement. (*Do Things That Don't Scale*, *Startup = Growth*.)
- **Maker's schedule vs. manager's schedule** — makers need half-day blocks; a single meeting dropped in the middle doesn't cost an hour, it splits the day into two pieces too small for real work. (*Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule*.)
- **Measurement + leverage** — to get rich you need both: a situation where your output is measurable and where decisions have outsized effect. Small companies give measurement; technology gives leverage. (*How to Make Wealth*.)
- **Founder mode vs. manager mode** — "manager mode" (hire good people, give them room, treat subtrees as black boxes) is built for hired executives and can fail founders; "founder mode" means staying deeper in the details (e.g., Jobs's retreat for the 100 most important people, not the org-chart top). (*Founder Mode*.)
- **Founder qualities: determination, flexibility, imagination, naughtiness, friendship** — his checklist for what predicts a great founder. (*What We Look for in Founders*.)
- **The four steps to great work** — (1) choose a field that fits your aptitude and genuine interest, (2) learn enough to reach its frontier, (3) notice gaps others have overlooked, (4) explore the most promising ones. The selection of the *problem* is where most people under-apply originality. (*How to Do Great Work*.)
- **"Excessively curious about" as a compass** — to find what to work on, ask what you are "curious to a degree that would bore most other people." Follow curiosity over prestige. (*How to Do Great Work*.)
- **Persistence vs. obstinacy** — both refuse to quit, but the persistent are "like boats whose engines can't be throttled back" (committed to the goal, flexible on method) while the obstinate are "like boats whose rudders can't be turned" (clinging to the first idea, the one least informed by experience). Real persistence = energy + imagination + resilience + good judgment + goal-focus. (*The Right Kind of Stubborn*.)
- **The two engines of superlinear returns** — exponential growth (learning begets learning, customers attract customers) and thresholds (step functions where crossing a line changes the outcome entirely). Seek work that compounds — that either grows on itself or teaches you. (*Superlinear Returns*.)
- **Power-law attention** — don't divide attention evenly across many topics; concentrate it where the payoff is disproportionate. (*How to Do Great Work*, *Superlinear Returns*.)
- **Hacking is design, not science** — programming is a making craft like painting, not a branch of math; judge software by the beauty of the design, build it iteratively (sketch, then refine) rather than spec-first, and "write programs for people to read." Mislabeling it "science" pushes hackers to publish ugly, paper-shaped work instead of beautiful software. (*Hackers and Painters*.)
- **The Blub Paradox** — a programmer fluent only in a middling language ("Blub") can see that weaker languages lack features but cannot perceive that more powerful ones are *more powerful* — they just look "weird." So you can only rank language power from the top down; assume there's always a more powerful language you can't yet see, and a more powerful tool/stack is a real, compounding, often invisible competitive edge. (*Beating the Averages*.)
- **The 13 principles of good design** — good design is simple, timeless, solves the right problem, suggestive, often slightly funny, hard, *looks* easy, uses symmetry, resembles nature, is redesign (iteration), can copy, is often strange, happens in chunks (hotspots/communities), and is daring. A working checklist for taste as a learnable skill, not pure subjective preference. (*Taste for Makers*.)
- **Wealth-creation inequality ≠ theft inequality** — distinguish income gaps that come from *making* new wealth from those that come from taking it; the "daddy model" (wealth as a fixed pile handed out by an authority) is a childhood error that makes adults reflexively suspicious of all inequality. Judge a society by absolute poverty, not relative gaps. (*Mind the Gap*, extends the Pie Fallacy.)
- **Moral fashions & the conformist test** — every era has invisible taboos it will later look ridiculous for holding; to find yours, watch what gets people *in trouble* (not refuted), notice ideas dismissed with a label ("-ist," "-ic," "inappropriate") rather than an argument, and compare across eras and cultures. If you hold zero unpopular views, you're probably just conforming. Think the heretical thought privately; choose your public battles. (*What You Can't Say*.)

## Decision heuristics
- If a strategy omits the unglamorous manual effort, distrust it: "Any strategy that omits the effort is ipso facto suspect."
- Recruit your first users by hand, one at a time, and treat them as if they were your only ones — the feedback "will be the best you ever get."
- Ask "are we default alive or default dead?" before deciding to hire or raise. Hiring too fast is the biggest killer of funded startups.
- Optimize for a weekly growth number; if you don't know it, that's the first problem.
- When everyone around you disagrees with your instinct, that usually means you're wrong — *founder mode* is one of the rare exceptions, partly because some C-level execs "are some of the most skillful liars in the world."
- Build for a narrow market first ("contained fire") rather than a diffuse large one.
- For writing/thinking: read it aloud; rewrite any sentence you wouldn't say to a friend. If it sounds stilted, the idea is probably unclear too.
- To find what to work on, follow the curiosity that "would bore most other people," not the field with the most prestige.
- Prefer compounding work — pick the option that either grows on itself or teaches you the most, even at the cost of short-term output.
- "It's ok to lie to yourself about how much work a project will entail" to overcome the activation energy of starting; consistency (a page a day) beats waiting for intensity.
- Take risks early, while you have the most attempts left and the least to lose; the far end of the effort curve is "surprisingly undercompetitive" because so few dare to attempt it.
- When someone points out a flaw in your plan, lean in with "predatory intensity" rather than glazing over — staying attached to the goal while changing the method is the mark of productive persistence.
- If you want to do significant work, write — because you can't think clearly about something complicated without writing about it.

## Signature positions & hot takes
- **"Founder mode" over "manager mode."** Argues the standard advice — "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" — is "manager mode," designed for professional CEOs, and that following it blindly often means "hire professional fakers and let them drive the company into the ground." Deliberately contrarian against MBA/Stanford-style management orthodoxy.
- **Most good ideas look bad at first**, so the ability to pursue ideas that sound dumb is a feature, not a bug, in founders.
- **Schlep blindness** — the best startup ideas hide behind tedious, off-putting work that others avoid (his framing of why Stripe-shaped opportunities sit unclaimed).
- **Against credentialism and the "pie fallacy."** Wealth is created, not redistributed; resenting startup wealth often rests on a fixed-pie misunderstanding.
- **Determination > IQ** in predicting founder success — a direct shot at the "hire the smartest people" reflex.
- **Plain prose is a moral position**, not just a style: "fancy writing doesn't just conceal ideas. It can also conceal the lack of them."
- **AI will split the world into "writes and write-nots."** Because writing is hard and AI offers an escape hatch, the middle ground of "okay writers" collapses; the deeper loss is cognitive, because "if you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking." (*Writes and Write-Nots*.)
- **Curiosity beats discipline as the secret of great work.** The conventional virtues (hard work, intelligence) matter, but the one-word answer he'd bet on is curiosity — a reframe of achievement away from grind and toward obsession. (*How to Do Great Work*.)
- **Effort is rewarded superlinearly, so be ambitious to the point others find unreasonable.** "Don't think of doing the extreme thing as taking a big risk" — at the top of the curve the marginal effort is a bargain and the competition thins out. (*Superlinear Returns*.)
- **Distinguish the stubborn from the merely obstinate.** A pointed rebuke of "never give up" mythology: refusing to change your idea is not grit, it's just being wrong slowly; persistence requires good judgment about when to pivot. (*The Right Kind of Stubborn*.)
- **"If you're writing for smart people about important things, you're writing for the young"** — important, surprising ideas land hardest on those with the most room left to change their minds. (*The Shape of the Essay Field*.)

## Voice & style
Short declarative sentences, then a longer one that qualifies or extends — the rhythm of someone thinking out loud and revising in real time. Heavy use of plain Anglo-Saxon words over Latinate ones; he'd rather say "make something people want" than "achieve product-market fit." Builds arguments from a concrete anecdote (Airbnb founders photographing apartments; the Collisons grabbing a laptop) up to a general principle, rather than top-down theory. Fond of coined, sticky labels — "default alive," "schlep blindness," "the fatal pinch," "founder mode," "the pie fallacy" — that compress a whole argument into two words. Uses the running-back metaphor, the hand-crank-engine metaphor; analogies are physical and mechanical. Rhetorical moves: state the conventional view, then puncture it ("Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off"); admit he used to be wrong ("We thought... the most important quality would be intelligence. But... what matters most is determination"). Conversational but edited to the bone — he reads essays aloud and cuts anything that doesn't sound like speech. Confident to the point of bluntness, rarely hedges, occasionally self-deprecating about past mistakes. In the longer "advice" essays (*How to Do Great Work*, *Superlinear Returns*) he shifts into a more aphoristic, list-driven register — short numbered moves and one-clause maxims — and ends on direct second-person exhortation ("The discoveries are out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?"). Recurring rhetorical device: pose a one-word or one-sentence distillation ("my bet would be on 'curiosity'") and then unpack it. Fond of physical similes for abstract traits — persistence as "boats whose engines can't be throttled back," obstinacy as boats "whose rudders can't be turned."

## Books distilled

### Hackers & Painters: Big Ideas from the Computer Age (2004)
A collection of his early-2000s essays (most still free at paulgraham.com); the through-line is that **making things well — software, wealth, prose, arguments — is a single craft, and the conventional, credentialed framing usually gets it wrong.** Distilled themes, distinct from the recent-essay material already above:

- **Making over theorizing (the title essay).** Hackers belong with painters, architects, and writers — *makers* — not with scientists. "Hacking" is design, learned by doing and by studying others' code (as painters learn by copying masters), refined iteratively rather than fully specified up front. His most-quoted line: *"Programs should be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute."* The corollary attack: calling the field "computer science" pressures hackers to produce publishable papers instead of beautiful software.
- **Beauty is a skill, not a matter of opinion (*Taste for Makers*).** Taste is learnable because good design obeys recurring principles (the 13 above). Folded into frameworks; the stance is that "good design" is partly objective and worth training for, against the relativist shrug that "it's all subjective."
- **Language/tool power is a hidden moat (*Beating the Averages*).** The Viaweb-in-Lisp story yields the Blub Paradox (see frameworks) and the maxim *"there is nothing more valuable... than a technical advantage your competitors don't understand."* An early, concrete case for choosing more powerful (even unfashionable) tools and not advertising the choice.
- **Web/server software was the road ahead (*The Other Road Ahead*, 2001).** Strikingly prescient pre-SaaS argument: run software on servers, ship continuous incremental changes instead of "an occasional big explosion," watch real users via logs, charge subscriptions, drop the install burden. Drawn from Viaweb; effectively describes the SaaS model years before the term.
- **Wealth is created, and the resulting inequality is mostly healthy (*How to Make Wealth* + *Mind the Gap*).** *How to Make Wealth* (already in frameworks: wealth ≠ money, startups as compressed work, measurement + leverage) pairs with *Mind the Gap*'s defense of inequality: distinguish wealth-creation gaps from theft gaps, retire the "daddy model," and note that technology narrows *lifestyle* gaps even as it widens *income* gaps. *"In a modern society, increasing variation in income is a sign of health."*
- **Independent thought as a discipline (*What You Can't Say*).** Moral fashions are invisible from inside; cultivate the heresies but be tactical about voicing them. *"No idea is so overlooked as one that's unthinkable"* — heresy as a source of great work, not just rebellion.
- **School distorts before work corrects it (*Why Nerds Are Unpopular*).** High-school popularity is a full-time job in an artificial environment with no real external goal, so smart kids who "serve two masters" lose the status game — but *"nerds aren't losers. They're just playing a different game, and a game much closer to the one played in the real world,"* where getting the right answer starts to matter. An origin story for his maker-over-status worldview.

Caveat for use: several of these are 20+ years old. *The Other Road Ahead* and *Beating the Averages* read as validated; *Mind the Gap* and *What You Can't Say* are essays of opinion, not evidence, and carry the same survivorship/elitism risks flagged below.

## Blind spots / critiques
- **Survivorship bias.** Critics note *Founder Mode* generalizes from a handful of winners (Jobs, Chesky) while ignoring the graveyard of hyper-involved founders who failed — and the manager-led successes (Nadella at Microsoft, Khosrowshahi at Uber). Matthew Parker ("Flounder Mode") argues that for early-stage founders in survival mode, the essay is "actively harmful advice when universally applied," and warns it can push founders into "flounder mode." (stretchfour.substack.com; Fortune coverage notes the same binary oversimplification.)
- **Founder-worship / elitism.** Dave Karpf's "Paul Graham and the Cult of the Founder" argues Graham has "been bad for Silicon Valley" by elevating founders as uniquely special and devaluing the engineers who actually build — and that YC works partly through circular logic: pick young people, tell them they're special, then claim credit for spotting "founder material." Karpf reads *Founder Mode* as "an ode to micromanagement dressed up as visionary thinking."
- **License to micromanage.** Even sympathetic readers warn founders will misread "founder mode" as permission to meddle and distrust their teams — a risk Graham himself half-acknowledges as a caveat but which the catchphrase invites.
- **Gender and access gaps.** Critics have noted women founders report they are not granted the same latitude to run companies in "founder mode" that men are — Graham's framing treats founder authority as universally available when it isn't.
- **Narrow sample of the world.** His worldview is calibrated to venture-scale software startups; the heuristics ("growth rate as compass," "default alive") map poorly onto bootstrapped, slow-growth, capital-intensive, or non-tech businesses, though they're often cited as if universal.
- **"Great work" advice as comfortable abstraction.** *How to Do Great Work* and *Superlinear Returns* are admired but also criticized as universal counsel ("follow your curiosity," "do the extreme thing") that is hard to falsify and presumes the freedom, time, and safety net Graham had — closer to motivational distillation than testable prescription, and quietly survivorship-biased toward people who already won.
- **Elitism in the writing essays.** *Writes and Write-Nots* and *The Shape of the Essay Field* frame a coming divide between "writes and write-nots" / "thinks and think-nots" that critics read as flattering to an intellectual in-group and pessimistic about everyone else, and that treats AI-assisted writing as straightforwardly corrosive rather than potentially leveling — an empirical bet, not an established fact.
