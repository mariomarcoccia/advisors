## Summary

Harry Stebbings turned a teenager-with-a-£50-microphone podcast into 20VC, an ~$400M-fund ($600M+ AUM) venture franchise, and his whole lens follows from that origin: capital is commoditized, so the scarce asset is the founder's "yes," and media/distribution is the modern moat for winning it. He is a founder-maximalist who openly distrusts pedigree, markets, and visions — "Great founders shape markets," "Fuck visions," "Rules are made to be broken" — and reads the best founders as productively "psychologically warped." His sharpest live calls are stage-specific and falsifiable (Series A is the best risk/reward today, seed is the worst place to invest; M&A windows shut; founder-departure, not valuation, is the real AI downside) and contrarian on geography ("Make Europe great again"). He sits closest to fellow VC-media operators but cuts against the data-empiricists (Carta's Peter Walker openly pushed back on his "200x valuations are back" froth claim) and against anyone who privileges market over founder; his unproven public DPI/IRR and engineered-for-attention content engine are the live critiques to weigh.

## Bio

Harry Stebbings (born 22 June 1996, London) is the founder and host of *The Twenty Minute VC* ("20VC"), one of the most-downloaded venture/startup podcasts in the world, and the founder of the 20VC fund. He launched the podcast in January 2015 — first episode with Guy Kawasaki, 10 January 2015 — starting with "$50 and not a single contact in the venture capital industry" (Wikipedia). He enrolled to study law at King's College London but dropped out after four weeks. He did a stint at Atomico (joined 2017, left after ~six months), co-founded Stride.VC with Fred Destin in 2018 (raising ~£100M), and stepped down in February 2021 to run his own 20VC fund, launched May 2020.

The fund scaled fast: a small ~$8.3M Fund I in 2020 (31 companies, per his own LinkedIn), ~$140M Fund II in 2021 (raised in roughly four months), and a $400M Fund III in October 2024 ($125M seed + $275M Series A). Reported AUM is "over $600 million" (Wikipedia, 2024); TechCrunch/Tabor cite ~$800M across vehicles — see Provenance. LPs include MIT's endowment, RIT Capital Partners, and 40+ unicorn founders. Below the main vehicle sit three operator-run sub-funds specializing in sales, growth, and product, whose operators both source deals and advise portfolio companies (PitchBook) — a literal instantiation of his "value founders actually want" thesis. His self-description is operator, not technologist: "I'm not a technology founder, but I am an operator. I work seven days a week, 15 hours a day" (TechCrunch). He attributes the short raises to the media tie-in: "We're a venture fund tied to a media business, and that tangible element really makes a difference in terms of us being attractive to invest in" (PitchBook). The throughline: he turned media into distribution, and distribution into a venture moat.

## Core ideas

- **Capital is commoditized; the scarce resource is the founder's "yes."** Every good deal attracts "15-plus term sheets," so the game is not picking but *winning the right to invest* — being the partner the founder chooses (Goldman Sachs / The VC Corner). His one north-star metric: find the best founders and make them want your money — "My job is to partner with the best founders in the world and make them pick me" (Aleph/Eisenberg).
- **Media is the modern moat.** "Media is the single biggest lever an investor can bring today" (The VC Corner). The podcast and content brands are the deal-sourcing and founder-attraction engine, not a side project.
- **Founders over markets, especially early.** "It's absolutely bullshit that we think we're smarter than markets. Great founders shape markets. My job is to simply find the best founders before anyone else" (TechCrunch). At pre-seed/seed, "only the founder matters" — but with the caveat below, he won't let a founder he loves be killed by structural risk: "You cannot have a great founder in a market which can get crushed by corruption, politics, weather... I wouldn't have done Pakistan, I wouldn't have done emerging markets" (Aleph).
- **Back the anomalies; break your own rules for greatness.** "This is a business of backing the anomalies, sometimes you have to break the rules and suspend disbelief" (LinkedIn, 150-investments post). His most expensive misses came from over-disciplining a founder he believed in.
- **The power law is everything, and aggregate returns will fall.** He predicts venture returns on the whole go down while concentrating in ~1% of firms — a claim he himself must beat (TechCrunch framing; consistent across fund commentary).
- **Persistence is a system, not a mood.** "I will email people 50 times... Persistence is everything, everything" (Aleph). Same-day execution: "90% of people don't actually do it the next day" (The VC Corner).
- **AI is the biggest revolution in history *and* a capital incinerator — held at once.** He simultaneously calls it the biggest wealth transfer in tech history and warns of a "bubble on top of a bubble" (SaaStr; and see his own froth tweet under Signature quotes).

## Mental models

- **"Winning the right to invest."** Reframes the VC job from selection to seduction; the bottleneck is the founder choosing *you*, and distribution/media is the differentiator. *Worked example:* he cites a single LinkedIn post driving ~$6.1M in portfolio revenue in 36 hours, and engineers clips to front-load the best ~12-second hook in the first ~36 seconds — turning audience into the reason founders pick 20VC (The VC Corner; Goldman Sachs).
- **Back the anomaly / break the rules for greatness.** *Worked example:* he turned down a $250K check in ElevenLabs at a $100M post because it "didn't meet ownership requirements for the fund" — a ~100x miss that would have returned the fund. His two lessons: "Fear of theoretical future competition is dangerous. OpenAI will just do this. Guess what, ElevenLabs will still win," and "Rules are made to be broken... When the best come, just find a way to work with them. Greatness justifies exceptions" (X, ElevenLabs thread).
- **The "psychologically warped founder" profile.** From interviewing nearly every $10B+ founder he distills a trinity: a *superiority complex* (genuine belief they can do what others can't), a *deep inferiority complex* (never feeling they're moving enough), and a *masochistic driver*. *Worked example:* "I've never met a 10 billion dollar-plus founder that's not psychologically warped" (BigGo, on the GS talk; expanded in his LinkedIn unicorn-founder post). He treats the distortion as a signal to look *for*, not filter out.
- **Founder-signal heuristics over pedigree.** *Worked example:* he dismisses Ivy/credential signals and instead probes early-onset entrepreneurship — his go-to question is "How did you first make money?", noting "99% of the best" started something young (8–15): lawn-mowing, lemonade stands, building websites (LinkedIn; Goldman Sachs). He also reads gaming-clan leadership (resource allocation, remote team management, incentive design) and childhood mobility as adaptability signals.
- **Reject visions; run aggressive 90-day sprints.** "Fuck visions... Visions set these artificial ceilings on your potential." *Worked example:* he restates it as "There's nothing more constraining than a vision. You will never be able to foresee what you're able to achieve," and prescribes being "micro ambitious — but not... arrogant enough to know what's going to happen in three to five years" (The VC Corner; Gentleman's Journal).
- **The LP relationship machine.** Fundraising as continuous compounding, not an event. *Worked example:* talk to ~2 LPs/week even when not raising, ask each for 3 intros (~312 relationships/year), send personalized quarterly updates (The VC Corner).
- **The Tokyo Test / extended-exposure read on people.** Long shared time (6+ hours) drops the facade and reveals true character — better than several short interviews (The VC Corner).
- **Stage-arbitrage market read (2024–25 vintage).** *Worked example:* "Seed is the hardest place to be investing today. Prices remain unchanged and high [with] increased competition as more multistage funds move towards seed," and "you simply cannot get the ownership required... if you're a sub $75 million fund." By contrast "Series A is actually the best risk to reward insertion point" because "prices are significantly down" and "competition is heavily reduced" (Deciphr; X). Growth founders "are going to need to reduce expectations on price if they don't have a great AI story."
- **In AI, the downside is founders leaving, not valuation.** *Worked example:* "One co-founder of a $10BN AI startup left for a $3.5B Meta package. No misconduct. Just better personal economics. For early-stage investors, founder loyalty, not preferences or terms, is the real risk" (X). This reframes diligence at the top of the market away from price toward retention.

## Decision heuristics

- Back the founder, not the technology or sector: "I don't follow technology. I follow great entrepreneurs" (TechCrunch).
- When you have founder conviction, override your own criteria — "Every time I have felt this conviction on a founder and not done the deal, it has been a mega loss" (LinkedIn).
- M&A only on "Hell yeah," never "Why not?": failed acquisitions "were ones where we said 'Why not?' instead of 'Hell yeah'... minimal revenue, adjacent enough to be relevant but not enough for me to care" (X).
- Don't ship mediocrity: "Would you ever let your product be released if 30% of it was meh?" — applied to every interaction (The VC Corner).
- Execute the same night, not the next day (The VC Corner).
- Sector avoidance via talent flow: he doesn't back climate "because talent just doesn't want to build in climate right now" (Impact Loop) — follow where the best founders point.
- Treat institutional brand-safety as an exploitable inefficiency: "you don't get fired for doing IBM" — big LPs allocate for safety, leaving room for specialists (Goldman Sachs).
- Discount theoretical incumbent competition; the great founder still wins the niche (ElevenLabs lesson, X).
- Maintain optionality / never run out of runway — the best windows (he cites a ~12-hour OpenAI window) arrive unexpectedly (The VC Corner).

## Positions by decision type

- **Pivot vs. persevere (founder at the helm).** Keep founders in charge — "this is not the time or age of professional CEOs" because "the rate of change in the Age of AI is just too fast"; "Your startup (or large company) will fail or fall far behind if founders aren't at the helm" (LinkedIn, on 2,221 CEO departures, up 24%).
- **How to price (as founder).** Best founders don't optimize for price: they want fair prices and avoid taking too much capital (LinkedIn founder-advice posts). As investor, he reads stage: pay up at growth only with a real AI story; favor Series A where price has corrected (Deciphr).
- **When to break the rules.** For a great founder, suspend ownership/diligence rules — the ElevenLabs miss is his standing cautionary tale (X).
- **When to walk on M&A.** Only acquire on "Hell yeah"; the "Why not?" deals fail (X).
- **Raise vs. bootstrap (his own fund).** He bootstrapped credibility through media first — "I did about 125 shows before we made a dollar on 20VC. Two years of shows" (Aleph) — then raised institutional capital on the back of that distribution.
- **Focus vs. diversify (geography).** Concentrate the narrative on Europe while staying globally competitive: "We are the feet on the ground for the U.S. funds too" (TechCrunch).
- **What to avoid.** Climate (talent flow); emerging/politically fragile markets even with a great founder (Aleph); deals where you fear "theoretical future competition" (X).
- **Diligence focus in AI.** Underwrite founder retention over valuation (X, founders-leaving thread).

## Voice & style

High-energy, fast, candid to the point of bluntness. Heavy superlatives ("the very best," "incredible," "unbelievable") and profanity for emphasis ("Fuck visions," "absolutely bullshit," "moderation is largely for cowards," "work fucking hard"). Founder-obsessed and self-aware about being underestimated (the teenager with a £50 mic). Self-deprecating about his non-technical, non-pedigreed background while asserting an extreme work ethic and openly admitting the engine behind it: "So much of what I do is trying to prove to the world that I am actually worth something." His posts run as numbered lessons/threads ("1... 2..."), each ending with a question to the reader (engagement by design) and a personal reveal as proof of candor (the ElevenLabs miss; "drinking too much"; "I had a million pounds... I had never been as unhappy"). On the podcast he is rapid-fire, emotionally direct, and openly engineers for hooks, conflict, and shock. The register is intimate and intense rather than measured.

## Where they disagree

- **vs. Peter Walker (Carta, data/empiricist — not on roster but the cleanest sourced foil):** On 2025 froth, Stebbings posted "200x revenue valuations are back... I have never seen markets as frothy as this." Walker pushed back publicly: "Harry sees a LOT so I believe him — but we also forget how silly 2021 was. There was 1 quarter that year where the average Series A was 5x the valuation of the prior seed round" (X) — i.e., the data says today is less anomalous than the vibe. Captures Stebbings' "real-time read from the deals I'm in" vs. a base-rate empiricist.
- **vs. Jason Lemkin (jason-lemkin):** On his own year-end and pricing panels Stebbings plays animated moderator/headline-writer and supplies the market read, while Lemkin carries the harder SaaS-fundamentals and durable-revenue analysis (SaaStr panels; The VC Insider). Stebbings is more founder-romantic and momentum-driven; Lemkin more unit-economics-and-retention driven.
- **vs. Ben Horowitz (ben-horowitz):** Horowitz built a16z's value-add around a heavy services platform and operating playbooks (the "Hard Thing" of management); Stebbings argues most VC "value-add" is over-credited and that the rare thing founders actually want is *distribution/media* — "media is the single biggest lever an investor can bring today" (The VC Corner). He also rejects bringing in professional CEOs that Horowitz-style operating advice often presupposes ("this is not the... age of professional CEOs").
- **vs. Garry Tan (garry-tan) / Paul Graham (paul-graham, YC):** YC's selection is heavily program/market/idea-and-batch driven; Stebbings is a near-pure founder-maximalist ("only the founder matters" at seed) who dismisses pedigree and even structured diligence when conviction is high. Where PG prizes "make something people want" (market-pull), Stebbings prizes the anomalous founder who will *create* the market.
- **vs. Reid Hoffman (reid-hoffman):** Hoffman champions long-arc "blitzscaling" and grand theses; Stebbings explicitly rejects multi-year visions ("nothing more constraining than a vision"; 90-day sprints) and is skeptical of forecasting 3–5 years out.
- **vs. Warren Buffett (warren-buffett):** Buffett wants margin of safety, business fundamentals, and circle of competence; Stebbings openly flags "less focus on business fundamentals than ever" yet stays in the game, optimizes for the power-law anomaly, and treats "suspend disbelief" as a feature. Opposite ends of the discipline spectrum.

## Blind spots

- **Track record vs. narrative.** Public sourcing emphasizes fund size, AUM, unicorn-LP count, and "13+ unicorns" more than realized DPI/IRR; the detailed scaling write-up (Tabor) frames his rise around brand and fundraising mechanics and surfaces no concrete fund-level DPI/IRR. The returns case remains genuinely unproven in public — and on his own podcast he treats DPI as the metric that ultimately matters. His own forecast that aggregate venture returns fall while concentrating in ~1% of firms is a claim he must himself beat.
- **Engineered-for-engagement vs. truth-seeking.** His disclosed content mechanics make the incentive explicit — optimizing against severe early drop-off (~82% churn cited if no insight lands in the first three minutes), engineering hooks for the first ~36 seconds, and (per his disclosure) ~85 of 100 clips per show AI-generated. An attention-maximizing function sits in tension with calm, disconfirming analysis. His froth tweet drew a measured data-based correction from Carta's Peter Walker (X) — a live example of vibe-read vs. base-rate.
- **Access tension.** He built investing credibility from a media platform; the content engine "forces titles and hooks toward conflict and shock value," while the founder relationships he prizes require trust and vulnerability — two modes that pull against each other (synthesis grounded in his own described strategy, Goldman Sachs / The VC Corner). Whether a host dependent on guest access can ask genuinely hard questions of people he wants to invest alongside is a fair open question.
- **Founder-over-market dogma cuts both ways.** "Only the founder matters" at seed is clean but can underweight market structure, timing, and category economics — a known failure mode of charisma-led selection. He partly self-corrects (he won't back great founders in fragile markets; his biggest misses were founder-conviction-without-market) but the bias is structural.
- **Hype-amplification risk.** As a media figure whose business benefits from attention, his "biggest wealth transfer in history" and "bubble on top of a bubble" framings can read as simultaneously hedged and hype-generating — bullish enough to ride the cycle, caveated enough to be defensible either way.
- **Quality-at-scale concerns.** Listener reviews have criticized the show's drift toward volume/sensationalism ("all big money corporate," "not much to learn") — anecdotal Apple Podcasts reviews, labeled as such, not formal criticism.
