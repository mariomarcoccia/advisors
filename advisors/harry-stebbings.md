## Summary

Harry Stebbings turned a teenager-with-a-$40-microphone bedroom podcast into 20VC, a ~$400M-fund ($600M+ AUM) venture franchise with 2,800+ episodes and 100M+ downloads, and his whole lens follows from that origin: capital is commoditized, so the scarce asset is the founder's "yes," and media/distribution is the modern moat for winning it. He is a founder-maximalist who openly distrusts pedigree, markets, and visions — "Great founders shape markets," "Fuck visions," "Rules are made to be broken" — and reads the best founders as productively "psychologically warped," screening for parental-relationship rupture, gaming aptitude, and childhood mobility. His sharpest live calls are stage-specific and falsifiable (Series A is the best risk/reward today, seed the worst; M&A windows shut; founder-departure, not valuation, is AI's real downside; time-from-$1M-to-$50M-ARR matters more than time-to-$1M) and contrarian on geography ("Make Europe great again," Project Europe). He is unusually willing to self-incriminate in public — a Fund I top-5 prediction that missed 5-for-5, a $100M+ miss on Suno and Granola to an operator-turned-GP, a normalized admission that he and peers have "stopped caring" about circular AI deals because they inflate his own marks. He sits closest to fellow VC-media operators but cuts against data-empiricists (Carta's Peter Walker on froth; a LinkedIn commenter pressing him on entry-ARR denominators) and against anyone who privileges market, capital-brand, or scale over the founder — Sequoia's David Cahn on whether VC brand actually moves outcomes, his own mentor Pat Grady on whether Europe is worth the bet at all. His unproven public DPI/IRR (he now discloses hit-rate-to-ARR-milestone, still not realized cash-back), his engineered-for-attention content engine, and the Project Europe backlash (brain-drain critique, gender-representation gap, a 7-day-workweek post that drew "unbelievably toxic" pushback he admits he "wasn't prepared for") are the live critiques to weigh.

## Bio

Harry Stebbings (born 22 June 1996, London) is the founder and host of *The Twenty Minute VC* ("20VC"), one of the most-downloaded venture/startup podcasts in the world, and the founder of the 20VC fund. He made the first recording for the podcast in his childhood bedroom in January 2015 at 17 with a $40 microphone — first episode with Guy Kawasaki, 10 January 2015 — starting with "$50 and not a single contact in the venture capital industry" (Wikipedia); listening back, he says the content wasn't great and he "sounded like a BBC presenter from the 1950s" (Airtree). He enrolled to study law at King's College London but dropped out after four weeks. He did not get 1,000 plays on a show for two years, was recording as often as five episodes per working day, and "didn't make money for three years" — "which is why I was friendless many years" (This Week in Startups). He did a stint at Atomico (joined 2017, left after ~six months), co-founded Stride.VC with Fred Destin in 2018 (raising ~£100M), and stepped down in February 2021 to run his own 20VC fund, launched May 2020. He has said he regrets, "for the first time," how he left that partnership — "I could have been a lot more graceful and I regret not having more grace and elegance there" (Burnouts).

The fund scaled fast: a small ~$8.3M Fund I in 2020 (31 companies, per his own LinkedIn), ~$140M Fund II in 2021 (raised in roughly four months), and a $400M Fund III in October 2024 ($125M seed + $275M Series A) — raised in ~4 months on the back of LP relationships (MIT, RIT Capital Partners) he says he'd built 7–8 years before the raise, and partly funded by $70M pledged over WhatsApp ("every great [decacorn] founder will easily do a million or 2 million by WhatsApp... when you've done 3,000 shows, it's pretty pretty easy to know everyone," Burnouts). Reported AUM is "over $600 million" (Wikipedia, 2024) to ~$650M (Forbes, 2025); TechCrunch/Tabor cite ~$800M across vehicles — see Provenance. Sourced accounts disagree on the fund's own disclosed hit-rate metric of choice: he has published that Fund I ($8M, 31 companies) produced 8 companies now at $50M+ ARR — a 25.8% hit-rate — and separately that he has made 170 investments in 10 years (allO thread). LPs include MIT's endowment, RIT Capital Partners, J. Rothschild Capital Management, and 40+ unicorn founders. Below the main vehicle sit three operator-run sub-funds — 20Sales, 20Product, 20Growth, each staffed by 8 operators from firms like OpenAI, Spotify, LinkedIn, Pinterest — who both source deals and advise portfolio companies (PitchBook; Forbes/Alex York): "Take 20Sales' money and you get eight of the best sales leaders on speed dial." The content side, by contrast, he runs essentially alone: asked on a year-end panel "How many people do you have, Mr. Stebbings?" he answered, "None. It's all me. It's just me working away" (SaaStr Big Fat Quiz).

His self-description is operator, not technologist: "I'm not a technology founder, but I am an operator. I work seven days a week, 15 hours a day" (TechCrunch). He deliberately keeps the fund itself small relative to US mega-funds: "I want to be the best-performing fund in Europe... I don't actually want to be Andreessen. I want a small team of incredibly ambitious, talented people" — citing his own "irritable" temperament as a poor fit for running "a giant VC firm encompassing HR, legal, compliance" (Tech.eu); 20VC's >$600M AUM and <20 employees sit against a16z's ~$45BN AUM and hundreds of staff. His most recent, most publicly contested initiative is Project Europe (launched March 2025): a €10M fund backed by 20VC, Berlin's Point Nine, NY's Adjacent, and 120+ tech founders (Klarna, Mistral, ElevenLabs, Shopify among them), investing €200K for 6.66% equity in founders under 25, deliberately structured as an investment rather than a grant ("grants are held with less accountability and less responsibility," This Week in Startups) and deliberately de-branded from "20VC" — run day-to-day by an outside CEO, Kitty Mayo (ex–Entrepreneur First), because, in his own words, "I honestly don't like the articles or the pictures about me... I don't actually love being [the] center of attention. I only do the media stuff because I wanted to be an investor" (This Week in Startups). Personally, he ties his own operating rhythm to his mother's multiple sclerosis: he walks a marathon with her every Sunday, then "jumps straight back to work" (CNBC Make It / NBC mirror), and has said the diagnosis was the explicit trigger for monetizing the podcast — "it was time to make real money" (Forbes/Zoya Hasan). The throughline across all of it: he turned media into distribution, and distribution into a venture moat — a project he is candid about pursuing "unhealthily obsessed[ly]," his own stated screening trait for founders, "quite like himself" (Forbes/Zoya Hasan).

## Core ideas

- **Capital is commoditized; the scarce resource is the founder's "yes."** Every good deal attracts "15-plus term sheets," so the game is not picking but *winning the right to invest* — being the partner the founder chooses (Goldman Sachs / The VC Corner). His one north-star metric: find the best founders and make them want your money — "My job is to partner with the best founders in the world and make them pick me" (Aleph/Eisenberg). "Venture is about finding, picking, winning and helping. And none of it matters if you can't win" (Forbes/Zoya Hasan).
- **Media is the modern moat — and a two-sided deal-flow machine, not just a distribution channel.** The VC Corner distills his thesis as "media is the single biggest lever an investor can bring today"; at Goldman Sachs this is framed as his "science of content." Concretely it runs both directions: a single LinkedIn post about portfolio company Fyxer (an email-autocomplete startup) drove "$3 to $4 million in ARR" within 72 hours — "hundreds of thousands of impressions, thousands of conversions" (The VC Corner; he has told the same story with a ~$4.5M figure on Burnouts and pegged 20VC's content spend at "$4 million into content just to be the number one content provider"), and — a second, independent worked example — "Today I interviewed a $50BN founder for 20VC. In the post interview chat, I mentioned a company we are leading the seed round for. The $50BN founder asked to angel invest and be a lighthouse customer for them. How media can drive a fund is just insane" (X). He treats every interview as a live warm-introduction and co-investment event between the guest and his own portfolio, not merely content for an audience.
- **Founders over markets, especially early — now with a quantified backtest.** "I follow great entrepreneurs. I think it's absolutely bullshit that we think we're smarter than markets. If there's one thing we have to learn, it is that great founders shape markets. And if that's the case, my job is to simply find the best founders before anyone else" (TechCrunch). At pre-seed/seed, "only the founder matters," restated even more starkly: "I don't focus on the business. Yes, I love business, unit economics and how to build leverage into a model. But pre-seed and seed are so transient; I'm interested in human motivation. Understanding at its core what is driving someone to build the business they're building is everything for me" (Airtree). He has run the actual numbers on this bias: "I would have made more money if I'd just invested in every single founder that I'd ever met... Time and time again, I have missed great companies because I focused on the market, not on the people" — citing two named misses, a seed check at "$2 million on a $10 million price" now worth $19BN, and ElevenLabs (Burnouts). The caveat remains: he won't let a founder he loves be killed by structural risk — "You cannot have a great founder in a market which can get crushed by corruption, politics, weather... I wouldn't have done Pakistan, I wouldn't have done emerging markets" (Aleph).
- **Back the anomalies; break your own rules for greatness — but the sizing evidence cuts both ways.** "This is a business of backing the anomalies, sometimes you have to break the rules and suspend disbelief" (LinkedIn, 150-investments post). His most expensive miss (ElevenLabs, a ~100x) came from following an ownership-percentage rule; his single best Fund I outcome (a $50K check now marked at $8.7M, ~174x, explicitly labeled a "Fund returner") came from *breaking* the opposite sizing rule — "Don't write small checks. If it is less than 1% of the fund, it won't move the needle... Wrong... You will always underestimate the size of your winners" (X). Read together, his real position is less "rules are for breaking" than "portfolio-construction sizing rules are unreliable predictors of outcome size in either direction."
- **The power law is everything, aggregate returns will fall — and his own prediction record on it is close to zero.** He predicts venture returns overall go down while concentrating in ~1% of firms (TechCrunch framing). He has publicly tested his own pattern-matching against this and found it wanting: "After the deployment period for 20VC Fund I, I did an analysis of portfolio. I predicted the top 5. Three years later, not one of the top 5 is as predicted. The true value from seed is always the messy middle" (X) — a rare, self-incriminating data point most VCs never publish, since most only publicize hits.
- **Persistence is a system, not a mood.** "I will email people 50 times... Persistence is everything, everything" (Aleph). Same-day execution: "90% of people don't actually do it the next day" (The VC Corner). He explicitly credits the *line* itself — "it's a game of who can survive the longest" — not to himself but to Jason Calacanis, "the most seminal advice in content" given to him eight years earlier (This Week in Startups) — a provenance correction on a line the dossier previously sourced only to his own retelling.
- **AI is the biggest revolution in history *and* a capital incinerator — held at once.** He simultaneously calls it the biggest wealth transfer in tech history and warns of a "bubble on top of a bubble" (SaaStr), while also admitting the VC community, himself included, has stopped scrutinizing the mechanics behind the boom: "Nvidia investing $100 billion into OpenAI and the start of the circular deals... all of us giving up on caring about things like circular deals. We've all stopped caring. If it marks up my fund, great. It's just the way it is now" (SaaStr).

## Mental models

- **"Winning the right to invest."** Reframes the VC job from selection to seduction; the bottleneck is the founder choosing *you*, and distribution/media is the differentiator. *Worked example:* the $3–4M-in-ARR-in-72-hours Fyxer LinkedIn post and the $50BN-founder-becomes-angel-and-lighthouse-customer episode above; he engineers clips to front-load the best ~12-second hook in the first ~36 seconds, turning audience into the reason founders pick 20VC (The VC Corner; Goldman Sachs). Content mechanics behind it, concretely: "With every show that we do, we create 60 to 70 clips, 20 for each different platform. Different hooks, different fonts, different colored fonts, different intros, different music" — TikTok as the cheap test channel ("TikTok doesn't actually really give a [expletive] how much you post... measure and test it"), YouTube/Instagram as the "spend what's proven" formal channels because YouTube "denigrate[s], downgrade[s] you if you just continuously post" (Burnouts).
- **Back the anomaly / break the rules for greatness.** *Worked example:* he turned down a $250K check in ElevenLabs at a $100M post because it "didn't meet ownership requirements for the fund" — a ~100x miss that would have returned the fund, one he says on a separate occasion could have been "a $300 million position" had he done a $25M seed round instead of passing outright because "voice AI, OpenAI are going to do this... this is like the most obvious play for OpenAI" (Burnouts). His two lessons: "Fear of theoretical future competition is dangerous. OpenAI will just do this. Guess what, ElevenLabs will still win," and "Rules are made to be broken... When the best come, just find a way to work with them. Greatness justifies exceptions" (X, ElevenLabs thread). Cross-reference Core ideas for the mirror-image $50K→$8.7M example, where breaking a *different* rule (minimum check size) produced his best outcome rather than his worst miss.
- **The "psychologically warped founder" profile — now with three named, quantified signals.** From interviewing nearly every $10B+ founder he distills a trinity: a *superiority complex*, a *deep inferiority complex*, and a *masochistic driver*. "I've never met a 10 billion dollar-plus founder that's not psychologically warped" (BigGo, on the GS talk; expanded in his LinkedIn unicorn-founder post). *Worked example, sharpened:* on the Burnouts podcast he names the actual pattern-matched signals and stats behind the trinity — "Parental relationship breakdown. 90% of the best founders have a breakdown in some form of their relationship with a parent... if it's a boy... 90% of the time it's a breakdown with their father relationship... [Two,] there's huge correlation between high skills in gaming and high skills in entrepreneurship... And then number three is you're moving around a lot in childhood." He cites, on glorified entrepreneurship, the founder of Calm: "'You don't understand. This is about enjoying being punched in the face'... I actually relish it. Like, you have to be a masochistic. And I think that very few people are like that" (Burnouts). He treats the distortion as a signal to look *for*, not filter out.
- **Founder-signal heuristics over pedigree.** *Worked example:* he dismisses Ivy/credential signals and instead probes early-onset entrepreneurship — his go-to question is "How did you first make money?", noting "99% of the best" started something young (8–15): lawn-mowing, lemonade stands, building websites (LinkedIn; Goldman Sachs). Forbes independently confirms this as a formal screen — "the 'first job' screening question," walking away if a founder isn't "unhealthily obsessed (quite like himself)" — and a second version from a different profile: the "first dollar" test, gauging obsession by "how they made their first dollar" (Forbes/Alex York). He also reads gaming-clan leadership and childhood mobility as adaptability signals (cross-ref the founder-trinity model above, which supplies the quantified version of this same instinct).
- **Reject visions; run aggressive 90-day sprints.** "Fuck visions... Visions set these artificial ceilings on your potential." *Worked example:* he restates it as "There's nothing more constraining than a vision. You will never be able to foresee what you're able to achieve," and prescribes being "micro ambitious — but not... arrogant enough to know what's going to happen in three to five years" (The VC Corner; Gentleman's Journal).
- **The LP relationship machine — compounding introductions, not a fundraise event.** Talk to ~2 LPs/week even when not raising, ask each for 3 intros (~312 relationships/year), send personalized quarterly updates (The VC Corner). *Worked example, extended:* he attributes the ~4-month close of the $400M Fund III directly to relationships with MIT and RIT Capital Partners cultivated 7–8 years earlier — "How were we able to raise this fund quite quickly? Because I built the relationships well before the fundraise" (Forbes/Alex York). He runs the same discipline (and the same A/B-testing instinct he applies to content) on LP updates themselves: "I've AB tested sending [an LP update] without a picture of my mother and me walking a marathon and a PS about it, and with — when I do it with a picture of me and my mother, we have a 92% response rate. When we do it without, I have a 61%... we've done it across 12... so you've got good data there to suggest there's a meaningful uplift" (Burnouts).
- **The Tokyo Test / extended-exposure read on people.** *Worked example, his own words:* "It's the Tokyo test. You have to fly to Tokyo with them and on that flight you have to be engaged and entertained enough for the entire flight... I do it for hires and investments. Because after an amount of time, the facade drops. You can only uphold that persona for an hour, for two hours, for three hours. You're going to be walking for six and a half hours" (The VC Corner). He applies it to *both* hiring and investing, and literalizes it in his own routine — the weekly marathon-walk with his mother is the same extended-exposure instrument turned on himself; the claim is that a single 6.5-hour shared activity out-reveals three separate interviews plus reference checks, because performance can't survive that long.
- **Stage-arbitrage market read (2024–25 vintage).** *Worked example:* "Seed is the hardest place to be investing today. Prices remain unchanged and high [with] increased competition as more multistage funds move towards seed," and "you simply cannot get the ownership required... if you're a sub $75 million fund." By contrast "Series A is actually the best risk to reward insertion point" because "prices are significantly down" and "competition is heavily reduced" (Deciphr; X). Growth founders "are going to need to reduce expectations on price if they don't have a great AI story." A related, distinct heuristic he applies to growth-rate underwriting rather than entry price: "I always say I don't care how long you take to get to a million in revenue, but I care desperately about how long it takes for you to go from one to fifty" (David Cahn conversation) — time-to-scale, not time-to-first-revenue, is the diligence metric.
- **In AI, the downside is founders leaving, not valuation.** *Worked example:* "One co-founder of a $10BN AI startup left for a $3.5B Meta package. No misconduct. Just better personal economics. For early-stage investors, founder loyalty, not preferences or terms, is the real risk" (X). This reframes diligence at the top of the market away from price toward retention.
- **"Obliterate, Don't Automate."** New, AI-era named framework, a sharper restatement of "back anomalies" specifically about picking B2B/AI theses. *Worked example, his own words:* "Avoid backing software that merely automates existing workflows to make old businesses slightly faster. The true generational opportunities come from completely reinventing industries, like putting an AI doctor in everyone's pocket, rather than selling incremental efficiency to a corporate middleman" (X, Suno/Granola thread). He pairs it with a quantified claim about model dependency risk (attributed in the same thread to a conversation with Mike Mignano): "Roughly 80% of non-coding enterprise tasks can run well on non-frontier models."
- **The angel investor 2×2 matrix (Proactive/Reactive × Internal/External).** Fully specified quadrants: *Proactive* ("you have a thesis that you go after, for example, DTC"); *Reactive* ("your mind is mentally passive to whatever incredible founders want to work on"); *Internal* ("you think about things privately... a very closed brand"); *External* ("you share with the world... building the sign outside of your shop"). *Worked example — his own explicit self-placement:* "I'm biased, and right now, I don't think you want to be internal... in a world of capital abundance, you have to have a brand. It's a game of packaging" (Airtree) — this directly operationalizes the "media is the moat" Core idea and names the market condition (capital abundance) that makes brand the only remaining differentiator.
- **"Neutralizer, not a double negative."** Investor emotional-support calibration framework for working with a struggling or overconfident founder. *Worked example:* "The job of an [investor/advisor] is to be the cheerleader when things are really bad and to be actually the critic when things are really good. You need to neutralize the person... it's really important that you're a neutralizer, not a double negative" (Burnouts).
- **Silence as an interview technique.** *Worked example, named:* "There's a brilliant Elon Musk interview where there's silence and then he reflects on that and what comes next is what is so special, and I really think about getting comfortable in those awkward at times silences" (This Week in Startups).
- **"Chanel vs. Walmart" positioning.** *Worked example:* "The future of venture capital will be Chanel and Walmart. Walmart is Sequoia, SoftBank... walls of money that are almost infinite in supply. Chanel has a very specific customer base and a very precise product" — Forbes frames this as Stebbings implying 20VC will be "the Chanel of venture capital" (Forbes/Zoya Hasan), operationalized by a deliberate $15M Series A check-size cap rather than competing on AUM (cross-ref "best fund, not biggest fund" under Positions).
- **"10 year overnight success."** A named compression of his own founding arc, directly contradicting the outside narrative of a fast-blown-up wunderkind. *Worked example:* "I started 20VC from my family home with a $40 mic. Insane to see multiple studios being built at the same time in the new 20VC HQ. 10 year overnight success. LFG." — posted alongside a photo of the new studio construction, a literal decade-apart artifact comparison (X).
- **The "AI co-attach test."** Named heuristic for distinguishing real AI monetization from marketing: "will customers pay as much or more for the core product because of AI?" *Worked example, falsifiable and company-specific:* he names six public SaaS companies — GitLab, Atlassian, Adobe, Monday, Bill, HubSpot — as having failed this test as of year-end 2025 (LinkedIn, 2025 Year in Review).
- **"Build 10 companies with 10k employees in 10 years."** Project Europe's stated mission framework, in his recurring formulation (per CEO Kitty Mayo's paraphrase-as-direct-quote of his line): "Harry always says: build 10 companies with 10k employees in 10 years" (Sifted). *Worked example:* the fund's actual structure — €200K for 6.66% equity in founders under 25, 150 mentor-investors drawn from Europe's biggest founders — is the literal instantiation of the slogan.

## Decision heuristics

- Back the founder, not the technology or sector: "I don't follow technology. I follow great entrepreneurs" (TechCrunch).
- When you have founder conviction, override your own criteria — "Every time I have felt this conviction on a founder and not done the deal, it has been a mega loss" (LinkedIn).
- Distrust portfolio-construction sizing dogma in *either* direction: the $50K→$8.7M win came from ignoring a "≥1% of fund" minimum-check rule; the ElevenLabs miss came from enforcing an ownership-percentage rule. Neither rule reliably predicted outcome size (X, both threads).
- M&A only on "Hell yeah," never "Why not?": failed acquisitions "were ones where we said 'Why not?' instead of 'Hell yeah'... minimal revenue, adjacent enough to be relevant but not enough for me to care" (X).
- Don't ship mediocrity: "Would you ever let your product be released if 30% of it was meh?" — applied to every interaction (The VC Corner).
- Execute the same night, not the next day (The VC Corner).
- Sector avoidance via talent flow: he doesn't back climate "because talent just doesn't want to build in climate right now" (Impact Loop) — follow where the best founders point.
- Treat institutional brand-safety as an exploitable inefficiency: "you don't get fired for doing IBM" — big LPs allocate for safety even after receiving detailed negative assessments of large platform funds, leaving room for specialists (BigGo, summarizing his GS Talks-at-GS appearance; the GS page itself carries no transcript).
- Discount theoretical incumbent competition; the great founder still wins the niche (ElevenLabs lesson, X) — though he has also cited it as his own costliest recurring diagnostic error (the ElevenLabs and $19BN-seed misses, Burnouts) when applied in the *other* direction (over-weighting the incumbent threat and passing).
- Maintain optionality / never run out of runway — the best windows (he cites a ~12-hour OpenAI window) arrive unexpectedly (The VC Corner).
- Time-to-first-revenue is unimportant; time-from-$1M-to-$50M-ARR is what he underwrites (David Cahn conversation).
- For angels specifically: size to survive a total loss (he put 20% of a $500K net worth into angel investing early on) and diversify by count, not by fractional dollars — "10 deals at 10k each... because 10k out of a 100k pool means on the upside, you'll see some great returns. Whereas 1k from 100 people is tough to make look good" (Airtree).
- Keep a consistent angel check size as a discipline signal: "when people write a 75k cheque and a 25k cheque, that looks like your convictions are all over the place" (Airtree).
- Treat deal flow as a tradable, reciprocal asset: "I spend 90 minutes a day sending deals to different people... You're never going to get anything back unless you give first" (Airtree).
- Price matters less for an angel than for a VC — optimize instead for which 2–3 founders "you'll do anything for, any time of day" (Airtree).
- Don't worry about signaling risk on passed deals: "So long as you're open and transparent about it, it's fine" (Airtree).
- Under stress, don't respond in real time: "One of my biggest lessons has been don't respond in real time... We talk about one-way and two-way door decisions. Most things are two-way door decisions actually" (Burnouts).
- Decision-by-trusted-proxy for hard calls: "Whenever you have a hard decision, think of one person you really, really respect... for me, it's Pat Grady. He's the head of Sequoia... anytime I have a really hard decision, I just think, what would Pat do" (Burnouts).
- Grants under-produce accountability relative to investment, even at the earliest, most philanthropic-feeling stage: "grants are held with less accountability and less responsibility... it's like when you give free classes, the participation is lower than if they're paid for" — the explicit reasoning behind structuring Project Europe as €200K-for-equity rather than a grant (This Week in Startups).
- Frame a low-conviction, high-volume program as "experiments," not underperformance: a 30% pull-through rate is fine "because you're starting so early you don't need to have 80% pull-through like you might want from a seed fund or a series A fund" (This Week in Startups).

## Positions by decision type

- **Pivot vs. persevere (founder at the helm).** Keep founders in charge — "this is not the time or age of professional CEOs" because "the rate of change in the Age of AI is just too fast"; "Your startup (or large company) will fail or fall far behind if founders aren't at the helm" (LinkedIn, on 2,221 CEO departures, up 24%).
- **How to price (as founder).** Best founders don't optimize for price: they want fair prices and avoid taking too much capital (LinkedIn founder-advice posts). As investor, he reads stage: pay up at growth only with a real AI story; favor Series A where price has corrected (Deciphr).
- **When to break the rules.** For a great founder, suspend ownership/diligence rules — the ElevenLabs miss is his standing cautionary tale (X); the $50K→$8.7M win is the mirror-image counter-case (breaking a minimum-check-size rule instead).
- **When to walk on M&A.** Only acquire on "Hell yeah"; the "Why not?" deals fail (X).
- **Raise vs. bootstrap (his own fund).** He bootstrapped credibility through media first — "I did about 125 shows before we made a dollar on 20VC. Two years of shows" (Aleph) — then raised institutional capital on the back of that distribution, later formalizing the mechanism as WhatsApp-native ("every great [decacorn] founder will easily do a million or 2 million by WhatsApp," Burnouts) and relationship-compounding (2 new LPs/week, 3 intros each) rather than a discrete sales process.
- **Focus vs. diversify (geography).** Concentrate the narrative on Europe while staying globally competitive: "We are the feet on the ground for the U.S. funds too" (TechCrunch). Explicitly rejects the alternative of relocating to compete head-on in the Valley — "do I want to bet my whole strategy on beating Mark Andreessen when he's going to drive founders to his house?" (Forbes/Zoya Hasan) — and disagrees with his own primary mentor, Pat Grady of Sequoia, who has told him "we have to be in the US to be relevant. Europe's a sinking ship" (Burnouts).
- **Scale the fund vs. stay small (his own firm).** Explicitly chooses performance-per-dollar over AUM-maximization: "I want to be the best-performing fund in Europe... I don't actually want to be Andreessen" — a stated, self-aware operating-model choice grounded in his own "irritable" temperament rather than pure strategy (Tech.eu). Quantified: 20VC's >$600M AUM and <20 staff against a16z's ~$45BN AUM and hundreds of employees.
- **How to structure a philanthropic/mentorship-adjacent early-stage vehicle (Project Europe).** Investment, not grant, for accountability; deliberately de-branded from "20VC" and run by an outside CEO rather than by himself, because he "[doesn't] actually love being [the] center of attention" (This Week in Startups); an explicit age-banded ICP (18–25) he defends against the "illegal"/"shortsighted" criticism as no different from any firm having "an ideal customer profile... You have to start somewhere" (This Week in Startups).
- **How to respond to public backlash.** Largely by continuing to publish rather than engaging critics directly — Sifted noted he "didn't return [their] request for comment" on the Project Europe pile-on; but on the 996/7-day-workweek backlash he did engage directly and partially conceded ground: "I wasn't prepared for the criticism he received" and "I think that's [uncritical 996] very ignorant to do... if you don't allow people breaks in there and a gym workout, it's just moronic" (CNBC Make It / NBC mirror) — a documented instance of walking back a viral claim under pressure rather than doubling down.
- **What to avoid.** Climate (talent flow); emerging/politically fragile markets even with a great founder (Aleph); deals where you fear "theoretical future competition" (X) — while simultaneously naming that exact bias (over-weighting incumbent risk) as the mechanism behind two of his most-cited misses (Burnouts).
- **Diligence focus in AI.** Underwrite founder retention over valuation (X, founders-leaving thread); underwrite growth *rate* (time from $1M to $50M ARR) over speed-to-first-revenue (David Cahn conversation); apply the "AI co-attach test" to distinguish real monetization from marketing.
- **Whether VC brand/capital meaningfully changes startup outcomes ("kingmaking").** Takes the affirmative side against a skeptical peer investor: pressing David Cahn (who argues "you can't make a company succeed" with capital alone) by citing Cahn's own portfolio company Profound as a counterexample — "you don't think... Sequoia and the subsequent quick round has helped them significantly get great talent, get great customers, and get subsequent funding" (David Cahn conversation). Consistent with his own "media is the moat" thesis: capital-plus-brand is not neutral.

High-energy, fast, candid to the point of bluntness. Heavy superlatives ("the very best," "incredible," "unbelievable") and profanity for emphasis ("Fuck visions," "absolutely bullshit," "moderation is largely for cowards," "work fucking hard"). Founder-obsessed and self-aware about being underestimated (the teenager with a $40 mic). You encouraged me to do English'" (David Cahn conversation) — repeated near-verbatim across at least two separate appearances, a genuine verbal tic rather than a one-off line. ## Where they disagree

- **vs. Peter Walker (Carta, data/empiricist — not on roster but the cleanest sourced foil):** On 2025 froth, Stebbings posted "200x revenue valuations are back... I have never seen markets as frothy as this." Walker pushed back publicly: "Harry sees a LOT so I believe him — but we also forget how silly 2021 was. There was 1 quarter that year where the average Series A was 5x the valuation of the prior seed round" (X) — i.e., the data says today is less anomalous than the vibe. A second, independent instance of the same pattern: when he disclosed Fund I's 25.8% hit-rate to $50M+ ARR, a LinkedIn commenter (Kiran Mehta) asked for the missing denominator — "it would be even better if you could share what ARR they were at, on average, when investing" — the identical "impressive headline, no base rate" critique, this time from an anonymous commenter rather than a named data professional.
- **vs. Jason Lemkin (jason-lemkin):** On his own year-end and pricing panels Stebbings plays animated moderator/headline-writer and supplies the market read, while Lemkin carries the harder SaaS-fundamentals and durable-revenue analysis (SaaStr panels; The VC Insider) — e.g. Lemkin's "Product of the year is Claude 3.5 or 3.7... They did not work until Claude," a data-and-product-quality claim distinct from Stebbings' more macro/founder-romantic picks. Stebbings is more founder-romantic and momentum-driven; Lemkin more unit-economics-and-retention driven.
- **vs. Ben Horowitz (ben-horowitz):** Horowitz built a16z's value-add around a heavy services platform and operating playbooks (the "Hard Thing" of management); Stebbings argues most VC "value-add" is over-credited and that the rare thing founders actually want is *distribution/media* — the lever The VC Corner distills as "the single biggest lever an investor can bring today," evidenced by a single LinkedIn post generating $3–4M in ARR for portfolio company Fyxer in 72 hours (The VC Corner). He also rejects bringing in professional CEOs that Horowitz-style operating advice often presupposes ("this is not the... age of professional CEOs").
- **vs. Garry Tan (garry-tan) / Paul Graham (paul-graham, YC):** YC's selection is heavily program/market/idea-and-batch driven; Stebbings is a near-pure founder-maximalist ("only the founder matters" at seed) who dismisses pedigree and even structured diligence when conviction is high. Where PG prizes "make something people want" (market-pull), Stebbings prizes the anomalous founder who will *create* the market.
- **vs. Reid Hoffman (reid-hoffman):** Hoffman champions long-arc "blitzscaling" and grand theses; Stebbings explicitly rejects multi-year visions ("nothing more constraining than a vision"; 90-day sprints) and is skeptical of forecasting 3–5 years out.
- **vs. Warren Buffett (warren-buffett):** Buffett wants margin of safety, business fundamentals, and circle of competence; Stebbings openly flags "less focus on business fundamentals than ever" yet stays in the game, optimizes for the power-law anomaly, and treats "suspend disbelief" as a feature. Opposite ends of the discipline spectrum.
- **vs. David Cahn (Sequoia — not on roster, a live on-air disagreement):** Cahn's stated view is capital brand-name doesn't create outcomes — "the lesson that punches you in the stomach in venture is you can't make a company succeed." Stebbings pushes back directly, citing Cahn's own portfolio company Profound as a counterexample of Sequoia's involvement moving talent, customer, and funding outcomes — a genuine, on-record disagreement about whether "media is the moat" and brand-capital more broadly are real forces or self-serving narrative (David Cahn conversation).
- **vs. his own mentor, Pat Grady (Sequoia — not on roster):** Grady's view: "we have to be in the US to be relevant. Europe's a sinking ship... we should move to the US today." Stebbings: "I disagree with him that we have to be in the US to be relevant. But we disagree and continue to on that" (Burnouts) — notable as a disagreement with the single person he says he defers to on every other hard decision ("what would Pat do").
- **vs. Nicolas Colin (The Family — not on roster, Project Europe critic):** Colin's critique of the fund's under-25/high-volume-of-young-founders model: "I've felt nothing but unease since the project launched... When I look at projects such as Project Europe, I think of emerging countries that invest heavily in higher education but fail to create enough high-skilled jobs, leading to brain drain, unemployment, and social unrest." Stebbings' implicit counter (per This Week in Startups) is that the alternative — advising young Europeans to leave for the US — is worse: "I wanted to fundamentally change the Vibes... you can build businesses here." Stebbings did not respond to Sifted's specific request for comment on Colin's critique.
- **vs. Lucas Bédout (Hyperline — not on roster, Project Europe critic):** Bédout's position is structurally opposite Stebbings' "more founders, more seed capital" thesis: "Europe does not need more seed capital or more young entrepreneurs... I actually think Europe needs [fewer], higher quality companies that can cover the whole continent and get big enough to compete with US players" (Sifted) — a direct fork on whether Europe's problem is volume of founders or concentration of scale-stage capital; Stebbings' TWiST appearance instead locates the real gap in operator depth and dead liquidity markets, closer to (but not identical to) Bédout's scale-focused framing.
- **vs. Suranga Chandratillake (Balderton Capital — not on roster, 996 critic):** On the 7-day-workweek post, Chandratillake: "Calling on founders to work insane hours nonstop is just bad advice... If you're a CEO, don't listen to a jumped-up finance bro in a hoodie who has never done your job telling you how to do it!" — an explicit challenge to the legitimacy of a non-operator VC prescribing founder hours (TheNextWeb). Notably, Stebbings later partially converges with Chandratillake's separate, softer point that Silicon Valley hustle is performative: "Stebbings now agrees with this view and said hustle culture is 'over-glamorized' in the States" (CNBC Make It / NBC mirror) — disagreement on the prescription, convergence on the diagnosis.
- **vs. Sarah Wernér (Husmus — not on roster, 996 critic):** "What Europe really needs isn't more hustle-porn, it's more aggressive funding... If a team of 10 is burning out to keep up with a 50-person U.S. VC or Chinese government-backed startup, the problem isn't their stamina, it's their cap table" (CNBC Make It / NBC mirror) — a clean, structural counter-thesis to Stebbings reading Europe's competitive gap through hustle/vibes rather than capital structure, in the same pattern as Peter Walker's froth pushback.
- **vs. himself, across audiences.** On the CNBC/NBC-mirrored 996 fallout he argues Europe's gap is *not* capital ("I don't think it's a lack of access to cash at all") but work ethic and storytelling ("Americans are much better at telling exciting stories"). On the This Week in Startups appearance discussing the same underlying thesis (Project Europe / "make Europe great again"), he argues the real gap is *not* work ethic but structural: lack of operators who've "seen 100 million, 200 million, 300 million, 400 million" in scale, and "local liquidity markets [that] are completely dead" because regulators "stop every M&A transaction." Two different, not fully reconciled explanations for the same claim, offered in different venues within months of each other.

## Blind spots

- **Track record vs. narrative — sharper than before, still short of realized returns.** Public sourcing emphasizes fund size, AUM, unicorn-LP count, and unicorn count (PitchBook/Tabor cite "13+," the Goldman Sachs page's own blurb says "ten" — a second, independent instance of inconsistent self-reported/press-reported scale metrics, alongside the AUM discrepancy below) more than realized DPI/IRR. He has since disclosed one concrete number — Fund I ($8M, 31 companies) hit 8 companies at $50M+ ARR, a 25.8% hit-rate — which sharpens but doesn't resolve the gap: hit-rate-to-an-ARR-milestone is a real, falsifiable number, but it is still not DPI, IRR, or cash actually returned to LPs, and a LinkedIn commenter immediately asked for the missing entry-point denominator. His own forecast that aggregate venture returns fall while concentrating in ~1% of firms is a claim he must himself beat — and his self-published Fund I top-5 prediction (0-for-5) is direct evidence his own stock-picking, as distinct from his fund's blended outcome, has been unreliable so far.
- **Engineered-for-engagement vs. truth-seeking.** His disclosed content mechanics make the incentive explicit — optimizing against severe early drop-off (~68–82% churn cited across sources if no insight lands in the first 3 minutes), engineering hooks for the first ~36 seconds, atomizing every show into 60-70 platform-specific clips, and (per disclosure) ~85 of 100 clips per show AI-generated. An attention-maximizing function sits in tension with calm, disconfirming analysis. His froth tweet drew a measured, data-based correction from Carta's Peter Walker; his Fund I hit-rate post drew a similar ask-for-the-denominator pushback from an ordinary commenter — a repeatable pattern of vibe-read/headline-stat vs. base-rate.
- **Self-aware conflict-of-interest admission on circular AI deals.** "We've all stopped caring [about circular deals]. If it marks up my fund, great" (SaaStr) is a rare, unflattering, explicit statement that his own analytical rigor bends around what inflates his own marks — a documented instance of the incentive problem rather than an inferred one.
- **Access tension.** He built investing credibility from a media platform; the content engine "forces titles and hooks toward conflict and shock value," while the founder relationships he prizes require trust and vulnerability — two modes that pull against each other (synthesis grounded in his own described strategy, Goldman Sachs / The VC Corner). Whether a host dependent on guest access can ask genuinely hard questions of people he wants to invest alongside is a fair open question.
- **Founder-over-market dogma cuts both ways.** "Only the founder matters" at seed is clean but can underweight market structure, timing, and category economics — a known failure mode of charisma-led selection. He partly self-corrects (he won't back great founders in fragile markets; his biggest self-named misses were actually the *opposite* error, over-weighting incumbent-competition risk over founder quality on ElevenLabs and a $19BN seed deal) but the bias is structural and he has not fully reconciled the two failure modes he names.
- **Hype-amplification risk, now with a direct admission of miscalibration.** His "biggest wealth transfer in history" / "bubble on top of a bubble" framings can read as simultaneously hedged and hype-generating. The 996/7-day-workweek post is the clearest documented instance: he "wasn't prepared for the criticism he received," and on reflection partially walked it back ("very ignorant," "moronic" without breaks) while still defending the underlying claim that speed matters against Valley/China competition — a genuine, on-record example of a viral take outrunning his own considered position, not a hypothetical risk.
- **Project Europe's gender-representation gap, confirmed by the fund's own CEO months after launch.** Of the initial 150 individual LPs announced, only 5 were women; of the fund's 10 partners, only 2 are women (by a September 2025 follow-up, 27 of 200 investors, ~13.5%). CEO Kitty Mayo's own explanation implicates the informal, WhatsApp-network-driven sourcing method that both the Forbes profile and this critique independently credit for Stebbings' speed advantage elsewhere: "these networks depend on who's in the WhatsApp chats. Because the team started with quite a strong male presence, that has network effects" (Sifted) — the same mechanism, cutting both ways.
- **Persistent negative peer reception, not a one-week news cycle.** Months after Project Europe's launch, its own CEO confirmed continuing friction: "I got less support than I was expecting from the wider ecosystem... some of the most prominent posts about Project Europe were knocking it rather than supporting it" (Sifted) — corroborating, rather than resolving, the initial critic pile-on (Colin, Bédout, and others).
- **Quality-at-scale concerns.** Listener reviews have criticized the show's drift toward volume/sensationalism ("all big money corporate," "not much to learn") — anecdotal Apple Podcasts reviews, labeled as such, not formal criticism. He has separately conceded a version of this himself when asked what criticism actually stings: "when people really criticize my interviewing style... I interrupt too much" (This Week in Startups) — a rare admission of a specific, correctable flaw rather than a deflected one.
