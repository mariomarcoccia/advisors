## Summary

Co-founder and (former) co-CEO of PostHog (open-source product analytics turned 16+ product developer suite, YC W20, ~$1.4B valuation, ~300k customers), an ex-enterprise-sales VP who built the anti-enterprise-sales company and documents every belief in public, including his own mistakes. His lens: **radical transparency is a competitive moat and a hiring filter, not a virtue**; **the enemy is obscurity, not competitors**, so brand must be weird and polarizing rather than conversion-optimized; **velocity beats validation** ("unless you already know that you're good at product you're probably bad at product"); and **org design is the product**: teams of six or fewer, engineers decide what to build, PMs as "compass," no deadlines, no formal 1:1s. Sharpest contrarian calls: 100% inbound because developers cannot be cold-sold; price on cost plus a modest margin and never "to value"; give away more to earn more; VC "is a tool, not a religion"; a "team, not a family" with four-month severance. He opposes sales-led SaaS GTM (Lemkin), PM-owns-the-roadmap orthodoxy (Horowitz), and crunch culture, and he qualifies his own advice constantly ("isn't universally good advice"). Watch the seam: an insider Glassdoor review calls the marketed autonomy day-to-day, not directional, and in 2025 he himself conceded "too much top down communication." His own texts also hold unresolved tensions (deadlines vs no deadlines, anti-measurement brand vs measured growth experiments, transparency vs the pay exception).

## Bio

James Hawkins co-founded PostHog in 2020 with technical co-founder Tim Glaser, going through Y Combinator's W20 batch. They quit their jobs in August 2019, pivoted five times (six products and 100+ user meetings in nine months), and PostHog was the sixth idea, a tool forged from their own pain: "the first thing that worked for us... was self-hosted product analytics because we pivoted so many times and every time we had to set up product analytics over and over again and we're just getting frustrated having to implement it." (YC Podcast, auto-caption.) Before PostHog, Hawkins was VP of Sales at Arachnys, an enterprise outbound-sales background he deliberately inverted. After a Hacker News launch in February 2020 and a $3M seed (closed April 2020 on SAFEs), PostHog raised a $9M Series A and $15M Series B, over $27M in total by 2023, then a **$70M Series D led by Stripe at a ~$920M valuation** (reportedly after Patrick Collison tweeted "out of the blue" that the website was cool) and, by late 2025, a further raise at $1.4B. The company grew from 37 people (Nov 2023) to roughly 150 (Oct 2025), ~300,000 customers (mostly free) and 16-17 products. In May 2026 he and Tim split the co-CEO role by emphasis: Hawkins goes deep on AI product and engineering, Tim goes wide on execution. His judgment is worth borrowing because he has put the whole operating manual on record (handbook, pricing, raise mechanics, pivots, annual self-reviews) and built a cashflow-positive business on bets that contradict SaaS orthodoxy.

## Core ideas

- **Radical transparency is strategy, not virtue.** Trust was the original reason: "Trust was the original reason for it... the foundation of trust we felt was transparency. So... instead of just having like a one pager landing page we're going to like really fully explain everything we can about who we are what we're trying to do." (YC Podcast, auto-caption.) "There really hasn't been a single situation where transparency hasn't been to our company's advantage." (How to run a transparent startup.) It doubles as a recruiting filter and politics-killer; see Mental models.
- **The real risk is that no one cares.** "the actual risk is like no one cares. That's... by such a long way the biggest risk that you're taking in an early stage startup is like no one has any attention for you because they're watching Tik Tok all day." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) Marketing becomes a fight for attention against TikTok, not against B2B rivals.
- **Velocity over validation.** "unless you already know that you're good at product you're probably bad at product which means that you will learn more by doing than by planning in advance." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) "You will always get more clarity by building something and getting it into users' hands, so don't spend ages at this level." (PMF game.)
- **Developer-led, not PM-led.** The founding wedge, stated by his co-founder: "Whilst everyone else is focused on product managers, we've believed since Day 1 that product analytics should be developer-led." (Tim Glaser, Series B announcement; not Hawkins's words.) In Hawkins's own 2023 version: "The change is that we've realized that the simple way PostHog can achieve this is to help engineers be better at product." (Our simpler goal.)
- **Product-led and inbound, because developers cannot be cold-sold.** "I cannot think of any harder group than developers to convince, via a cold-call or email, to buy software. We should focus on inbound." (Handbook: how we make money.) "PostHog doesn't do any outbound sales at all. We're a completely inbound business." (Churn.FM.)
- **Org design is the product.** He built PostHog as "the exact place I'd want to work" because prior startups "all had many flaws - usually through a lack of organization design." (What motivates me.) Hence small teams, no managers, no PM-owned roadmap.
- **Pricing is a living product, priced on cost, not on value.** "Pricing isn't just how you make money, it defines your identity, how you market, and how you sell." (Pricing advice, Vanagas house view.) His own voice: "the more we give away for free, and the less we ask from users on day 1, the more money we make." (Pricing lessons, founders.) See Mental models: Cost-plus pricing.
- **People priority stack.** Team first, then users, then customers, then investors, in his own voice: "You also have investors, who have given you lots of money. Don't fall in love with them - you must prioritize your own team and their performance, then users then customers, in that order." (How we raised $3M.) The 2020 house essay says the same in "we" voice: "If we keep our team first and users second, then our investors will take care of themselves!" (Why you may not need a sales team, house "we" under his byline.)
- **Simple strategy.** "One of the things I've learned at PostHog is the simpler a strategy, the more likely it's right." (Our simpler goal.) "The longer your strategy gets, the more useless it becomes." (Nail Self Serve.)
- **Earned ambition and long horizons.** Start with "a relatively unambitious satellite launching business" like SpaceX, earn the bigger mission, and raise when you do not need to. (YC Podcast, auto-caption.) "how do we make sure this customer is still using us in another year's time is much more our focus than how do we get as much money as we can out of them today." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.)
- **Optimism as a precondition.** "You cannot change the world without first believing you can change the world." (Handbook: values.)

## Mental models

- **The PMF Game (5-level "snakes and ladders").** Five sequential levels: find a significant problem, validate it by talking to users, get users to use the product, keep them coming back, onboard your first five reference customers. *Gist:* "Finding product-market fit is a bit like playing snakes and ladders. There are numerous traps, winning takes a little luck, and the only certainty is it's never a straight line." *Worked example:* they built a sales-territory product for 15 companies who "wanted to try it," sent the invite link, "and only one clicked the link." Lesson: "There's no substitute for seeing what people do instead of what they say." (PMF game; YC Podcast: "14 of them didn't even click the link... we hadn't done any of the user interviews correctly.")

- **Attention economics: be remarkable, not optimized.** You compete with "what they're listening to on the radio or what they're doing on their phone or whatever else," and with Mr. Beast for online attention. *Worked example, billboards:* brand campaigns with no conversion goal: "you're unlikely to decide to install like our SDK whilst you're driving your car down the freeway... we're not going to care about conversion. We are just going to try and raise awareness." *Worked example, inverting 80/20:* "there's no point doing 80[/]20 like there's it's just a missed shot..." and "if I could like double our traffic and half our conversion rate, I would take that." (all YC Podcast, auto-caption.) The mechanism is polarization: "it needs to be so weird that it polarizes... we don't care if they disproportionately hate it."

- **Don't 80:20 the brand (increasing marginal returns).** *Worked example:* the merch store escalated from mailing items from his house to a Shopify store with a "PostHog shower curtain," custom site components, a team photo shoot and limited-edition tees. His rule: "A lot of design or brand work has an increasing marginal return. This is because a great brand's purpose is that you stand out. You do not stand out by being average!" and "So, if you're serious, go nuts on your brand. Go weirdly hard." (How not to be boring.)

- **"What would stop me from using this thing?"** A pre-launch adoption-blocker audit. *Worked example:* "This was the most important conversation Tim and I had before launching PostHog." They concluded "the majority would turn away at the website, the docs, and the readme first," so they built a distinctive site and a public handbook explaining what, why and how. He also writes that "Everyone judges books by their covers" and that a templated site signals "The team behind the product isn't very strong." (How not to be boring.)

- **Brand is the whole company.** Brand covers investment mix across functions, hiring process, how you decide what to build, board composition and pricing mechanics, not only visuals. *Worked example:* the piece itself is brand work: "I don't care if it converts people, I just want to leave an impression." Polarization is the test: "If your brand isn't going to turn off some people that might have bought your product, then it isn't strong enough to stand out." (How not to be boring.)

- **Funny beats useful for reach.** *Worked example:* "if I just write something I think is funny, it can sometimes go a thousand times further in terms of reach. So if I'm just trying to raise awareness, it has to be funny." The Slack marketing channel enforces it: "your joke is just not funny... it has to be better this you look like you're trying." He is candid it may be rationalized fun: "perhaps I'm doing it because I find it fun and I'm justifying it because I run a company." (YC Podcast; Executive Brand, auto-caption.)

- **The website is the sales team.** *Worked example:* "since like 2021ish, the website's our sales team. like there isn't a single customer that won't go through the website before they buy the product... we don't have to hire a sales team." Pre-launch they wrote the critiques into the site: "they don't have a clear business model... we said hey this is how we think the pay product is going to work in future." (YC Podcast, auto-caption.) Companion statements: "Our website and product are our storefront - not a sales team. It needs to be one of our core competencies." (CEO diary: How come your website is so nice?, 2021.) "Our open source and free user base is our sales team." (Nail Self Serve, 2022.) His sequencing caveat: a mediocre templated site is "exactly what you want on day one"; treat the website as a product only once you have "some consistency and a sense of brand," and "no one cares about brand consistency in your first year."

- **Writing for developers (five-part framework).** Be human, be concise, pick a side, be engaging, be precise. *Worked example:* hitting the Hacker News front page is "one of the most surprisingly important skills I've realized I have": "Out of five posts that I hoped would get there, four did." Rules: write the title first ("if you can't get it right up front, you haven't got a clear enough idea"), quantify ("We grew a lot because of X" becomes "We had our first 100 users from X"), ban "A lot", "Soon", "Quickly", "Better", and "Most of your time should be spent rewriting." (Writing for developers.)

- **Features tell, benefits sell. Well, they used to.** *Worked example:* Salesforce hero copy is "a benefit sandwich with a light feature filling"; Datadog leads with "what it is." Rule by audience: "For executives who don't use the software, and certainly don't implement it – go nuts. For everyone else, benefits-oriented language is a poor user experience." PostHog's own multi-product headline ("Open Source Product OS...") started "a large debate internally"; an A/B test showed "no impact on conversion rate," which he read as permission, not proof. (Tell me about features, not benefits.)

- **A wide company with small teams (≤6).** Handbook rules: "A small team should strictly be between 2-6 people"; "A small team has the final call in which of its features get into production, with no need for external QA/control"; each team is responsible for usage, quality and revenue in its area. *Worked example in his voice:* "No more than six people per team. Any more than that and it all goes to hell: more meetings, more process, more bottlenecks, and more bullshit." (Product management is broken; Handbook: small teams.) Origin: Jeff Lawson (Twilio) describing "the greatest innovation at Amazon was that it felt like thousands of small startups." (How we designed our company for speed.)

- **Ship the org structure.** *Worked example:* strategy is to "build every single piece of software that a software team needs", potentially 100 products; the org choice is control, design and polish versus velocity: "we're not going to win on design polish here. We're going to win on engineering velocity." Evidence he uses: a YC batch of ~500 people ships "probably like 800 or 900 products" while big companies need that many people for one; AWS is "hundreds of small teams." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Autonomy or control.** "There are two ways to grow a team - autonomy, or control." Transparency is the enabler: you cannot give autonomy without shared context. He carves out exceptions: "perhaps in design-led companies, where control might be a competitive advantage and is more important than autonomy, or in companies that aren't managed consistently." (How to run a transparent startup.) In 2023: "You won't manage to compete on both polish via controlling a team more tightly and being wider at the same time, which requires autonomy." (Multi-product pricing.)

- **Designing for speed: nine choices.** No design by default; no PM by default; hire for autonomy; each team a mini startup; nearly everyone an engineer; no meetings Tue/Thu; late mover; VC; trust and feedback over process. *Worked example:* "no design by default" was sequenced: first a design system with a designer, then reactive design that does not block merging. "At the time of writing, we are 37 people, only 11 of whom are in Go To Market or Operations roles." His test: "figure out if you value speed and autonomy over polish and control... and accept there are trade-offs! Just make them consciously." (How we designed our company for speed.)

- **PMs don't own the roadmap; engineers decide, PMs are the compass.** *Worked example, Karl and session replay:* "Karl disagreed and built it anyway. It ended up being wildly popular with customers, and changed our entire company strategy!" "We think of PMs as the team's compass. They don't decide the destination, but they provide information to let the team know if they're headed in the right direction." (Product management is broken; Dear future head of product describes the head-of-product role as gathering context "into clear actionable opinions" and removing bottlenecks, never owning a roadmap.)

- **Documented reversal: "I used to think you don't need product people."** *Worked example:* "Hiring a product person felt like it would slow us down." Yet "By PostHog's series B, our team showed me that I was totally wrong." Trigger: "the most important part of getting the team to click was our engineers telling Tim (my cofounder) and me that we needed to hire a product person." Why: "we didn't know every user ourselves any more, and there was too much data to hold in our heads." Profile: PMs who can code, "scrappy, anti-plan type folk who wouldn't create a detailed year long roadmap." Anti-rule: "product doesn't dictate to engineers by producing feature requirements." Two PMs in a team of 30. (I used to think you don't need product people.)
- **How the PM reversal actually happened: a full-team feedback session.** At the first offsite in 2020, each person received feedback from everyone else, moderated "to be 70% constructive and as specific as possible." The founders heard that "we've seen product done badly in our previous careers, and as a result, we aren't building this discipline at all into the company." An engineer, Paolo, had "proactively acted like a product manager," gathering "deep feedback from dozens of users"; they moved him into product and hired Marcus as VP Product in 2021. His pre-reversal doubts, in his own words: "Don't they just dictate what to build to our team, thus annoying our team, and probably understanding our (technical) users less well than our engineers?" The condition that made it acceptable: "We've not had to sacrifice engineering autonomy. Both Marcus and Paolo can write code, and they both ship from time to time." (CEO diary: Things I learned last year.)

- **Pipeline-driven product management as a failure mode (from his enterprise past).** *Worked example:* "Here's a list of features the enterprise sales team wants" produced "lots of initial enterprise contracts, but negative outcomes in the long term": a frustrating product, "No word of mouth growth", "High customer churn". "If product focuses on enabling existing pipeline to close, versus customer retention and growth, you end up in the above failure mode." (I used to think you don't need product people.) The ceo-diary-1 version is the "losing loop" below.

- **Losing loop vs winning loop.** Losing: invest in sales assets, build features salespeople need, "Annoying our users with poor UX since the product was built for selling and not using", churn, repeat. Winning: invest in open source, developers at big companies adopt it, adoption widens within each company, "Making more money as big companies happily pay for the value." *Worked example:* despite 100+ subscribers, "Weirdly enough, at the time, we felt we provided a worse experience to paying customers like this than to free users." Marketing test: "Can we do this without building any more paid features?" (Winning from the back.)

- **Late-mover advantage, in markets with a $1bn+ incumbent.** "There are tons of competitors in product analytics, and they've pretty much all got 300+ person teams, around $100M or more in funding, and years and years of headstart. Yet, we're totally confident we can win as a late mover." Rule: "we simply won't build a new product unless there is a $1bn+ competitor already offering it. We love competition. It shows you there's demand." (Winning from the back; How we designed our company for speed.)

- **Nail X: strategy in two words.** "That's why, at PostHog, we communicate strategy as 'Nail X', where X is the thing we need to nail. It's simple and effective." *Worked example:* "Nail Enterprise" (OSS PMF, then $20K-$40K mid-size, then $200K+ large) became "Nail Funnels" (get five reference customers) and then "Nail Self Serve" in June 2022, after self-serve deals closed in weeks while "almost none of the customers that have put us through a big buying process have converted to revenue yet." Pillars: Quality ("work like a Swiss watch"), self-service subscription, deployments. (Nail Self Serve; I used to think you don't need product people.)

- **Five reference customers.** Defined as "genuinely delighted customers, using the product a lot, paying full price", with the instruction to "drop everything if needed to get it right with this group." *Worked example:* a board meeting realized PMF existed for the free open-source product but not the paid one; going deep on a handful showed funnels were weak, "We 'nailed funnels', and revenue started climbing as a result." (I used to think you don't need product people; Multi-product pricing; Winning from the back.) Same loop later with session replay: "focusing on more demanding, paid users improved the product for everyone."

- **Needs-and-haves ICP, plus an anti-ICP.** Score every customer, closed and not: "Have they used one of our free products successfully?", "Do they have a centralized data team?", "Is engineering making the purchasing decision?", "Do they need to control their data?" *Worked example:* healthcare looked like an industry segment but "it's the need to control data that is important," so they omitted industry and revenue proxies. "Tracking customers that never bought anything was jut as useful as tracking those that did." (sic) "Once that was done, we quickly had hundreds more." (How we found our ICP.)

- **Pre-PMF machine: learn, ship, repeat; default to starting over.** "The most perfect pre-product-market-fit startup, is a machine that repeatedly (i) learns (ii) ships (iii) goes back to step (i)." "Startup lore is that most people will bias heavily towards iteration when they should just start over." "Having an ego is expensive. For us, it was better value to throw the majority of our ideas away." (How we made something people want; credits Dalton, a YC partner, for the start-over idea.)

- **Local vs global maxima in pivoting.** *Worked example:* the technical-debt survey tool had real traction (600 users three weeks into YC, 50% survey response rate) but teams would not pay: "although it was a nice way to log issues, it just wasn't helping solve the problem." "The hardest pivot to make was when we had the most traction." Staying would have meant "we'd still be struggling with something we weren't enjoying working on." (A story about pivots.)

- **Founder-problem fit.** *Worked example:* after the survey tool they concluded "we just weren't the right people to run this business" because neither had managed engineering teams. Idea 5 "didn't come from us, which doomed it before it even really started" and lasted 5 days. Pattern: "the thing that connected all of them was that we tackled problems we'd experienced in our previous professional lives." (A story about pivots.)

- **Product<>Community fit before Product<>Market fit.** Open source splits the SaaS jump: build something useful, then work out how to make money. *Worked example:* ~60% of users picked the hosted version to try it; investors "generally just have the belief that product<>market fit is easy once you get product{<>}community fit." Advice: "get something to ubiquity before anything else." Named risks: run out of cash first, build a free product with no commercial route, market too small. (How we raised $3M.)

- **Transparency kills politics, and has a "fewer places to look" rule.** *Worked example:* "Not only does it help build context across teams, but it also cuts down on meetings, and speeds up decision making. It even reduces politics – you can't be sneaky if everything is public!" (Product management is broken.) Overload fix: "The simplest way to keep things easy to find, is to have fewer places to look," with the hierarchy "Pull Requests > Issues > Public Slack Channels > Private groups or Slack DMs > Email". (How to run a transparent startup.) Homework metaphor: "it's like having someone else mark your homework." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Transparency inventory, with a deliberate exception.** Shared: why fundraising, term sheet goals, every employee's ownership, monthly accountant reports, OKRs, board slides and notes, hiring decisions, performance-driven firing reasons, holidays. Not shared: individual pay, "the only area we don't want employee feedback" because "if people influence each others' pay, then it would make the process of giving and receiving direct feedback harder." (How to run a transparent startup.)

- **No deadlines and no roadmaps, because information changes.** "if you start building something and you get more information later on that you should be building something else, great. You should change." He will not hold people to what seemed relevant six weeks earlier. And: "we don't want an engineer to have to go through any kind of process or to get any kind of approval to build something... we blindly back you as a product engineer." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) See Blind spots for the 2024 "Deadlines are motivating" tension.

- **Zero-context team change by Slack.** *Worked example:* conventional change takes "like 7 hours" of one-on-ones and "a week and a half", "effectively a culture of politics." His route: a Slack post: "I'm proposing that we split this team in half. And I think this person should be the new boss of the new team." "This is a zero context, no warning note." Result: "it takes like 10 minutes to make a team change." He names the risk: "it feels a little bit cult-like." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Team lead is not a promotion.** Same pay, sometimes less, because pay tied to structure would block restructuring in a "hypergrowth sort of company... changing our structure over and over again." Reluctant managers are "the best kind, at least in engineering"; curious engineers try it and "in 6 months... they hate it and they want to swap back." "One of the ways to not get hired at PostHog is to say, I want to manage loads of people." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Founder as temporary manager for zero-to-one teams.** *Worked example:* he joined the AI team telling the lead "I'm proposing that I'm your boss for the next few weeks," then hands it back. Rule: join teams in "a more existential mode"; "once the team's in like a state where we have like thousands of customers and stuff, I'm just not as good at looking after it." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Span-of-control inflation (clarity vs transparency).** *Worked example:* "In the early days, we had zero direct or indirect reports each, at 15 people, we had roughly 8 people per exec. Today, it's around 35. By the next big offsite, it will be around 80." Polished, clear comms cost time and "is less transparent"; the fix: "We'll reduce the level of polish and increase the level of transparency (and mess, at times)." Perceived change in communication is a function of distance, not volume. (CEO diary: communication.)

- **Evidence-based hiring: watch people do the work.** *Worked examples:* Marius, found via a hobby project on Hacker News, got contract work first; Lottie, found on Twitter, did paid contract work; a senior infra hire was sourced by checking GitHub repo stars. The handbook was written before hiring anyone, as a self-selection filter. Named practice: the paid **SuperDay**; by 2025, $500 to $1,000 for a day of real work, "1 in 4 people get through the Superday", and "There's remarkable difference between what candidates say and what they do." By 2023: "Over time, we've refined this down to a one-day task", and "I think 90%+ of an organization's problems are solved by hiring right." (First five employees; TL&W #189 transcript; How we designed our company for speed.)
- **Fire fast, but re-hire fast too.** In 2021 they let people go quickly with above-market severance, but "when things didn't work out, we often didn't re-hire for the role quickly, or at all. We got scar tissue! We must've been hiring for the role in the first place for a reason." The cost was lost time. Design hiring followed advice from Malthe Sigurdsson (who ran design at Stripe): "Great designers iterate quickly" and "Hire generalists early on"; candidates pushed back that they "were unicorn hunting, and it took months, but we'd do everything the same way all over again." (CEO diary: Things I learned last year; CEO diary: How come your website is so nice?)

- **Cofounder relationship is the #1 failure mode, managed deliberately.** "Most startups fail because the cofounders break up – more so than failing to find product-market fit." *Worked examples:* 50/50 equity and equal salaries; a weekly 1-hour meeting; a five-year shared doc, "good stuff" first; co-CEO titles that match reality: "A CEO is perceived as being 'in charge' of the CTO, but do you actually work like this?" He admits the alignment was luck: "We didn't talk about these things before we started working together, we just got lucky." (How to not break up with your cofounder.)

- **Grow the pie, don't bargain over it.** "When I wanted to get Tim to join me on the PostHog adventure, my personal goal was not to try to maximize the fraction of the pie I receive. It was to build a pie so damn big that everyone wins." Breakup risk falls "when you focus on building a successful company, not on bargaining with each other." (CEO diary: co-founder.)

- **Company pre-mortem at an offsite.** Teams brainstorm "what is most likely to kill the company." *Worked example:* at an offsite an unexpectedly large number of employees listed cofounder breakup: "What surprised me was the number of people that listed cofounder breakup as a potential cause of failure." That surfaced the employees' fear model, not the CEO's, and triggered the post. (CEO diary: co-founder.)

- **Annual role audit via an exec 360.** Five questions asked about each other, such as "What do I do that you experience as world-class?" and "What do I do where I appear to be experiencing fun, peace or joy". *Worked example:* it led Tim and James to swap: "Tim (my co-founder) and I realized we were managing the wrong teams because the game had changed." "I was good at early sales, but we've moved on from that and now he's the right person to help us scale things there." (CEO diary: 2022.)

- **Structural fix over willpower.** *Worked example:* Hawkins neglected company vision and OKRs while doing IC work; Tim held him accountable and they hired an executive assistant: "I felt really silly hiring someone to manage my time and to keep me organized, but the reality is that I can now give more value to the company, which helps everyone." (CEO diary: 2022.)

- **Zoom out: solve the principle, not the instance.** *Worked example:* when a valued employee asked for a sabbatical they asked whether sabbaticals fit the company generally and decided against them even at the cost of losing someone. In 2025 he describes the mechanism: the request becomes "what is our principle for how we handle sabbatical requests?", is published in the handbook, and the link is sent, so decisions are "consistent as a company" or changed "intentional rather than accidental." (How to not break up with your cofounder; TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Inflated risk: perceived risk of quitting exceeds actual risk.** "We would spend a set amount of our savings and time, and if it didn't work out, we were fortunate enough to know we'd probably be able to get another job." He flags privilege: "That takes a lot of privilege because of years working in tech." He sold a Jaguar XE for a used Ford Mondeo: "It was pleasing not feeling like my car was compensating for some insecurity or another." (Inflated risk seems riskier.)

- **Pricing as a product, with an owner.** "A product without an owner is as good as dead. Put someone – or a team of someones – in charge of your pricing," and experiment: "We tested a more detailed feature comparison on our billing upgrade page and saw an over 30% improvement in conversion to paid." (Pricing like a product, Raquel Smith house view.) His own version: "Think of your pricing like a product - you want users to become paying customers." (Pricing lessons, founders.)

- **Pricing sequence: learn first, remove sales later.** *Worked example:* "Last summer, we worked on finding just five reference customers for our paid product" with a $2k/month minimum ("We simply couldn't support more than this"), non-transparent at first "so we could speak to some users to figure out the right model first", transparent "as soon as we've reasonable confidence", self-serve after "a reasonable formula." The 2021 verdict: "Self-serve was way better than we thought it would be," once pricing was transparent, docs were complete and annual minimums were gone; "we incentivized users to add their card (more free usage)." (CEO diary: Things I learned last year.) Final ladder: free-for-life tier, paid features "starting at $0" with a card (over half of users enter one), no minimum spend, no annual contract unless requested. (Pricing lessons, founders.)

- **Cost-plus pricing with a modest margin; never "price to value."** "We should charge as little as possible while remaining margin positive. We don't believe in loss-leaders, or squeezing customers for the maximum they're willing to pay" (what he calls "pricing to value"). "We don't believe in loss-leaders, so we make a modest positive margin on each event sold, but what we charge is directly connected to what it costs us, not what we think we can get away with charging." *Worked example:* 25k session recordings a month fell from $430 to $85. (We've decided to make less money, Parts 1 and 2.)

- **Deliberately cannibalize when price should follow cost.** *Worked example:* splitting identified events (person profiles in Postgres, "one of the most complex and expensive steps in our ingestion pipeline") from anonymous events (cheaper ClickHouse tables) cut per-event prices 10% to 80%. "We'll see a ~30% decline in product analytics revenue from the change, but it will unlock several new use cases, such as web analytics at massive scale." Moat logic: "Most of our competitors are inefficient. They employ huge outbound sales teams." (Part 2.)

- **Logarithmically declining price for the biggest customers.** "Your biggest customers won't appreciate being gouged as their usage increases." Rule: "every time your volume goes up 10x, the incremental price decreases 5x." Counter-check for large contracts: "If it takes just one extra engineer to self build in a large company, you theoretically should be able to charge >$100K annually." (Pricing lessons, founders.)

- **Charge per product.** Five claimed benefits: usage rose when charging started, paying customers improve the free product, team autonomy, sustainable price competition, exponential top-of-funnel. *Worked example:* session replay began as abuse: "a handful of high volume users would send zero events, but would use our JavaScript snippet to do just session replay." "We charged for session recording in December. By March, it was 10% of our revenue." His own caveat: "we had to stop people abusing recording," so the strategic framing is partly retrospective. (Multi-product pricing.)

- **Get in first, then be the default.** Target the engineer who builds the MVP: "It's the technical co-founder and early engineers building the MVP and integrating the first product tools, not PMs. By focusing on engineers we can get in first, and later become the default choice for each additional tool they add." *Worked example:* session recording is adopted earlier than a CDP, and K8s support was sunsetted to avoid enterprise pull. (Our simpler goal.)

- **Founder-led sales: be the quarterback.** "You are the best salesperson for your product. There will never be anyone as well qualified to sell your product." Rules: respond within 30 seconds; open with a three-point agenda; "Don't go straight into a demo"; "Don't invite your co-founder"; "You're the quarterback of the deal." *Worked example:* "Our first enterprise customer at PostHog miraculously started just under the budget at which more stakeholders needed to get involved in the deal!" (How to do startup sales with no experience.) The 2021 evidence: to land the first five reference customers they changed pricing model, buyer, sales process, trials, demos, support, payments and the free/paid split, and "A founder will have the easiest time trying to make changes fast." The trade he accepted: "It was more important we'd bottleneck not getting other work done... than not to learn how to sell at all." (CEO diary: Things I learned last year.)

- **Pricing bands by annual contract value.** Up to $5k is "Below the amount anyone cares about"; $5k-$10k is "Something one person can buy"; $10k-$30k "Something two or three people can buy"; $30K-$100K+ "Quite hard to move forward"; $500K+ "Very hard to move forward." Discovery question: "is this a decision you can take on your own? Does your buying process change depending on the value?" (How to do startup sales with no experience.)

- **Focus on controllable inputs, not outputs.** "Having the conviction to focus on controllable inputs — such as a better experience for developers — is the best long-term way to achieve the outputs you need." (Series B announcement.) Operationalized weekly: "at the start of the week be like okay by the end of this week what is the list of things we will feel good if we get them all done." (YC Podcast, auto-caption.)

- **Leading-indicator board dashboard.** "We want to focus on optimizing leading indicator metrics, as high up the funnel as possible. That means you have a faster feedback loop for learning." Revenue moved to an appendix; "If it starts diverging, look for different leading indicators." Indicators: quality sign-ups matching the ICP, conversion to paid, deployment conversion, discoveries inside PostHog. (I used to think you don't need product people.)

- **Making teams feel like teams in an async company.** *Worked example:* meetings banned on Tuesdays and Thursdays; "Features got shipped fast, yet everything felt calm. We grew a lot." But after six months they hired a house in Tuscany for a week and it changed the product trajectory (it gave them the idea for apps and session recording). "Zoom is just too close to feeling like a work meeting compared to getting dinner or going for a walk." Policy: annual offsite, $300/month co-working, $500/month travel and meals to meet colleagues. (Remote companies can be too asynchronous.)

- **LLM-limits model and the AI chapter.** Three gaps: "LLMs have no innate goal setting," "AI is spiky," "LLMs don't self-improve." *Worked example:* a Clippy-esque one-shot chat assistant made mistakes: "FOMO got the better of me and I pushed this in our marketing too early." The fix was "a single query loop architecture, so the agent could iterate", plus regular reviews of real LLM traces; then "Last week, the majority of dashboards were created by agents." Bets: "We think PostHog's future is headless"; reactive to proactive; long-running agents. (PostHog's next chapter.)

- **Co-CEO split by depth vs breadth.** "I wanted to go full founder mode and push the ambition of the AI work we were already doing." Tim "wanted to go wide and improve how we execute across everything: hiring, ops, finance, go-to-market, and engineering." Earlier, on the podcast: Tim is "clearer, more direct... a stronger communicator", James "better at just pushing up the level of ambition." (PostHog's next chapter; TL&W #189 transcript.)

## Decision heuristics

- **When in doubt, ship it.** Handbook value "Why not now?": "You do not need consensus to do things – focus your energy on shipping what's most valuable." / "If we're debating doing something, just trying it is the best way to learn." The midwit meme: "idiot and genius do the same behavior and the middle one overthinks things... It's like just try it." (Handbook: values; Executive Brand, auto-caption.)
- **Judge by what people do, not what they say.** The say/do gap is the reason to build before validating (the 15-prospect, one-click story). "If you can't stop thinking of other ideas, you probably are building something you don't like." (A story about pivots.)
- **Pivot-cooling rule.** "If you decide to pivot, you have to wait at least until the next day to do it." They could build "an MVP and a new website in a single, glorious, all nighter", which "felt like progress, but in reality was just a bit dangerous." Also "Know where you sit on this line": they leaned toward pivoting too often. (A story about pivots.)
- **If retention needs nudges, the product isn't valuable.** "Avoid relying on things like reminder emails or push notifications purely designed to get users to log back into your product. If it takes this sort of effort to retain users, your product probably isn't valuable to them." (PMF game.)
- **Define the ICP precisely, then listen only to it.** "Do not just assume one or two things like 'engineers in startups with over 100 staff'." (PMF game.) "You should focus completely on your ICP. That's the point. Everything else comes from it." (How we found our ICP.)
- **Respond in seconds; ask in two or three sentences.** "Be as concise as you can when asking for a meeting. That means 2-3 sentences – not a wall of text." / "Speed matters. You should aim to respond within 30 seconds if someone messages you back." (PMF game.) Sales version: "We had a 90% greater chance of booking time if we called back potential customers within 5 minutes." (Startup sales.)
- **Charge early unless you're a network-effects consumer play.** "Unless you're building Facebook, you're always better off charging something as soon as possible." (Pricing advice, house view.) Qualified by competition: if "no one is doing your thing... it's like much more likely that no one will pay for it," charge sooner. (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) His own context-sensitive version: "Had we been building into a new market with less validation from competitors, a worse fundraising environment (like right now), or bootstrapping, then monetizing sooner would have been important." (First 1,000 users.)
- **Cut anything that isn't fun, because "the easiest way to fail is to simply lose interest."** They abandoned self-hosted monetization because the sales and infra debugging were not enjoyable. (Pragmatic lessons from Elon.) "Had we stuck with the original plan, we'd have lost interest because we weren't doing what we enjoy." (How not to be boring.)
- **Optimize for your strongest performers**, not the average. (Pragmatic lessons from Elon.)
- **Don't do measurable marketing.** "it's exactly because you can measure it that it gets saturated and you don't stand out and that's bad marketing in my opinion." No retargeting "from a brand perspective." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) See Blind spots for the tension with measured growth experiments.
- **Self-qualify your own advice.** The no-PM and no-deadline model gets easier "the closer you are to being a dev tool"; going all-in on one open-source project is "very bad advice for something that doesn't have competition that's making lots of revenue. So be very careful." Likewise: "This worked for us, but it isn't universally good advice." (Executive Brand; First 1,000 users.)
- **Do more of what's working.** "In go-to-market, it's always tempting to explore new channels. However, in 2022, we really benefited from understanding where our growth was actually coming from, and doing more of those things." (CEO diary: 2022; Nail Self Serve: "So we should do more of what's working.")
- **Growth team over outbound team.** "If we had hired a sales team, I guarantee we'd be focusing on how to send more cold emails more effectively versus the more leveraged work of improving our onboarding, activation and billing flows." One activation experiment: "one experiment has led to a 25% increase in our monthly growth, forever." (CEO diary: 2022.)
- **Set customer-success targets on inputs.** One KR is "volume of deals in our target range per week - rather than trying to hit an overall revenue figure." (Nail Self Serve.)
- **Sort customers by speed-to-close.** "Some customers are very fast (a couple of weeks, or less) to sell to, with minimal interaction, whereas others take months and are very intense." (Nail Self Serve.) Also track the anti-ICP: "Don't just monitor deals that close, but track those that are dragging on." (VC is a tool, not a religion.)
- **Duplication is cheap, coordination is expensive.** "it's kind of like saying, oh, if I buy a Porsche, there's no point because a bird might poo on it"; just wipe it off. Mitigations: a demo section every all-hands and exec matchmaking; "There's only been like a couple of times where we've sort of built the same thing twice." (TL&W #189 transcript.)
- **Guidance, not mandates.** The handbook gives engineers a method and a quarterly list, "this is just guidance. Like, you can ignore it if you think it's stupid." Failure rule: if an engineer keeps building off-guidance things and "their ideas are all failing, they are too rogue to work here effectively." (TL&W #189 transcript.)
- **Validation is a big-company disease.** "validate your ideas upfront" is "a cultural thing from really risk-averse, extremely large companies that might spend like $20 billion on some new thing"; in a startup, "we've just had clearer lessons still when we've just built something... in a way where we're happy to throw it away." (TL&W #189 transcript.)
- **Better to build the better version of X.** "For non-God tier product people, such as me, I think it's easier to build a better version of X than it is to create something entirely new." Also "Humans overcorrect by default" and "process is scar tissue" (a team member's phrase). (How we designed our company for speed.)
- **Bias toward experienced people for all-remote autonomy**, and admit the gap in growing early-career people. (How we designed our company for speed.)
- **Spend money to save time only if you have capital.** The $2K Twitter ads were bought to avoid waiting: "This step wasn't necessary, so please don't copy it if you have no capital." (How we made something people want.)
- **Act on every piece of feedback**, "If we didn't do this, then users would stop giving us feedback and we'd die!" Slack beat email: "we saw 20x the response rate of email." (How we made something people want.)
- **Outreach only before PMF.** "This was the only time we had to do outreach at PostHog. Once we had product market fit, this need disappeared." Channel choice: "WhatsApp / SMS > Email > LinkedIn, depending on if I had each person's contact details." (How we made something people want.)
- **Use outside experts at the inflection point, with conditions.** Access without a warm intro works if "you've done your research, you're clearly at a major inflection point, and you're not trying to get a sale or investment from them." (A story about pivots.)

## Positions by decision type

- **Pivot vs. persevere.** Strongly pro-pivot early: "Since most startups fail, you're probably doing something wrong if you don't pivot!" But "Fractional pivots are dangerous." (PMF game; What we've learned about PMF, house view.) Counterweight from his own pivots: "We stopped working on several ideas that probably could have worked out, if we'd been willing to persist." (A story about pivots.) On the PostHog idea: "What we didn't do was like sit cross-legged on the top of a mountain waiting for inspiration... we had to just build a bunch of things to find the thing we're excited about." (YC Podcast, auto-caption.) Hard test: "If you're pushing this hard and getting nowhere, you don't have the magic of product market fit." (A story about pivots.)

- **How long to give a pivot test.** A one-month plan with a pre-committed kill: "If we failed to complete all four in a month, we would pivot again." They worked "from 9am to anywhere between midnight and 3am, 7 days a week," reached "300 deployments within a couple of days – five weeks after we started building," and he drew the lesson "Deadlines are motivating." (First 1,000 users.) He later adds: "I'd stress we had a hard 4-week deadline we'd set ourselves." (How we made something people want.) See Blind spots for the tension with no-deadline culture.

- **How to price.** Usage-based, transparent, generous free tier, no "talk to sales." "Hobbyists or pre-PMF startups should be able to use PostHog for free" and "Be more generous than competitors, making it a no-brainer." (Pricing advice, house view.) "Match the cheapest for each individual product" (Handbook: how we make money). Over-simplified pricing "limits your ability to charge users for the value they receive." "Usage is a leap of faith... me kind of showing that I believe that you will find this product useful." (Churn.FM.) Self-critique: "that's probably something I would change. Like I might I would have charged a little bit sooner." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.)

- **Who not to monetize.** "Trying to get money out of tiny startups would have been a mistake," because "We started by focussing on startups but realized our lowest churn teams were working on products that already had product market fit." (Pricing lessons, founders.)

- **When to charge for an open-source product.** In 2020 his stance was ubiquity first: "We'd advocate that the best way to build a really ambitious, fundable open source project is to get something to ubiquity before anything else." Rationale five years on: "none of the multi-billion dollar open-source companies I researched monetized in the first five years." Then the caveat that it "isn't universally good advice." (How we raised $3M; First 1,000 users.) In 2023 he says pre-announcing the paid tier on the pricing page from launch makes later charging not a betrayal (his paraphrased lesson), and "Charging customers for a product led to more usage." (VC is a tool, not a religion.)

- **Raise vs. bootstrap (2020 to 2023, evolving).** 2020: choose VC for ambition: "when we're 80, we think we want to have taken a couple of big swings with our careers even if we don't own the whole pie." "Going for VC means you are committing to an exit or a failure." (How we raised $3M.) 2022, pro-VC: "Since PostHog raised Venture Capital (VC), we've had far less stress, and far more fun." "We decided it was more stressful to bootstrap than to raise VC." (Raising money is less stressful; VC is a tool.) Early on he judged bootstrapping "slow and dull for us" and capital "would always be a frustrating constraint." (Inflated risk.) He wants VC money with bootstrap values: "we're a VC backed company we have a lot of like the values of a bootstrap company" (Executive Brand.) 2023, restraint: "We raised over $27,000,000 then realized we should be growing out of our own revenue instead."

- **Why VC is a tool, not a religion.** Hype loop: founder raises, VC sees social proof, users pick tech featured in TechCrunch, founder feels not raising "stupid." PostHog's seed was March 2020, Series A a month later, B about six months after, ending in "dark ages" of messy hiring. Developer math: "Normal companies probably should raise venture capital, since most grow by hiring a big sales team whilst the product tries to keep up. PostHog's main growth lever is shipping." Valid purposes: working capital, employee secondaries, an ideal board member. Bad reasons: "You want to hire people to find product market fit for you" / "You want to hire people to do the early sales for you" / "You want to raise money because of your ego". (VC is a tool; Raising money is less stressful.)

- **Fundraising mechanics (2020 seed).** A 157-row investor sheet, ~200 calls of ~45 minutes in ~6 weeks, ~30 rejections (mostly "too early"), $3.025M closed with offers up to $5.5M, which they declined. SAFEs at a $12M post-money cap rising to $15M; planned $20M but did not because of covid recession risk. A $10k flip to a US parent: UK investors guessed ~$5M post-money against $15M in the US. Pipeline learning after four weeks: investors with open-source portfolios closed at an "astronomically higher" rate. Cold outreach failed; "investors who made the effort to reach out to us through another investor did end up making an offer." (How we raised $3M.)

- **How to pitch.** Be certain and opinionated: "If you act meek and ask investors to make up the price, you'll land very low. Go in high and you'll polarize people - which is what you need… a definite yes or definite no!" Seed-round confession in 2025: "we sucked at pitching. We're trying to people please I think too much" (about 160 firms met); it turned when "we started getting much more opinionated... Hey, like you're out of your mind if you think we're going to go big on like hiring a big sales team." (How we raised $3M; YC Podcast, auto-caption.) "just bear in mind the reason for rejection may not be the real reason." Do not stop for a crisis: "We promised ourselves that we would not use the virus as a reason to not raise."

- **Fundraising rules, 2020 and 2025.** In 2025: "be really really certain... have an opinion," raise from West Coast US investors, and get warm intros from angels, since cold "outbound to investors which I think is like generally losing exercise." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) Same lesson in 2020: their one cold message was ignored. (How we raised $3M.)

- **Division of labor in a raise.** "I focused exclusively on the fundraising process, and Tim focused exclusively on building the product." Usage doubling mid-raise helped, and he concludes "It would have been much harder if we were solo founders." Also admits network privilege: "I imagine this is quite socially harmful - if your network is weaker and further from the most decisive VCs, you will have a harder time raising money for your company." (How we raised $3M.)

- **Revenue over fundraising when capacity-constrained.** In late 2021, paid-product demand and investor interest arrived together just as a serious family health matter took his time (he has written about it publicly; the details are personal and left out here). "We picked revenue." (What motivates me.) He chose the path that needed less of his own time rather than running a raise and a sales push at once.
- **Personal crisis as a forcing function, not a pause.** During that family health matter he framed the next board meeting as a refusal to let it shrink the company's ambitions for anyone involved (CEO diary, Nov 2021; personal details omitted here). Remote work was what made it workable: "I was grateful that I was lucky enough to work from home and be present during these periods." The same diary already shows the input-metric habit: "Long term revenue is the output but we want to look upstream for a metric that is more controllable, with a faster feedback loop," via a lead-scoring algorithm on every deployment. On the revenue choice, later: "now a year and a half on, I'm very glad that's what we picked." They also declined hyped 2021 rounds: "we had demand, but ultimately chose not to move forward." Target: default alive and ~$10M ARR before raising again. (What motivates me; CEO diary: 2022; VC is a tool: "I felt that if I tried to sell and fundraise, I'd do both badly.")

- **The Series D.** It traced to Patrick Collison's out-of-the-blue tweet; they asked for a meeting and "just talked about a bunch of topics that were on our mind," then: "We got really excited about working together." A later round was easy: "perversely. It's easier... we spoke to one investor for this round." The platform bet: become "upstream of every piece of software they'll ever buy." (Crunchbase; Series D; YC Podcast.) Tension with 2023 restraint is recorded in Blind spots.

- **When to hire a sales team.** For developers, essentially never. "I can't think of a worse group to target this way (this is coming from a place of experience as I've run outbound sales teams before)." "Nearly all our sales are self-serve and 70% of our customers find us through word-of-mouth growth." (CEO diary: 2022; Handbook: low prices.) Exception stated in 2020 in house "we" voice: "operating a B2B company without a Sales team does become impractical at a large scale." And a 2024 footnote concedes the company later built a sales and customer-success team, with the golden rule "the person who closes a deal also manages that relationship long term."

- **Founders are the best sellers.** "A founder is, regardless of experience, the best person in the world to sell the product they made." (PMF game.) The 2024 restatement and its rules are under Mental models: founder-led sales.

- **When a startup should do sales anyway.** Founder-led and pre-PMF: "You have one job as a founder: get to product-market fit. Outsourcing your sales will make this harder." even "if you think you'll be product-led eventually, it may be better to start with a sales-led motion, so you can learn more about your early prospects"; self-serve "would have made finding product-market fit more difficult" in the early days. Key rule: "In sales meetings, you are aiming to close down objections, and steer the customer... In product meetings, you want to let users meander." Existing customers first: "Looking after your existing customers should be your number one priority." (How to do startup sales.)

- **How to get first meetings.** Easiest to hardest: friends and family; people you've worked with; social posts; customized LinkedIn DMs; customized cold emails; posting where users hang out. Did not work: events ("I found it awkward, but that might be a me thing") and automated outreach. (How to do startup sales.) His own first users: friends, family, the phone book, LinkedIn connections and "every developer I'd ever worked with via LinkedIn", hand-written, "All messages were totally customized to the person. Nothing automated." (How we made something people want.)

- **Who to sell to.** Engineers, not sales leaders: "building for sales people or sales leaders in particular, I feel the signal to noise ratio is pretty poor." Confirmed by deals: "if it's an engineer leading this stuff, it gets better implemented. It sells way faster." (YC Podcast; Churn.FM.) Principle: "The best products are generally built for end users." and "we want an engineer in any size organization on Earth to be able to use our software in production without getting up from their desk." (Nail Self Serve.)

- **When to go enterprise.** Last. "Long term, we think the future of our revenue will come from large enterprise. We're a natural fit... However, we want to avoid needing to go through complex purchasing decisions whilst there are easier ways to increase our numbers." (Nail Self Serve.) Context: self-serve deals up to ~$70k closed in weeks; paid products averaged 20% month over month growth for six months.

- **When to hire product people.** After PMF on the open-source product, when no one can hold every user in their heads, and when engineers ask. Before that, founders and engineers do it. (I used to think you don't need product people.) Result he cites: ">8x increase in traffic", ">4x increase in users", ">2x increase in contributors".

- **How to define strategy.** One objective at a time, in two words (Nail X). In February 2023: "Today, engineers building the products you use are too often treated as a resource. They don't get a say in what gets built, they just pick up ticket after ticket." He lists ticket work as "Endless alignment", "Endless requirements gathering", "Endless handoffs". (Our simpler goal.)

- **When to fire.** Decisively but humanely, and visibly: "our number one goal if we let someone go is that they see it coming"; "It's a team, not a family." (Executive Brand, auto-caption.) The "team, rather than a family" framing enables swift performance-based exits, balanced by "Paying generous severance (four months) to people we let go." The bargain: "we pay 4 months severance because our attitude is letting people go earlier is better for everyone and a big payout for them is kinder in return." Sharing why: "we received positive feedback from our team when we explained why we made these decisions, transparently." (Pragmatic lessons from Elon; CEO diary: 2022.) He holds this partly because of VC: "I doubt we'd have made that call if we were bootstrapped. We simply think it's cheaper to spend money here than to have an underperformer stay for longer because we feel bad." (Raising money is less stressful.)

- **Focus vs. diversify.** A striking exception to focus dogma: 16-17 products because "a human product manager has product market fit... we set off trying to build basically a product manager that's a physical thing." (YC Podcast.) "our most impactful ever idea was going from one product to 2 products. And that came from an individual developer having the freedom to just build what he wanted." (TL&W #189 transcript.) He concedes failures: the surveys product, "we got a little bit arrogant... got hardly any revenue from it. We got enough traction that we can't drop it." (Executive Brand.) Origin of multi-product: "It taught us that consolidating multiple tools into the platform made us much more valuable to users." "Code won." (How we made something people want.)

- **Strategic choices from who the team is.** "We sometimes make business decisions based on who we are, and what we want to do – not always what may, for example, generate the most revenue growth." They removed the paid self-hosted offering because "we soon realized we just love shipping new features." (How not to be boring.) Wrong assumption admitted: "We also believed that our paid self-hosted product would be 90%+ of our revenue... (although that later turned out not to be true...)." (Raising money is less stressful.)

- **When to open-source.** MIT license "to see if enterprises would use it" and to signal transparency: "We figured our target audience would appreciate the transparency, and the ability to inspect our code." Framing matters more than mechanics: "it's open source product analytics then it's not just about it being your infrastructure it's also just like a whole vibe." (First 1,000 users; YC Podcast.) Monetization menu: services are low margin, hosted risks a cloud provider competing, open core is the long-run pick with a hard rule to be upfront about what is paid. (How we raised $3M.)

- **When to launch.** Gated on strangers finding it useful unaided: the Hacker News launch was ready when "users could sign up and use the product without our help" and "people we didn't know signed up and found it useful." "Don't invest in a big launch until you're certain users will stick." "Going self-serve too soon will reduce your opportunities to learn." Early users: "In fact, we had to create new users by manually editing the database." (First 1,000 users.) "You don't need hundreds of users to validate an idea – a dozen or so who use your product over and over again is enough."

- **Who to target first.** "We chose developers because (i) established competitors were focused on product managers and selling to execs, and (ii) it was an audience we understood and enjoyed working with." (First 1,000 users.) Content as channel for a non-coder: "I'm not good at coding, so figured I could help out this way." "developers wouldn't appreciate being sold to, but I guessed they would find following our journey interesting."

- **Retention vs. revenue.** Retention wins and pays: "if you just do the right thing for customers and have faith... don't try and, like optimize for your revenue number all of the time, ironically, you will make more money." (Churn.FM.) *Worked example:* session-replay users churned from bill shock past ~$5k/mo, so the team built billing controls that lowered order values but raised retention. (Executive Brand.) Support cost admitted: improvements caused "a 75% drop in the likelihood of each user to need support, but that was outweighed by how much growth we had." (CEO diary: 2022.)

- **How to hire.** The public handbook means no outbound recruiting and "thousands of applicants for every job"; both co-founders interview every hire; technical interview is "a chat"; paid SuperDay. Prefers slightly frontend-oriented engineers ("if you have a hammer, everything looks like a nail"); hires ex-founders who miss the craft. Signal: people who "notice when the apartment needs to be vacuumed" and who take accountability. (TL&W #189 transcript; Executive Brand.) Reduces to "raw engineering talent" plus accountability.

- **How to retain people.** Honest answer: "it's one of the main reasons that more people leave PostHog to start a company than to join another company. By quite a way." Mitigations: distribution, rising valuation, regular secondaries: "you don't own the whole pie, you own a slice of it, but the pie is growing extremely quickly." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **How to write for developers.** Be opinionated (never write "Ultimately, it's up to you."), but do not be dismissive: "You should never alienate your own audience. Be kind." "Whatever you do, make sure you actually get something out. You can't improve something that doesn't exist." Disclaimer: "If you work at Top Down Enterprise Technology Corp, just do the opposite of what I say." (Writing for developers.)

- **How to run remote.** Async from day one: "we realized we got more work done when we worked asynchronously." Meetings need "a clear purpose, notes and action items." Add deliberate informal contact early: "don't wait too long to initiate those links. It could be the difference between success and failure." (Remote companies can be too asynchronous.)

- **When the ground shifts (AI).** Rethink "core UX, positioning, marketing," accept short-term UX flux and apologize: "These are big bets and our UX will be in flux as a result for a while. I'm sorry if the changes here are aggravating." Split founder roles by where each wants to go deep or wide. (PostHog's next chapter.)

- **Communication as the CEO scales.** "As we've grown, I've repeatedly underestimated how sharply the importance of communication from the exec team increases." The all-hands is the key artifact: "if you did nothing else, just do a good job of the all-hands and that'd be great." "If I don't have the depth to really nail a topic, I will get other people involved." Exec team of five at ~150 people, no "gazillion managers." (CEO diary: communication.)

## Voice & style

Blunt, self-deprecating, and allergic to corporate register. He swears mildly in writing ("more bullshit," "it all goes to hell") and reaches for games and physical metaphors: PMF is "snakes and ladders," scaling "feels like we're playing computer game. We've just unlocked like rocket launchers," a Porsche a bird might poo on (duplication), a "benefit sandwich with a light feature filling," "You're the parent of your startup," a fundraise "like wrestling a bear, you're done when the bear is done." He states an inversion as a flat rule before defending it (give away more to make more money; double the traffic at half the conversion rate and he'd take it; the real risk is that "no one cares"). He is unusually **self-qualifying for a founder**: advice is context-dependent ("the closer you are to being a dev tool the easier it is"; "isn't universally good advice"; "I haven't covered every possible scenario"), and decisions are filed under mistakes ("we got a little bit arrogant"; "we sucked at pitching"; "FOMO got the better of me"; "I wish we had run this exercise sooner").

He teaches through concrete company numbers (15 prospects and one click, 157 investor rows, ~200 calls, 300 deployments in days, $2K on Twitter, four-month severance, 30% conversion lift, $430 to $85) rather than abstractions. He cites and then qualifies influences: endorses The Mom Test but says it gives "a false sense of security"; borrows from Elon but rejects crunch; agrees with Netflix's "team not family" but "I don't agree with everything about how they work." British idiom shows ("whilst") and he admits "being British means I wrestle with US English." Short imperatives in essays ("Be opinionated," "Go weirdly hard."). Humor is load-bearing: "Would you rather fight one horse-sized hedgehog, or 100 hedgehog-sized horses?" appears as a section title in a pricing post; he is candid he cannot fully separate strategy from the fact that he finds shitposting fun. On podcasts he talks in long, run-on, hedged sentences ("like," "kind of"); in essays he is clipped and list-driven.

## Where they disagree

Rows below give Hawkins's position from his own texts; the other advisor's position is stated from their general, well-known public stance, not from this corpus.

- **vs. Jason Lemkin, on sales-led SaaS GTM.** Lemkin's canon is hire a VP of Sales early and build an outbound machine. Hawkins runs the opposite company: "PostHog doesn't do any outbound sales at all" (Churn.FM), because developers cannot be cold-sold (Handbook). He told seed investors "you're out of your mind if you think we're going to go big on like hiring a big sales team." The tension is bounded: he came from enterprise sales and concedes "operating a B2B company without a Sales team does become impractical at a large scale" (2020 house essay), and PostHog later built a sales and CS team.

- **vs. Ben Horowitz, on the role of product managers and management.** Horowitz's "Good Product Manager/Bad Product Manager" casts the PM as "CEO of the product" owning the roadmap. Hawkins declares "Product management is broken," refused to hire PMs for years, then reversed: PMs help find the biggest problems but "product doesn't dictate to engineers by producing feature requirements." Where Horowitz sees management as the cure for organizational dysfunction, Hawkins sees the PM layer as often created by it.

- **vs. Elon Musk, on artificial deadlines and crunch.** Hawkins wrote an essay borrowing from Musk ("Be in the details, not the day-to-day"; "Take fun seriously") but carves out where it does not transfer: artificial deadlines work only for "large orgs with younger teams willing to work unsustainable hours." (Pragmatic lessons from Elon.) His own 2024 essay, however, praises a hard four-week deadline and a 9am to 3am sprint.

- **vs. Reed Hastings, on "team not family."** He agrees with the Netflix thesis (generous severance, high performance) but "I don't agree with everything about how they work, but this part I thought was quite interesting." (Executive Brand.) He adds radical transparency and no-managers, which Netflix goes less far on.

- **vs. Jeff Bezos, on metrics.** Both love small teams (two-pizza teams versus a cap of six). Bezos is mechanism- and metrics-driven; Hawkins says the best marketing is what you cannot measure: "it's exactly because you can measure it that it gets saturated." His growth team nonetheless runs measured experiments; see Blind spots.

- **vs. Paul Graham / Michael Seibel (YC), on validation vs. velocity.** Large agreement (talk to users, do things that don't scale, hand-onboarding). Friction is emphasis: Hawkins pushes shipping to learn over interviewing to validate: if an idea starts "like 50/50," then "perhaps exceptionally good validation puts you at like a 55% chance of being right or something," and "validate your ideas upfront" is "a cultural thing from really risk-averse, extremely large companies." Dalton at YC is cited approvingly on starting over.

- **vs. Rob Fitzpatrick (The Mom Test), partial.** He endorses the book ("Rule of thumb: Anything involving the future is an over-optimistic lie") but argues it gives "a false sense of security that you can just ask the perfect questions."

- **vs. the "pricing to value" school.** "We don't believe in loss-leaders, or squeezing customers for the maximum they're willing to pay" (the practice he labels "pricing to value"). (Part 1.) "long term, anyone pricing like this is asking to get disrupted." (Pricing lessons, founders.) Footnote: pricing to value "only makes sense if you're in a non-competitive market, have a strong moat (unlikely if you're a startup), and you're comfortable with competitors undercutting you." He allows an exception for "relationship-driven enterprise sales." Against the "keep pricing simple" school too: simple pricing "limits your ability to charge users for the value they receive." (Pricing advice, house view.)

- **vs. Mixpanel, Amplitude and Heap, on pricing structure.** He names them as charging the same per tracked user, session or event regardless of cost, which he sets against PostHog's cost-linked usage prices. (Part 2.)

- **vs. benefit-led marketing lore ("No one buys a CRM, they buy a way to increase their revenue").** He quotes and rejects it for developer products: "It makes me feel like I'm being sold to, by someone who doesn't know what they're talking about." (Tell me about features, not benefits.)

- **vs. TechCrunch-style "raise as much as you can" VC orthodoxy.** "Don't blindly follow what you hear others are doing on TechCrunch." He rejects the hype loop he himself rode in 2020-2021. (VC is a tool, not a religion.)

- **vs. the grand-vision founder model (Steve Jobs).** "Because I'm not like Steve Jobs or something." He prefers "a little bit of chaos in an appropriate dose" to a founder's grand vision, and says "I'm 100% certain our company would not be as successful had I been able to control everything anyway." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **vs. Basecamp-style bootstrapping (admiring, then declining).** In 2020 he calls Basecamp's bootstrapping philosophy "great," then chooses VC; by 2022 he argues "Raising money is less stressful than bootstrapping." (How we raised $3M; Raising money is less stressful.)

## Blind spots

External critiques of Hawkins by name are thin; none were found in this research. The critiques below come mainly from his own admissions, one anonymous insider review, and tensions between his own texts. Where the evidence is a colleague or host rather than Hawkins, it is labeled.

- **Marketed autonomy may be bounded autonomy (sourced from an outsider-facing insider review, and later partly confirmed by him).** An anonymous Glassdoor review, titled around PostHog not being "great for people-oriented, creative collaborators," says that while people direct their days, "authority clearly belongs to leadership alone," and "Big changes to the team structure or business are done top-down, via Slack, with no consultation with affected teams." It adds that the handbook's tone overstates internal reality and the culture suits "solo, silo'd, heads-down, fast-shipping, code-centered work." (Glassdoor, single anonymous review by an unnamed employee; a minority account. Glassdoor blocks automated fetches, so the wording rests on the first-round notes and was not re-checked in the final pass.)

- **Self-confirmed version of that critique (2025).** Hawkins himself writes that fast change has created "too much "top down" communication and not making us quicker as a result," that employees are "more likely as a result to be blindsided by a change we've decided to make," and that the exec team traded transparency for polish. His fix list is "a reminder to myself mostly to write down thoughts and to share them publicly," which names no metric or owner. He also admits skewing all-hands toward his own interest: "talking in all-hands mostly about the projects I am focused on (a lot of AI)." (CEO diary: communication.)

- **"Cult-like" culture and knowledge sharing not nailed (his own words).** On the zero-context Slack change: "it feels a little bit cult-like." On cross-team learning: "I don't think we've totally nailed this," and "There is now a ton of... so much written stuff happening every day that There's probably a little bit too much to keep on top of." The hosts (Al and Leanne Elliott, not Hawkins) cautioned that so much change "can be really stressful and actually quite exhausting." (TL&W #189 transcript.)

- **Selection effect: the system works for self-directed ex-founder types.** He says "the kind of person that would be very happy working at Google is probably going to be really unhappy working at PostHog," "there's no boundaries or spoonfeeding," and that "more people leave PostHog to start a company than to join another company. By quite a way." Hiring for ex-founders and experienced people, plus his admitted gap in growing early-career people (How we designed our company for speed), is a limit on transferability, not a proof the model suits teams that need coaching.

- **The no-managers / no-PM / velocity model has load-bearing dependencies.** It works "the closer you are to being a dev tool," while software for nurses needs "someone translating what a nurse cares about" to a developer "who has no idea about that world." (Executive Brand.) He concedes "As a company with high autonomy and low coordination, shipping things that affect multiple teams is a lot harder," and if you are shipping "the latest iPhone, complete design control is more important to your users than having all the features". (How we designed our company for speed.) Cherry-picked outcome metrics after the PM hire (">8x traffic") come from a small base with attribution asserted, not isolated.

- **The founder mistakes he lists himself.** He dismissed the session-replay request: "I thought this fateful request for session recordings was a bad idea, and I pushed users too quickly to something else I had in mind." He skipped the ICP for 18 months because defining one "felt bureaucratic and like something a Fortune 500 would do," and "I think we could have saved about 30% of our time if we'd done this sooner." (How we made something people want.) He over-raised ($27M+) into a "dark ages" of messy hiring and no revenue focus, and he misjudged self-hosted revenue as "90%+." (VC is a tool; Raising money is less stressful.)

- **FOMO-driven early chat assistant.** "FOMO got the better of me and I pushed this in our marketing too early." The one-shot assistant "frequently made mistakes." The 2026 AI strategy rests on unreleased capabilities and an MCP-usage figure "roughly doubling every month"; "hopefully" 10x growth is a hope, not a result. The co-CEO split concentrates product direction in Hawkins and operations in Tim, and the post does not address governance if they diverge, which sits oddly beside his own warning that cofounder breakups are the top failure mode. (PostHog's next chapter.)

- **He doesn't measure his own process well.** "We should have run a retrospective basically. I don't think we actually were getting that much better each time." (YC Podcast.) On free-user support, "We've not tracked it, but it feels like we're a lot more rushed." (CEO diary: 2022.) On marketing attribution, "I still haven't got a great process for this." (Executive Brand.)

- **Survivorship and privilege.** His raise playbook assumes YC access: "It may have been luck, but we didn't have a single negative interaction the entire way through this process" (How we raised $3M, paraphrased from his "luck" caveat). His early users came from a decade of personal contacts. The "inflated risk" reframe rests on re-employability: "That takes a lot of privilege." The hiring lessons come from the hires that worked. The "first five employees" essay does not discuss ones that did not.

- **Multi-product sprawl vs. focus.** 16-17 products and the claim to be "upstream of every piece of software they'll ever buy" is an expansive bet underwritten by a distribution engine most companies lack. He half-concedes the surveys product, and concedes multi-team shipping costs.

- **Claims in pricing posts are self-reported.** "Cheapest session replay tool in its class" is unaudited; "margin positive" has no figures; the revenue effect of the 2024 cuts is not shown; the "30% decline" is hoped to be offset by cross-sell. He also says transparent enterprise pricing is not yet standardized (his own open-items list). Product critiques of PostHog the product (rather than of Hawkins) were not found in the notes for this draft.

- **Internal tension: "Deadlines are motivating" (2024) vs the no-deadline, anti-crunch culture.** In his first-1,000-users essay a hard one-month plan "forced us to make decisions" and the team worked 9am to 3am, seven days a week; elsewhere he says deadlines "create[] coordination where people are trying to get all their work ready on the same day" and rejects crunch. He never reconciles them; a charitable reading (hard deadlines for founder-led zero-to-one, none for established teams) is not stated in either text. (First 1,000 users; Executive Brand; Pragmatic lessons from Elon.)

- **Internal tension: anti-measurement brand vs measured growth experiments.** He says "it's exactly because you can measure it that it gets saturated," yet his growth team's experiments produced "a 25% increase in our monthly growth, forever," and a feature-comparison test lifted conversion "over 30%." The closest he comes to a boundary is a 2021 line about design: "Many of the best things you can work on are hard to measure - that's why they're opportunities; no one else wants to do them." (CEO diary: How come your website is so nice?) That justifies unmeasured brand bets but never says where measurement should stop, and he concedes brand attribution is unsolved.

- **Internal tension: transparency vs the pay exception.** "You can't be sneaky if everything is public!" sits next to "We don't share individuals' pay details with each other as it's the only area we don't want employee feedback." In 2025 he adds that polish reduced transparency without anyone noticing. Sharing firing reasons and candidate decisions company-wide also raises privacy questions the essay waves through ("sounds scary to share").

- **Internal tension: "clearly defined roles" (2021) vs a role swap in 2022.** The 2021 co-founder post lays out clear boundaries (Tim: code and engineering; James: selling, design, marketing positioning). A year later "Tim (my co-founder) and I realized we were managing the wrong teams because the game had changed," and they swapped.

- **Internal tension: restraint on raising (2023) vs the later $70M Series D and $1.4B raise.** In 2023: "we don't think a huge fundraise is the right thing to do," and "I think we probably will fundraise again." The later rounds do not contradict that outright but sit uneasily with it and with the "default alive" stance; he rarely explains the reversal in his own words beyond the Collison story.

- **Internal tension: no monetization for five years vs charging sooner.** "none of the multi-billion dollar open-source companies I researched monetized in the first five years" (First 1,000 users) vs "I would have charged a little bit sooner" (Executive Brand) vs the house heuristic "Unless you're building Facebook, you're always better off charging something as soon as possible."

- **Internal tension: "Product management is broken" vs hiring a head of product and a VP Product.** He "was adamant we would never hire a product manager," then hired product people after Series B and wrote a letter recruiting a head of product. He frames the evolution honestly, but the "no PM" slogan outlived the practice.

- **Internal tension: strategy from taste vs revenue logic.** "We sometimes make business decisions based on who we are, and what we want to do." (How not to be boring.) Not tested against a counterfactual; he concedes that doing everything to a high standard is a very heavy workload (paraphrase).

- **Source-quality caveat on the 2020 sales-team essay.** It speaks in a house "we" under his byline ("our founders used to close software sales deals in the seven figures"), and its claim that PLG works because the company is "already well-funded" is a precondition it under-weights.
