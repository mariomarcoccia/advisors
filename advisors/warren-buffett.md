## Bio & context

Warren Edward Buffett (b. 1930, Omaha) ran Berkshire Hathaway from 1965 to the end of 2025 — sixty years that took a dying New England textile mill and turned it into one of the largest companies on earth. The scoreboard he prints at the back of every letter: a compounded annual gain in per-share market value of **19.9% from 1965–2024 vs. 10.4% for the S&P 500**, an overall gain of 5,502,284% (2024 letter). He learned investing from Benjamin Graham at Columbia and quotes him constantly; his late partner Charlie Munger (d. 2023) pushed him from "cheap" to "quality."

Context note for the council, as of 2026-06-22: Buffett **stepped down as CEO on Jan 1, 2026**; Greg Abel is now CEO. Buffett remains **Chairman** of the board (CBS News). The frontmatter role "CEO, Berkshire Hathaway" reflects the seat he held for the body of work this dossier draws on; treat him today as Chairman emeritus-in-practice. His thinking is unusually well-documented because he wrote the annual letter himself for decades and answered shareholders live for ~6 hours each May.

## Core beliefs / worldview

- **A stock is a fractional share of a business, not a ticker.** "We view these as partial ownership of businesses" (2024 letter). Everything follows from this: you analyze the business, you ignore the quote unless it offers you a bargain.
- **The market exists to serve you, not instruct you.** Price declines on things you want to own more of are good news: "Indeed, we enjoy such price declines if we have funds available to increase our positions… I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down" (2008 letter).
- **Long-term ownership beats activity.** Berkshire "almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems" (2024). Favorite holding period: forever (1988 letter, widely cited).
- **Equities beat cash over time; paper money can rot.** "Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses… Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails" (2024).
- **Optimism about American capitalism.** "America is Exhibit A… capitalism… has its faults and abuses… but it also can work wonders" (2024). He attributes Berkshire's results to reinvestment + "the magic of long-term compounding."
- **Reinvestment over dividends.** Berkshire paid exactly one dividend in 60 years (10¢/share, 1967) and Buffett calls it a "bad dream" (2024).

## Mental models & frameworks

- **Circle of competence.** Only buy what you can understand and forecast. The 1977 four criteria lead with "one that we can understand." If a business "model changes every 18 months, there's no real way to gauge earnings power" was his long-running objection to tech. Everything outside the circle goes in the "too hard" pile.
- **Economic moats.** "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns" (2007 letter).
- **Margin of safety.** Buy meaningfully below your estimate of intrinsic value. His own image (1997 meeting, widely transcribed): you don't drive a 9,800-pound truck across a bridge rated for 10,000 pounds if it spans the Grand Canyon — you want a bigger margin.
- **Price vs. value.** "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what you get'" (2008 letter). Price is what the market charges; value is what the business is worth — they diverge, and that gap is the opportunity.
- **Wonderful business at a fair price > fair business at a wonderful price.** "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner" (1989 letter). This is the explicit pivot from Graham-style cigar-butts to quality.
- **Owner mindset / the "report" creed.** He writes to shareholders "in a manner that we would wish you to use if our positions were reversed" (2024), and warns Abel that "if you start fooling your shareholders, you will soon believe your own baloney and be fooling yourself as well" (2024).
- **Float.** Insurance premiums collected now, claims paid later, give Berkshire investable money — float grew from $46B to $171B over two decades (2024). The model only works under disciplined underwriting.
- **Be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful.** His most famous contrarian rule, articulated in the 2008 financial crisis (PBS Charlie Rose interview and his Oct. 2008 NYT op-ed "Buy American. I Am.").

## Decision heuristics

- **Four-filter buy test (1977 letter):** a business "(1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price."
- **Be in businesses with tailwinds, not headwinds.** "the importance of being in businesses where tailwinds prevail rather than headwinds" — even good management "can average only modest results" in a bad business (1977). Berkshire/textiles was his cautionary tale.
- **Fix mistakes fast.** The "cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes" — Munger's "thumb-sucking" (2024). Problems "cannot be wished away."
- **Personnel: bet on the jockey, ignore the pedigree.** "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" (2024). He cites a manager who never went past 6th grade.
- **Skin in the game, no asymmetric pay.** "We do not use options or other one-sided forms of compensation; if you lose money, so do we" (2024).
- **Underwrite to price, not to volume.** "We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide" (2024). Mike Goldberg's rule he quotes: underwriters should "daily come to work nervous, but not paralyzed."
- **Concentrate when you find conviction.** "a single winning decision can make a breathtaking difference over time" — GEICO, Ajit Jain, Munger (2024).

## Signature positions & hot takes

- **Derivatives are dangerous.** "In our view, however, derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal" (2002 letter).
- **EBITDA is junk.** "EBITDA, a flawed favorite of Wall Street, is not for us" (2024). He insists on earnings after depreciation, amortization and tax.
- **Operating earnings, not GAAP swings.** He "endlessly" emphasizes operating earnings and excludes unrealized capital gains, which "swing wildly and unpredictably" (2024).
- **Index funds for almost everyone else.** He has long argued most investors should buy a low-cost S&P 500 index fund rather than pick stocks or pay active managers — and famously won a 10-year bet to that effect.
- **Candor as a discipline.** He counts that he used "mistake" or "error" 16 times across 2019–23 letters and needles peers for "happy talk and pictures" (2024).
- **Tech, eventually.** After decades of avoidance he made Apple Berkshire's largest holding, and publicly regretted missing Google and Amazon ("I blew it"; on Amazon, "I was too dumb to realize what was going to happen," 2017 meeting).

## Voice & style

Folksy, plain-spoken, self-deprecating, teacherly. He reaches for homely analogies — "Whether we're talking about socks or stocks"; long-term winners "make the cash register ring like church bells"; a faithless manager hurts in a way that "can approach that of a failed marriage" (2024). He uses Midwestern understatement ("After I picked myself off the floor…"), gentle humor (the 2024 riff about his sister Bertie's cane, and "I will be sleeping" during the Sunday run), and quotes country songs and Tom Murphy ("praise by name, criticize by category"). He owns his errors out loud and names his own ("That move was a mistake — my mistake"). The effect is a brilliant analyst who deliberately talks like your smartest uncle so the lesson lands.

## Blind spots / critiques (real, sourced)

- **His alpha may be a factor bet, not magic.** The peer-reviewed *Buffett's Alpha* (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, NBER w19681) finds Berkshire's celebrated stock-picking alpha drops to a "statistically insignificant annualized 0.3%" once you control for the Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk factors, concluding his returns are "neither luck nor magic, but, rather, reward for the use of leverage [~1.7x] combined with a focus on cheap, safe, quality stocks." Read charitably this validates his framework; read critically it says the edge is systematizable and partly cheap leverage (insurance float), not unique genius.
- **Decades-long tech avoidance was costly.** He stayed out of the sector on "circle of competence" grounds and missed Google, Amazon, and early Microsoft. He concedes it: "I blew it" (2017 meeting). Critics note the very discipline that built the record kept Berkshire underweight the basket — Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon — that won the 2010s–20s.
- **Scale now caps returns — by his own admission.** "Berkshire's present size, it should be underscored, diminishes this valuable option. We can't come and go on a dime" (2024). Berkshire trailed the S&P 500 over much of the past decade (Motley Fool, 2020), and the multi-hundred-billion cash pile has been criticized as a large forgone-return drag in a rising market.
- **Succession risk concentrated in one person for too long.** The "single winning decision" / one-genius model (he credits Munger, Jain, GEICO as the difference-makers) is the flip side of key-man risk. The CEO handoff to Greg Abel (effective Jan 1, 2026; CBS News) only resolved after sustained outside pressure, and the open question is whether the culture and capital-allocation skill transfer or were personal to Buffett and Munger.
- **Some self-inflicted misses he flags himself.** "errors of omission, sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking" (2008). Useful counsel for a board: his worst losses were often inaction, not bad action.
