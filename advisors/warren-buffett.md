## Summary

Warren Buffett (b. 1930, Omaha) ran Berkshire Hathaway for 60 years, 19.9% compounded annual gain against 10.4% for the S&P 1965 to 2024, by treating a stock as a fractional share of a business, buying quality at a fair price with a margin of safety, almost never selling, and funding it all with low-cost insurance float. What makes him useful in a debate is a long, dollar-denominated ledger of situated decisions: dozens of acquisition origin stories (a two-hour meeting and a handshake at McLane, a week at Scott Fetzer, Fruit of the Loom from a 1955 Graham-Newman deal) and an equally long list of errors named with their price tags (GEICO sold in 1952 for $15,259, the Gillette preferred, Dexter paid for in stock, Gen Re under-reserved, a currency bet that cost $955M in 2005, BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy in 2023), plus the temperament of "lethargy bordering on sloth" and greed only when others are fearful. His sharpest contrarian positions: derivatives are "financial weapons of mass destruction," EBITDA is a lie, beta and "risk-free" bonds mislead while cash quietly rots, gold is sterile and Bitcoin is "a gambling token," whoever bashes all buybacks is "either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue," stock-funded deals cost owners money, and almost everyone should buy a low-cost S&P 500 index fund while a true specialist should own about six names.

He stands against the active-management and 2-and-20 complex, efficient-market and style-box thinking, leverage, macro forecasting and market timing, option-based pay, "independent" directors who live on fees, growth-at-any-multiple tech investing (a discipline he concedes cost him Google and Amazon), and dynastic control, so seat him when a decision turns on capital allocation, business durability, valuation discipline, accounting honesty, underwriting, or holding your nerve, and expect clashes with the blitzscaling instincts of Hoffman, the pace-of-innovation-beats-moats view of Musk, the crypto purism of Naval, the growth-first startup ethos of Paul Graham, and the debt-fueled empire-building of Ellison.

## Bio

Warren Edward Buffett took control of a dying New England textile mill in 1965 and turned Berkshire Hathaway into one of the largest companies on earth, an overall per-share gain of 5,502,284% by the 2024 letter. He learned investing from Benjamin Graham at Columbia (and quotes him constantly: "It was exactly 50 years ago that I entered Ben Grahams class at Columbia," 2000, where he adds that before 1951 his results were "no better than average"), ran the Buffett Partnership from 1956 to 1969 (limited partners compounded 25.3% for twelve years, never a down year against the Dow; the 1984 Columbia talk gives 29.5% for the partnership and 23.8% for limited partners against 7.4% for the Dow over 1957-1969), and then wound it down at the go-go-market top rather than "play a game I don't understand." His late partner Charlie Munger (d. Nov 28, 2023, "just 33 days before his 100th birthday") pushed him from Graham-style "cigar butts" to durable quality; Buffett calls him "the 'architect' of the present Berkshire" and himself "the 'general contractor'" (2023).

His judgment is unusually well documented: he wrote the annual letter himself for roughly 48 years, answered shareholders live for about six hours each May since 1994 (CNBC's archive holds 33 full meetings, 145 hours of video and about 3,000 pages of transcript), and left a public corpus of reasoning few operators match. He runs the shop with almost no headquarters: "we delegate almost to the point of abdication: Though Berkshire has about 377,000 employees, only 26 of these are at headquarters" (Owner's Manual, later edition).

Context as of 2026-10-23: Buffett **stepped down as CEO on Jan 1, 2026** (announced at the May 2025 meeting: "I think the time has arrived"); **Greg Abel** is now CEO and Buffett remains chairman. CNBC's archive describes the May 2, 2026 meeting as Abel's first as CEO, with Buffett on the arena floor with his fellow directors, speaking briefly to praise the board's decision a year earlier (CNBC editorial wording, not Buffett's). The frontmatter role reflects the seat behind the body of work this dossier draws on; treat him today as chairman and letter-writer emeritus. In his **Nov 10, 2025 Thanksgiving letter**, his explicit farewell as the long-form author, he said he would stop "writing Berkshire's annual report or talking endlessly at the annual meeting... I'm 'going quiet.' Sort of," continuing only via shorter annual Thanksgiving notes. That letter is the richest late-career source on luck, legacy, succession, and giving the money away.

## Core ideas

- **A stock is a fractional share of a business, not a ticker.** "you are not just buying some little ticker symbol, that bounces around every day, you are buying part of a business. Soon as I started thinking about it that way, everything else followed" (U. Florida 1998). In the 1984 Columbia talk the unifying theme of nine record-holders is the same: they "search for discrepancies between the value of a business and the price of small pieces of that business in the market," always "buying the business, not buying the stock." Analyze the business; ignore the quote unless it offers a bargain. His own test of conviction: "I don't want to buy any stock where if they close the NYSE tomorrow for five years, I won't be happy owning it" (U. Florida 1998, rbcpa transcript).
- **The market serves you, not instructs you.** Graham's Mr. Market is a manic-depressive partner who names a price daily: "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful" (1987). Buffett welcomes declines in things he wants more of: "I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down" (2008). The 1997 hamburger quiz is the same idea as arithmetic: someone who will be a net saver for the next five years should want a lower market, so "smile when you read a headline that says 'Investors lose as market falls.'" (1997; see Mental models).
- **Own productive, cash-generating assets over sterile stores of value.** Assets that compound beat assets that sit. The gold-cube argument of 2011 is the vivid form (see Mental models); the mirror image is his lifelong refusal of gold, cash for its own sake, and crypto, with one dated exception in the other direction (silver in 1997, see Positions).
- **Long-term ownership beats activity; reinvestment beats dividends.** "Our favorite holding period is forever" (1988); Berkshire paid exactly one dividend in 60 years (10 cents, 1967). "Wall Street makes its money on activity. You make your money on inactivity" (U. Florida 1998). His Partnership-era line on funds was "The tide continues to be far more important than the swimmers" (BPL, July 1967); the engine is reinvested, tax-deferred earnings compounding for decades, which is why selling a winner merely to lock in a gain is usually the mistake (and why he sold GEICO in 1952 for $15,259 only to watch it become about $1.3 million, see Named mistakes).
- **Equities beat cash over time; paper money can rot.** "Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses… Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails" (2024). Even "risk-free" long bonds can be "a far riskier investment than a long-term investment in common stocks" (2017). His 1979 version was inflation arithmetic: a business earning 20% "can produce a negative real return for its owners under inflationary conditions not much more severe than presently prevail."
- **Size is the anchor of performance, and he says so every decade.** 1966 (Partnership): "I feel substantially greater size is more likely to harm future results than to help them." 1997: "We weren't smarter then, just smaller." 2023: "size did us in" and "we have no possibility of eye-popping performance." The consistent corollary is that he tells owners to expect less, not more.
- **Reputation is the asset you cannot rebuild.** Stated under maximum pressure at Salomon: lose money and he is understanding; lose "a shred of reputation" and he is "ruthless" (1991 testimony). The Owner's Manual adds the self-deception clause: "The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private."
- **Accounting is an aid to thinking, never a substitute for it, and honesty in it is a moral matter.** "the accountants' job is to record, not to evaluate… accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it" (1986). Root of his crusades against EBITDA, "cash flow," options-hiding, "big bath" restructurings, and merger-reserve padding (see Positions and Signature quotes).
- **Optimism about American capitalism, tempered by candor about luck.** "I have yet to see a time when it made sense to make a long-term bet against America" (2022); in 2015 the arithmetic version: 2% real GDP growth less 0.8% population growth compounds, over 25 years, to "a staggering $19,000 increase in real GDP per capita," and "For 240 years it's been a terrible mistake to bet against America, and now is no time to start." And: "It is beyond arrogance for American businesses or individuals to boast that they have 'done it alone.' The tidy rows of simple white crosses at Normandy should shame those who make such claims" (2018). The farewell goes furthest: "I was born in 1930 healthy, reasonably intelligent, white, male and in America. Wow! Thank you, Lady Luck" (2025), the "ovarian lottery" theme in his own late words.
- **Give it away; do not rule from the grave.** Pledged more than 99% of his net worth; the Nov 2025 letter converted 1,800 A shares into about $1.35B of B shares for four family foundations and handed disbursement to his three children rather than a perpetual trust: "Ruling from the grave does not have a great record, and I have never had an urge to do so." He had argued the principle at the start (2006): "Even if the people above ground make their decisions imperfectly, they should be able to allocate funds more rationally than a decedent six feet under will have ordained decades earlier."
- **Skin in the game is a design principle, not a slogan.** "We eat our own cooking" (Owner's Manual); "I'll keep at least 99% of my net worth in Berkshire for as long as I am around" (1998); his own pay structure promises "your economic result from Berkshire will parallel ours during the period of your ownership: We will not take cash compensation, restricted stock or option grants that would make our results superior to yours" (2001).

## Mental models

Each model carries at least one sourced worked example (situation, action, quote, source). Cross-references to Core ideas avoid restating abstractions. Where a later source changed a position, the earlier and later dates are both given.

### Business quality and valuation

- **Circle of competence.** Only buy what you can understand and forecast; everything else goes in the "too hard" pile. Ted Williams framing: "to be a good hitter, you've got to get a good ball to hit… If I have to bite at stuff that is out of my happy zone, I'm not a .344 hitter" (1994).
  - *Worked example (the line was drawn in 1967, not 1991):* in the Partnership letter of Jan 1967 he wrote "I know about as much about semi-conductors or integrated circuits as I do of the mating habits of the chrzaszcz" and "We will not go into businesses where technology which is away over my head is crucial to the investment decision." The 1991 letter restates it: "We will never develop the competence to spot such businesses early… Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)."
  - *Worked example (staying in at a price, 1999):* the year he graded himself a D (his worst absolute and relative year to that point) he refused the tech boom: "we have no insights into which participants in the tech field possess a truly durable competitive advantage" and "If others claim predictive skill in those industries… we neither envy nor emulate them" (1999). In the classroom: "If I was teaching a class at business school, on the final exam I would pass out the information on an Internet company and ask each student to value it. Anybody that gave me an answer, I'd flunk" (U. Florida 1998, rbcpa transcript).
  - *Worked example (the cost, conceded):* on Google, which GEICO was already paying for clicks and whose founders had come to see him: "I had plenty of ways to ask questions or anything of the sort, educate myself. But I blew it," with Munger adding "We blew Walmart, too" ([2017 meeting, morning session](https://buffett.cnbc.com/video/2017/05/06/morning-session---2017-berkshire-hathaway-annual-meeting.html)); on Amazon, asked why Berkshire had not bought: "because I was too dumb to realize what was going to happen" and "I did not think that he could succeed on the scale he has" ([2017 meeting, afternoon session](https://buffett.cnbc.com/video/2017/05/06/afternoon-session---2017-berkshire-hathaway-annual-meeting.html)).
  - *Worked example (the mechanism behind the misses, CNBC May 2017 and May 2019):* in the May 2017 CNBC interview he gave the mechanism, not just the verdict: GEICO was paying "$10 or $11 a click for something that had no cost of goods sold" so he should have had insight into Google, and on Amazon, asked why not: "Stupidity." He also named an anchoring problem: "there's always an anchoring problem with buying stocks"; having looked at a price of X, buying at 10X is hard, and "That's cost people a lot of money at Berkshire." In 2019 he disclaimed the Amazon purchase: "It is not true to say that I am buying Amazon. It's true to say that Berkshire is what whatever shows up there" (one of his two portfolio managers bought it), and added "I've been a fan and I've been an idiot for not buying" (CNBC May 2019).
  - *Worked example (the circle can be expanded, slowly):* he skipped Coca-Cola for 52 years while "allocating major portions of my net worth to street railway companies, windmill manufacturers, anthracite producers, textile businesses, trading-stamp issuers, and the like… Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes" (1989), then bought a stake worth more than Berkshire's entire net worth three years earlier.
  - *Worked example (inside the circle, act fast on big checks, 2019):* the Occidental financing came as a Friday call from Bank of America's CEO, a Saturday call from Occidental's CEO, a Sunday meeting, and a deal by 11:00; Berkshire was "committed unequivocally come hell or high water for 10 billion dollars." His basis was oil prices, not Permian geology: "oil prices are the big determinant over time, obviously, if you're in oil. Same things true if you're in copper" (CNBC May 2019). In the 2019 meeting he stated the filter as "are you reasonably sure that you know what you're doing? And if it gets past that hurdle, then we go on to figure out the math of gain versus loss."

- **Economic moats / franchise.** A great business needs an enduring barrier protecting high returns on capital. The sharpest test is the *franchise*: "An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation… franchises can tolerate mis-management" (1991).
  - *Worked example (castle, Duke, moat):* "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns" (2007). Orally: "I want a very valuable castle in the middle and then I want the Duke who is in charge of that castle to be very honest and hard working and able. Then I want a moat around that castle" (U. Florida 1998, Tilson transcript).
  - *Worked example (how a moat narrows, Kodak vs Coke, 1998):* "Forget about share of market, share of mind." Kodak "owned that" thirty years earlier. What eroded it, explicitly not the manager: "It is not due to George Fisher. George is doing a great job, but they let that moat narrow. They let Fuji come and start narrowing the moat in various ways." Kodak is the cautionary tale in the Tilson transcript too: "they let that moat narrow… Coke's moat is wider now than it was 30 years ago." Coke's moat widens in small invisible steps: "You can't see the moat day by day but every time the infrastructure that gets built in some country that isn't yet profitable for Coke that will be 20 years from now. The moat is widening a little bit." The test: "Give me $10 billion dollars and how much can I hurt Coca-Cola around the world? I can't do it." (rbcpa transcript, 1998).
  - *Worked example (the moat is made of micro-behavior):* at See's, "if the salesperson smiles at that last customer, our moat has widened and if she snarls at 'em, our moat has narrowed" (1998); to managers: "we want to widen the moat. We want to throw crocs, sharks and gators ... into the moat to keep away competitors." The 2005 letter turns it into a priority rule: when short and long term conflict, "widening the moat must take precedence," because "no amount of subsequent brilliance will overcome the damage" of decisions that lock in higher costs or lower satisfaction.
  - *Worked example (low-cost moat, GEICO):* "In business, I look for economic castles protected by unbreachable 'moats.' Thanks to Tony and his management team, GEICO's moat widened in 1995." Four years later: "Others may copy our model, but they will be unable to replicate our economics" (1999); the corollary for the holder of the cost advantage: "a company holding a low-cost advantage must pursue an unrelenting foot-to-the-floor strategy" (2004). GEICO's expense ratio was 23.6% of premiums in 1995; by 2015 it was 14.7% against about 25% at State Farm and Allstate and about 40 cents of the premium dollar in the old agency system.
  - *Worked example (a moat that disappeared, newspapers, 2006):* the Buffalo News once enjoyed a "law of the newspaper jungle: Survival of the Fattest" ("I owe my fortune to two great American institutions: monopoly and nepotism"); then the internet, cable and satellite broke it: "the days of lush profits from our newspaper are over," and the general law: "When an industry's underlying economics are crumbling, talented management may slow the rate of decline. Eventually, though, eroding fundamentals will overwhelm managerial brilliance." He had warned in 1995 that the News "has lost still another notch." The lesson is one he credits to "a wise friend" rather than claiming: "If you want to get a reputation as a good businessman, be sure to get into a good business" (2006).
  - *Worked example (the competitor's test, Nebraska Furniture Mart, 1983):* "One question I always ask myself in appraising a business is how I would like, assuming I had ample capital and skilled personnel, to compete with it." Verdict on Mrs. Blumkin and her family: he would rather "wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny." In 2023 he applies a replacement-cost version to American Express and Coca-Cola (paraphrase of his reasoning in the letter): could he create a better worldwide business than those two enjoy? His sister Bertie's imagined answer is "No way."

- **Economic goodwill: the gift that keeps giving (1983, with the arithmetic).** Accounting goodwill is amortized and economic goodwill is not; "businesses logically are worth far more than net tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings on such assets considerably in excess of market rates of return. The capitalized value of this excess return is economic Goodwill" (1983).
  - *Worked example:* Blue Chip bought See's in early 1972 for $25 million when it had about $8 million of net tangible assets and earned about $2 million after tax. Accounting booked $17 million of goodwill amortized at $425,000 a year, yet by 1983 See's "earned $13 million after taxes on about $20 million of net tangible assets." Then the inflation test: See's ($2M of earnings on $8M of tangible assets) versus a mundane business ($2M on $18M). Double the price level and both must double earnings to $4M, but See's needs another $8M of capital and the mundane business another $18M; the mundane business ends worth $36 million, "only a dollar of nominal value for every new dollar invested," while See's is worth about $50 million, "over $3 of nominal value gained for each $1 invested." Conclusion: "During inflation, Goodwill is the gift that keeps giving," and the old advice to hold asset-heavy businesses for inflation protection was "long on tradition, short on wisdom."
  - *The tangible-asset bias he confessed:* his early training "to favor tangible assets and to shun businesses whose value depended largely upon economic Goodwill" caused "many important business mistakes of omission, although relatively few of commission" (1983). The same appendix gives the rule for the buyer: "A good business is not always a good purchase."
  - *Spurious goodwill:* when "an overexcited management purchases a business at a silly price," the silliness "ends up in the Goodwill account" (1983). In 1999 he proposed keeping goodwill unamortized and writing it down only when impaired, because "economic goodwill does not, in many cases, diminish. Indeed, in a great many instances — perhaps most — it actually grows in value over time" ("At See's, for example, economic goodwill has grown, in an irregular but very substantial manner, for 78 years").

- **Owner earnings and the "cash flow" fallacy.** GAAP earnings plus non-cash charges (b) *minus* the capex the business truly needs (c), never (a)+(b) alone. The canonical 1986 definition: "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation… less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment… that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume."
  - *Worked example:* built via the Scott Fetzer appendix (1986) to attack the sell-side: "you shouldn't add (b) without subtracting (c): though dentists correctly claim that if you ignore your teeth they'll go away, the same is not true for (c)."
  - *Worked example (FlightSafety, 2001):* FlightSafety was spending $162M for 27 simulators in 2002 against annual depreciation of $95M: "Those who believe that EBITDA is in any way equivalent to true earnings are welcome to pick up the tab." And the general rule: "Bad terminology is the enemy of good thinking. When companies or investment professionals use terms such as 'EBITDA' and 'pro forma,' they want you to unthinkingly accept concepts that are dangerously flawed" (2001).
  - *Worked example (BNSF, 2015 and 2023):* he computes railroad interest coverage as EBIT over interest, "not EBITDA/interest, a commonly used measure we view as seriously flawed" (2015). In 2023 he writes that spending above depreciation since the 2010 purchase "have totaled a staggering $22 billion" (more than $1.5 billion annually): "Ouch!"; BNSF's balance sheet carries its assets at $70 billion, but "it would cost at least $500 billion to replicate those assets and decades to complete the job." Dividends to Berkshire "will regularly fall considerably short of BNSF's reported earnings unless we regularly increase the railroad's debt. And that we do not intend to do."
  - *Where he allows an add-back (2015):* amortization of $1.1 billion, of which "about 20% of these 'real,'" the rest customer-relationship amortization from purchase accounting. "I suggest that you ignore a portion of GAAP amortization costs. But it is with some trepidation that I do that," because managers abuse the idea: "'Stock-based compensation' is the most egregious example. The very name says it all: 'compensation.'"

- **Judge managers on return on equity, not EPS (the "stopped clock").** "'Earnings per share' will rise constantly on a dormant savings account or on a U.S. Savings Bond bearing a fixed rate of return simply because 'earnings' (the stated interest rate) are continuously plowed back and added to the capital base. Thus, even a 'stopped clock' can look like a growth stock if the dividend payout ratio is low" (1979). The test: "The primary test of managerial economic performance is the achievement of a high earnings rate on equity capital employed (without undue leverage, accounting gimmickry, etc.) and not the achievement of consistent gains in earnings per share."
  - *The price-paid corollary (2004 and 2006):* his manufacturing, service and retail group earned a "very respectable 21.7% on average tangible net worth" in 2004 but only 9.9% on carrying value ("We purchased many of them, however, at substantial premiums to net worth"); in 2006 the figures were 25% against 10.8%. Business quality and owner return are different numbers.

- **The investor's misery index (1979).** Inflation plus the percentage of capital the owner must pay in tax to move the earnings into his pocket; "When this index exceeds the rate of return earned on equity by the business, the investor's purchasing power (real capital) shrinks even though he consumes nothing at all."
  - *Worked example:* Berkshire's book value went from $19.46 (1964) to $335.85 (1979), 20.5% compounded, but "If we should continue to achieve a 20% compounded gain… your after-tax purchasing power gain is likely to be very close to zero at a 14% inflation rate." His own honest limit: "We have no corporate solution to this problem."

- **Margin of safety.** Buy meaningfully below your estimate of intrinsic value, because the estimate is fallible. "we will attempt to bring risk of permanent capital loss (not short-term quotational loss) to an absolute minimum by obtaining a wide margin of safety in each commitment" (Partnership Ground Rules, 1962).
  - *Worked example (Wells Fargo 1990):* into a California bank panic he bought 10% of Wells Fargo for $290 million, "less than five times after-tax earnings, and less than three times pre-tax earnings," reasoning its roughly $1 billion of pre-tax earning power was a cushion large enough to survive the feared real-estate collapse (1990).
  - *Worked example (Washington Post 1973, told in 1984):* the company's market value was $80 million while its assets could have been sold for "not less than $400 million" (the properties were worth $2 billion by 1984). At a $40 million valuation its beta would have been higher: "This is truly Alice in Wonderland." The reframe: "If you buy a dollar bill for 60 cents, it's riskier than if you buy a dollar bill for 40 cents, but the expectation of reward is greater in the latter case." The bridge image: "When you build a bridge, you insist it can carry 30,000 pounds, but you only drive 10,000 pound trucks across it." And "You don't try and buy businesses worth $83 million for $80 million" (Columbia, May 17 1984). The caveat he attaches: "Since you don't have your hands on the $400 million, you want to be sure you are in with honest and reasonably competent people."
  - *Worked example (the Partnership's version, 1963):* at Dempster Mill the purchase price itself was the margin of safety; the numbers and his "never count on making a good sale" rule are in Positions (Dempster Mill, BPL 1961 to 1964).
  - *The 1997 margin-of-safety warning:* "Today's price levels, though, have materially eroded the 'margin of safety' that Ben Graham identified as the cornerstone of intelligent investing" (1997), with the Dow at 7,071 and the long Treasury at 6.89% at that year's meeting, and without calling a top.

- **Price vs. value / intrinsic value as discounted cash.** "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" (attributed to Graham, 2008). The definition he uses: "intrinsic value… the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life… is all-important and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments" (1994; the Owner's Manual says it "simply"). Book value is the other number: "Book value tells you what has been put in; intrinsic business value estimates what can be taken out" (1983).
  - *Worked example (the college analogy, 1983 and the Owner's Manual):* two children put through college at identical cost have identical "book value," but "the present value of the future payoff (the intrinsic business value) might vary enormously - from zero to many times the cost of the education." Berkshire's 1964 book value of $19.46 per share was the "largely-wasted education" (all of it sat in textiles) and overstated intrinsic value; by the late 1990s book value "far understates."
  - *Worked example (value and growth are one equation):* "the two approaches [value and growth] are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value… whose impact can be negative as well as positive" (1992). In 2000: those who contrast the two styles "are displaying their ignorance, not their sophistication," and "growth can destroy value if it requires cash inputs in the early years of a project or enterprise that exceed the discounted value of the cash that those assets will generate in later years." In 2019: "all investing is value investing. I mean, you're putting out some money now to get more later on."
  - *Honest imprecision:* "Using precise numbers is, in fact, foolish; working with a range of possibilities is the better approach" (2000); two people with the same facts "will almost inevitably come up with at least slightly different intrinsic value figures," even Charlie and he (Owner's Manual), which is why Berkshire never publishes its estimate. In 2019 he described his own estimate as "a range in my mind that might have a band maybe of 10 percent."

- **The Aesop / two-birds valuation rule.** Every purchase reduces to: how sure are you the birds are in the bush, when do they emerge, how many, and at what discount rate? "The oracle was Aesop and his enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was, 'a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush'" (2000). The same letter calls the approach the "Inefficient Bush Theory" and says dividend yield, P/E and price to book "have nothing to do with valuation except to the extent they provide clues to the amount and timing of cash flows." Speculation test, same year: it is "neither illegal, immoral nor un-American" but "We bring nothing to the party, so why should we expect to take anything home?"

- **Wonderful business at a fair price > fair business at a wonderful price.** The Munger-driven pivot from Graham cigar-butts. Munger's instruction, quoted by Buffett: "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" (2014). Buffett's own version: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner" (1989), with the reputation test: "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
  - *Dated tension (who taught whom, and when):* the lesson appears in print in **1979**, before most readers date it, from Buffett's own textile experience at Waumbec Mills (see Named mistakes): "turnarounds" seldom turn, and "the same energies and talent are much better employed in a good business purchased at a fair price than in a poor business purchased at a bargain price." Then in **1989** he credits Charlie as the early understander. In the **2023** tribute he reports Munger's 1965 instruction in full ("add to it wonderful businesses purchased at fair prices and give up buying fair businesses at wonderful prices. In other words, abandon everything you learned from your hero, Ben Graham. It works but only when practiced at small scale") and adds "With much back-sliding I subsequently followed his instructions." Read Munger's wording as a *size* argument. By **2019** the shift is stated as policy: "the idea of buying the cigar butts that are declining or poor businesses for a bargain price is not something that we try to do anymore. We try to buy good businesses at a decent price."
  - *Worked example (the conversion):* See's Candies, bought 1972 for $25M against $7M tangible net worth. "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power" (1991). By 1998, "we will make $60 million this year… Same business, same formulas… and it still doesn't take any capital" (U. Florida 1998). By 1999 it had earned $857 million pre-tax on that $25M price with "very little additional capital." "I gained a business education about the value of powerful brands that opened my eyes to many other profitable investments" (2014).
  - *Worked example (the price still matters, See's negotiation, 2017):* at the meeting he said that if the seller had asked for $5 million more he would have walked; later: "Charlie saved us on that one," and "you can afford to overpay a bit for a really fine business depending on your degree of certainty that it's a really fine business and is going to stay one for a long, long time." Limit: "you can't make that decision about most businesses" (CNBC May 2017). And the 2019 calibration: "there's some price at which we could've bought even See's Candy, and it wouldn't have worked. So, the business does not know how much you paid for it."

- **Risk = permanent loss of purchasing power, not volatility/beta.** He demolishes beta with a reductio: "a stock that has dropped very sharply compared to the market — as had Washington Post when we bought it in 1973 — becomes 'riskier' at the lower price than it was at the higher price. Would that description have then made any sense…?" (1993). Real risk is "whether his aggregate after-tax receipts… will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much purchasing power as he had to begin with" (1993). The 1984 version: "Our Graham & Dodd investors, needless to say, do not discuss beta, the capital asset pricing model, or covariance in returns among securities."
  - *Worked example (LTCM, 1998):* "Sigma's do not tell you about the risk of going broke" (U. Florida 1998). The rbcpa transcript has the recollection of Salomon quants: "a six Sigma event wouldn't touch us. But they were wrong. History does not tell you of future things happening." Partners with "300 or 400 years of experience" and "hundreds and hundreds of millions of their own money" "Essentially they went broke" (title he would give a book: "Why Smart People Do Dumb Things"). Berkshire's own bid: it offered $250 million for the net assets and would have put $3.75 billion on top ($3 billion from Berkshire, $700 million from AIG, $300 million from Goldman Sachs); bankers did the deal instead.
  - *The test he applies to himself:* "we don't have any formula that evaluates risk"; committees "would try and figure out what I wanted the answer to be, and they would tell me what I wanted to hear" (2019 meeting).

### Insurance, float and underwriting

- **Float.** Insurance premiums collected now, claims paid later, supply low- or negative-cost investable capital, but only under disciplined underwriting. "float is money we hold but don't own" (1994); "we prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%" (first stated 1995, restated 1998 and 2002).
  - *The valuation rule (1999, 2000):* "The key determinants are: (1) the amount of float that the business generates; (2) its cost; and (3) most critical of all, the long-term outlook for both of these factors." "An insurance business has value if its cost of float over time is less than the cost the company would otherwise incur to obtain funds. But the business is a lemon if its cost of float is higher than market rates for money."
  - *Worked example (the numbers):* float grew from $17.3M (1967) to $3,607M (1995, 20.7% compounded), $22,762M (1998), $27.9B (2000), $44.2B (2003), $65.8B (2010), $87.7B (2015) and $171B (2024), at an average cost "under zero." But "There's nothing automatic about cheap float" (2002): the cost was 18.98% in 1984 against an 11.58% bond yield, about 6% in 2000 after a $1.6B underwriting loss, and 12.8% in 2001 (the worst since 1984). In 1995 he counted float as an equity substitute: replacing $3.4B of float with equity would produce "more shares, equal assets and lower earnings."
  - *The four disciplines:* understand the exposure, evaluate the likelihood conservatively, price for a profit, "and (4) be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained. Many insurers pass the first three tests and flunk the fourth" (2011). Non-negotiable: "We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide" (2024). First stated 1995: "No matter what others may do, we will not knowingly write business at inadequate rates."
  - *Worked example (shrinking on purpose, National Indemnity 1986 to 1999):* NICO's written premium fell from $366.2M (1986) to $54.5M (1999) while its expense ratio rose to 41.2%, then rebounded to $605.6M by 2004. "Can you imagine any public company embracing a business model that would lead to the decline in revenue that we experienced from 1986 through 1999?" and "we instead consistently priced to make a profit, not to match our most optimistic competitor. We never left customers – but they left us" (2004). The enabling promise: "we have always promised NICO's workforce that no one will be fired because of declining volume, however severe the contraction." The 1979 antecedent: "We hear a great many insurance managers talk about being willing to reduce volume in order to underwrite profitably, but we find that very few actually do so. Phil Liesche is an exception: if business makes sense, he writes it; if it doesn't, he rejects it." NICO itself was bought on March 9, 1967 from Jack Ringwalt for $8.6M ("We just shook hands, and that was that"; Ringwalt was late "looking for a parking meter with some unexpired time. That was a magic moment for me") (2006).
  - *Worked example (a retroactive megadeal, Equitas, 2006):* Berkshire took $7.12B in cash and securities and agreed to cover Lloyd's pre-1993 claims up to $13.9B, "$5.7 billion above what Equitas had recently guessed its ultimate liabilities to be." Logic: "13 years after the last exposed policy had been written and after the payment of £11.3 billion in claims - we concluded that the patient was likely to survive." Float up about $7B; amortization up from about $300M to about $450M a year, so "our other insurance operations must generate at least that much underwriting gain for our overall float to be cost-free." Only Berkshire could offer "certainty" over a 50-year tail. In 2000 he had stated the principle behind it: "Charlie and I, however, like any proposition that makes compelling mathematical sense, regardless of its effect on reported earnings."
  - *Worked example (float as revolving fund, 2015):* float "should instead be viewed as a revolving fund" because "Owing $1 that in effect will never leave the premises" is not like owing a dollar tomorrow. Insurance goodwill is $15.5B and "almost the entire" figure "was already on our books in 2000. Yet we subsequently tripled our float." A mega-cat test: a $250 billion industry event (about triple anything ever seen) would still likely leave Berkshire with "a significant profit for the year because of its many streams of earnings."

- **Experience vs exposure; the three underwriting rules; the Noah rule (2001).** After 9/11: "all of us in the industry made a fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on experience, rather than exposure, thereby assuming a huge terrorism risk for which we received no premium." The three rules (numbered in the letter): (1) accept only risks you can properly evaluate and that carry profit expectancy, ignore market share; (2) limit aggregation from a single event and "ceaselessly search for possible correlation among seemingly-unrelated risks"; (3) avoid moral risk: "No matter what the rate, trying to write good contracts with bad people doesn't work."
  - *Worked example (D&O late in a bull market):* "Late in a bull market, for example, large losses from directors and officers liability insurance ('D&O') are likely to be relatively rare," yet exposure is exploding then, and the correct rate for D&O "excess" "might well, if based on exposure, be five or more times the premium dictated by experience."
  - *The Noah rule:* asked why he did not act before September 11: "The answer, sadly, is that I did - but I didn't convert thought into action. I violated the Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does." He had let General Re "take on business without a safeguard I knew was important." 2001 net worth fell $3.77B; Gen Re had "a staggering $800 million of loss costs that actually occurred in earlier years, but that were not then recorded."
  - *Risk appetite after the loss:* "We are perfectly willing to lose $2 billion to $2½ billion in a single event (as we did on September 11th) if we have been paid properly for assuming the risk that caused the loss (which on that occasion we weren't)" (2001); in 2006 "We remain prepared to lose $6 billion in a single event, if we have been paid appropriately for assuming that risk. We are not willing, though, to take on even very small exposures at prices that don't reflect our evaluation of loss probabilities." And "insurers produce outstanding long-term results primarily by avoiding dumb decisions, rather than by making brilliant ones" (2001).
  - *Worked example (Pascal's wager on mega-cat, 2005):* Katrina cost Berkshire about $2.5B and all 2005 hurricanes $3.4B. On whether the climate has changed: "Joe, Ajit and I don't know the answer to these all-important questions." They follow Pascal: since he did not know the answer, "his personal gain/loss ratio dictated an affirmative conclusion," so write mega-cat only at prices far above the prior year and with exposure that "would not cause us distress."
  - *Worked example (cheap reinsurance, GEICO 1981 to 83):* GEICO took in $3,051,000 of umbrella and product-liability premium, spent $2,979,000 on reinsurance and kept $72,000; losses are now $94.1M, of which $90.3M is uncollectable from "deadbeat reinsurers." "So much for 'cheap' reinsurance" (2002). Also: "Surprises in insurance are far from symmetrical. You are lucky if you get one that is pleasant for every ten that go the other way" (2005).
  - *Worked example (reading reserves, "rented suits," 2001):* "The losses didn't 'develop' - they were there all along. What developed was management's understanding of the losses" And "insurance accounting is a self-graded exam… A company experiencing financial difficulties… seldom proves to be a tough grader. Who, after all, wants to prepare his own execution papers?" The rented-suit story: the traveler pays a $4,500 mortuary bill and then two $10 invoices because "We buried dad in a rented suit." "There are a lot of 'rented suits' buried in the past operations of insurance companies."

### Capital allocation, payout and the cost of activity

- **Look-through earnings + the retained-dollar test.** GAAP understates an owner's economics by ignoring investees' retained earnings; a dollar retained is justified only if it eventually creates at least a dollar of market value.
  - *Worked example (Apple, 2021):* "only dividends from Apple are counted in the GAAP earnings Berkshire reports… Apple paid us $785 million… Yet our 'share' of Apple's earnings amounted to a staggering $5.6 billion" (2021). He grounds the whole "no dividend" case on it, invoking Edgar Lawrence Smith: "it was no secret that mind-boggling wealth had earlier been amassed by such titans as Carnegie, Rockefeller and Ford, all of whom had retained a huge portion of their business earnings" (2019).
  - *Worked example (the 1999 table):* undistributed operating earnings of the major investees: American Express $228M (11.3% owned), Coca-Cola $144M (8.1%), Freddie Mac $127M, Gillette $53M, M&T $17M, Washington Post $30M, Wells Fargo $108M, a total of $707M; less a hypothetical $99M of tax at 14%, plus $1,318M reported operating earnings, gives look-through earnings of $1,926M (1999). The 2000 version: $1,779M plus $740M less $104M is $2,415M. In 2015 the Big Four held an extra lever: each point of added ownership "raises Berkshire's portion of their annual earnings by about $500 million" and Berkshire's $4.7B look-through share compared with about $1.8B of dividends: "It's better to have a partial interest in the Hope Diamond than to own all of a rhinestone."
  - *The 2009 correction of his own rule:* the Owner's Manual (originally 1983) tested retention "on a five-year rolling basis." He now says, "I should have written the 'five-year rolling basis' sentence differently, an error I didn't realize until I received a question about this subject at the 2009 annual meeting," because any five-year market decline (1971 to 1975, "well before I wrote this principle in 1983") breaks the test. The replacement test is (1) did book value gain beat the S&P over the period, and (2) did the stock consistently sell at a premium to book, so every retained dollar was worth more than a dollar. He also retired book value as the scorecard in 2018.

  - *Worked example (where the retained-dollar test comes from, 1984):* the "Dividend Policy" essay first separates "restricted" earnings, which inflation conscripts, from the distributable kind. Consolidated Edison is the cautionary case: under punitive regulation "every time a dollar of earnings was retained for reinvestment in the business, that dollar was transformed into only 25 cents of market value," yet most earnings were reinvested while its work sites proclaimed "Dig We Must." The rule: "Unrestricted earnings should be retained only when there is a reasonable prospect - backed preferably by historical evidence or, when appropriate, by a thoughtful analysis of the future - that for every dollar retained by the corporation, at least one dollar of market value will be created for owners." His illustration is a risk-free perpetual 10% bond whose holder may take each coupon in cash or in more bonds: at 5% prevailing rates no rational holder takes cash, at 15% none reinvests (1984).
- **The sell-off beats the dividend (the arithmetic).** For a company reinvesting above its cost of capital and trading above book, a shareholder who sells a sliver of stock each year ends up with both more cash and more capital than one taking a dividend, and is taxed only on the gain. Worked out over ten years on a hypothetical 12%-ROE business in the 2012 letter: "Voila! – you would have both more cash to spend annually and more capital value." Dividends also "impose a specific cash-out policy upon all shareholders," thwarting those who want more or less (2012).

- **Buybacks as reverse dilution, but only below intrinsic value.** "Charlie and I favor repurchases when… its stock is selling at a material discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated" (2011); "what is smart at one price is dumb at another."
  - *Worked example (Apple's buyback, 2020):* despite selling part of the position in 2020, Berkshire "now owns 5.4% of Apple. That increase was costless to us, coming about because Apple has continuously repurchased its shares." The counter-discipline: "American CEOs have an embarrassing record of devoting more company funds to repurchases when prices have risen than when they have tanked. Our approach is exactly the reverse" (2020).
  - *The 1999 rule, stated as a single combination of facts:* "There is only one combination of facts that makes it advisable for a company to repurchase its shares: First, the company has available funds — cash plus sensible borrowing capacity — beyond the near-term needs of the business and, second, finds its stock selling in the market below its intrinsic value, conservatively-calculated." The fad: repurchases made "to pump or support the stock price… Buying dollar bills for $1.10 is not good business for those who stick around." Option-offset buybacks are a "'buy high, sell low' strategy." The arithmetic of limited benefit: repurchasing 2% of a company at a 25% discount "produces only a ½% gain in that value at most." He also owned an error: "at certain times in the past, I have erred in not making repurchases."
  - *Worked example (three-partner arithmetic, 2019 meeting):* a business worth $3 million with three equal partners; buying one out at $1.1 million or $1 million, "forget it"; at $900,000 "we'd take it," because the remaining business would be worth $2.1 million and each stake rises "from a million to a million and fifty-thousand." Rule: repurchase "only when you think you're doing it at a price where the remaining shareholders… are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before." Standing thresholds: "we would be delighted to repurchase our shares should they sell as low as 120% of book value" (2015); willing if the stock sold "25 or 30 percent less than it was worth" (2019); the "$100 billion" remark "I probably arrived at… in about three seconds when I got asked the question" (2019). The loyal-holder constraint: "we want to make very, very sure that we don't lose permanently money for anybody that buys our stock somewhere around intrinsic business value."
  - *The mirror image, issuing stock (BNSF 2009, with Company A and B):* "If we wouldn't dream of selling Berkshire in its entirety at the current market price, why in the world should we 'sell' a significant part of the company at that same inadequate price by issuing our stock in a merger?" Company A and B each have $100 per share of intrinsic value and both trade at $80; A offers 1.25 A shares per B share (giving $125 for $100), B holders end with 55.6% of the combined company, and "Not everyone at A, it should be noted, is a loser from this nonsensical transaction. Its CEO now runs a company twice as large." BNSF itself cost 95,000 Berkshire shares (6.1% of the shares outstanding) and about 30% of the total cost in stock; "the final decision was a close one" (2009).

- **The institutional imperative.** His 1989 name for the gravity that makes rational managers behave irrationally: institutions resist changing direction "as if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion"; "corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up available funds"; "any business craving of the leader, however foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies"; and peer behavior "will be mindlessly imitated." Recognizing it is half of resisting it.
  - *Worked example (National Indemnity 2004):* "Most American businesses harbor an 'institutional imperative' that rejects extended decreases in volume," plus a delay in consequences ("a form of self-deception that nearly destroyed GEICO in the early 1970s") and a fear factor: "To avoid pink slips, employees will rationalize inadequate pricing."
  - *Worked example (Tom Murphy's rule, 2004):* an employee asking to hire an assistant at $20,000 is "a $3 million decision," "given that an additional person would probably cost at least that amount over his lifetime, factoring in raises, benefits and other expenses (more people, more toilet paper)."
  - *Worked example (CEO pay, 2025):* see Signature quotes.

- **Interest rates as gravity.** Rates are the master variable beneath every asset price. At the 1999 dot-com peak: rates "act on financial valuations the way gravity acts on matter: The higher the rate, the greater the downward pull," and he pegged the most probable long-run stock return at "6%" ([Fortune 1999](https://fortune.com/article/warren-buffett-on-stock-market/)). Over 1964 to 1981 the Dow went nowhere (874 to 875) while GDP rose about 370% because long rates rose to about 15%; from 1981 to 1998 the Dow rose tenfold as rates fell, so extrapolating the bull run was "the Tinker Bell approach — clap if you believe."
  - *Worked example (the 1999 GDP arithmetic, in his own letter):* corporate profits grow with GDP; real growth of about 3% plus about 2% inflation gives about 5%, and "If profits do indeed grow along with GDP, at about a 5% rate, the valuation placed on American business is unlikely to climb by much more than that. Add in something for dividends, and you emerge with returns from equities that are dramatically less than most investors have either experienced in the past or expect in the future" (1999).
  - *Worked example (2017):* "Any investment is worth all the cash you're gonna get out between now and judgment day discounted back." In 1982, with 15% short rates, "it was silly to pay 20 times earnings for stocks." With about $95 billion in cash at about 1%: "it's selling at a 100 times earnings. And the earnings can't go up, which sounds like a pretty dumb investment and it is." And the honest limit: "I can tell you the right question. I can't tell you the right answer necessarily" (CNBC May 2017). In 2002 his hurdle for owning equities was "a very high probability of at least 10% pre-tax returns (which translate to 6½-7% after corporate tax)," or "we will sit on the sidelines."

- **"The Secret Sauce": a few huge winners carry everything.** The record came not from a high batting average but a handful of decades-long compounders. "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders" (2022). Coca-Cola and Amex (bought about 1994 to 1995): Coke dividends grew from $75M (1994) to $704M (2022), Amex $41M to $302M, on a fixed cost basis. "Our satisfactory results have been the product of about a dozen truly good decisions — that would be about one every five years" (2022). 2023: "By both luck and pluck, a few huge winners have emerged from a great many dozens of decisions." The Rip Van Winkle version: Berkshire bought or sold neither stock in 2023, "extending our own Rip Van Winkle slumber that has now lasted well over two decades," and "our share of AMEX earnings in 2023 considerably exceeded the $1.3 billion cost of our long-ago purchase."

- **The friction tax: the Gotrocks family and the "chair-changing" cost.** "the most that owners in aggregate can earn between now and Judgment Day is what their businesses in aggregate earn" (2005).
  - *Worked example (Gotrocks, 2005):* the Gotrocks family owns all of corporate America (about $700 billion a year of earnings); "Helpers" arrive in layers (brokers, managers, consultants, then "hyper-Helpers" charging performance fees), turning the family into "the Hadrocks," so frictional costs may take about "20% of the earnings of American business." Law: "For investors as a whole, returns decrease as motion increases." Check: the Dow rose from 65.73 to 11,497.12 in the 20th century, "a gain of 5.3% compounded annually," so an equal 21st-century rate implies a Dow of 2,011,011.23 by the end of 2099.
  - *Worked example (1983, the chair-changing tax):* a company earning 12% on equity with 100% annual share turnover and 1% commissions each way forfeits 2% of net worth a year, one-sixth of its earnings; 100-million-share days at 15 cents a side cost "about $7.5 billion" a year (roughly the combined 1982 profits of Exxon, GM, Mobil and Texaco). "what is good for the croupier is not good for the customer. A hyperactive stock market is the pickpocket of enterprise." He rejects the Adam Smith defense: "casino-type markets and hair-trigger investment management act as an invisible foot that trips up and slows down a forward-moving economy." Hence no stock split (then about $1,300 a share): "People who buy for non-value reasons are likely to sell for non-value reasons," with more than 90 to 95% of shares held by people who owned five years earlier.
  - *Worked example (2-and-20, 2006):* gross return 10%; the manager keeps 2 points plus 20% of the remaining 8, "leaving only 6.4 percentage points for his investors. On a $3 billion fund, this 6.4% net 'performance' will deliver the manager a cool $108 million. He will receive this bonanza even though an index fund might have returned 15% to investors." He closes with "the old adage" rather than a coinage of his own: "When someone with experience proposes a deal to someone with money, too often the fellow with money ends up with the experience, and the fellow with experience ends up with the money." In 2019 he added: "If you can raise $10 billion in a fund" at a 1 1/2 percent fee with ten-year lockups, "you and your children and your grandchildren will never have to do a thing"; a manager who was told about "2-and-20" replied "I can't get 3-and-30"; private equity returns "are not calculated in a manner that I would regard as honest."

- **Productive vs. unproductive capital (the gold-cube).** Compare what an asset *produces* over decades, not what it might be resold for. In 2011 he pits "pile A," all the world's gold, a 68-foot cube worth about $9.6T, against "pile B" of equal cost: all U.S. cropland plus "16 Exxon Mobils," with about $1T left over. "You can fondle the cube, but it will not respond" (2011). Generalized in 2018: a 1942 investment of $114.75 in an index would be "$606,811," but in gold "about $4,200, less than 1%… The magical metal was no match for the American mettle."
  - *Dated evolution (1979, 1997, 2011):* in 1979 he reported without endorsing a commentator's finding that 1964 book value "would have bought about one-half ounce of gold" and fifteen years later the same half ounce; his reading was that government "is unable to print gold or create oil." In 1997 he disclosed 111.2 million ounces of silver (pre-tax gain $97.4M, first bought 30 years earlier "because I anticipated its demonetization by the U.S. Government") and added "Inflation expectations, it should be noted, play no part in our calculation of silver's value." The 2011 letter is the settled position against unproductive stores of value; the 1997 silver was a supply and demand bet, which is why the two do not contradict.

- **Look for the important and knowable; what vs. when.** "What you really want to in investments is figure out what is important and knowable. If it is unimportant and unknowable, you forget about it" (U. Florida 1998, rbcpa). "You can figure what will happen, you can't figure out when it will happen. You don't want to focus too much on when but you want to focus on what." The Partnership said the same in 1966: "we tend to concentrate on what should happen, not when it should happen."
  - *Worked example (Coke's 1919 IPO):* Coca-Cola IPO'd at $40 in 1919 and fell about 50% within a year; $40 compounding at 14.63% for 86 years with reinvested dividends was "$5 million now." "You can always find reasons that weren't the ideal moment to buy it" (rbcpa 1998).

- **Generals, workouts and controls (the Partnership's three-way toolkit).** Generals are "undervalued situations"; workouts are "dependent on a specific corporate action for its profit rather than a general advance in the price of the stock"; controls are businesses he can steer. In 1957 the mix was about 85/15 generals to workouts (70/30 a year earlier). He also set yardsticks in advance ("A Word About Par," Jan 1962): "I believe in establishing yardsticks prior to the act; retrospectively, almost anything can be made to look good in relation to something or other." Worked examples are in Positions (Commonwealth Trust, Sanborn Map, Dempster Mill, Texas National Petroleum, Berkshire, Hochschild Kohn). Category returns, for scale: 1962 generals -1.0%, workouts 14.6%, Dow -8.6%; 1963 generals 20.5%, workouts 30.6%, Dow 18.4%; 1964 generals 27.8%, workouts 10.3%, Dow 16.7%.

- **Mono-linked chains (2004).** "If only one variable is key to a decision, and the variable has a 90% chance of going your way, the chance for a successful outcome is obviously 90%. But if ten independent variables need to break favorably for a successful result, and each has a 90% probability of success, the likelihood of having a winner is only 35%." So "it makes sense to look for – if you'll excuse an oxymoron – mono-linked chains."
  - *Worked example (zinc, 2004):* MidAmerican's zinc-recovery project from California geothermal brine (started 1998, operational 2002) was abandoned with a pre-tax loss of $579M ("In September, we threw in the towel"): "prospects have a way of 'teasing' their developers, and every time one problem was solved, another popped up." "In our zinc venture, we solved most of the problems. But one proved intractable, and that was one too many." Guideline: "stay with simple propositions."
  - *Second case (utilities, 2023):* the pact of "fixed-but-satisfactory" regulated returns "has been broken in a few states" and he did not "anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns" (see Named mistakes).

- **Have-to-be-smart-once vs. have-to-be-smart-every-day (1995).** In retailing "a retailer must stay smart, day after day"; "In retailing, to coast is to fail." The contrast: "if you were smart enough to buy a network TV station very early in the game, you could put in a shiftless and backward nephew to run things, and the business would still do well for decades." It tells him how much to pay for management in each type of business: R.C. Willey and Helzberg's were bought only because the managers "love to compete and have done so successfully for decades." In 1979 he put the same point in price terms: a network TV station sells "one thousand cents or more on the dollar… Despite a fancy price tag, the 'easy' business may be the better route to go."

- **Inversion and "What We Don't Do" (2009).** Munger's "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there" (via Jacobi, "Invert, always invert"). Four applications: avoid businesses whose futures you cannot judge, however exciting (autos in 1910, aircraft in 1930, TV sets in 1950: the growth was foreseeable, the profit margins were not; "Even the survivors tended to come away bleeding"); "We will never become dependent on the kindness of strangers" (in September 2008 Berkshire "was a supplier of liquidity and capital to the system, not a supplicant," with $15.5B committed, $9B to three businesses and $6.5B for the Wrigley deal, at the cost of $20B+ in cash earning "a pittance": "But we sleep well"); tolerate "a few bad decisions" by autonomous managers over "the many invisible costs that come from decisions made too slowly"; and no wooing of Wall Street.

- **Fat pitch (Ted Williams, 1997).** From Williams's *The Science of Hitting*: the strike zone is 77 baseball-sized cells; the best cell yields .400 and the worst (low outside corner) .230. "waiting for the fat pitch would mean a trip to the Hall of Fame; swinging indiscriminately would mean a ticket to the minors." The honest cost: "just standing there, day after day, with my bat on my shoulder is not my idea of fun." The asymmetry (my paraphrase of the passage) is that an investor, unlike a hitter, is not called out on strikes, which is why waiting is possible at all.

- **The orangutan test and Graham-and-Doddsville (1984).** Imagine a national coin-flipping contest: of 225 million Americans, after ten flips "approximately 220,000 people" have called ten in a row, and after twenty flips "215 people" have turned a dollar into "a little over $1 million." The key move: if 40 of the 215 winning orangutans came "from a particular zoo in Omaha, you would be pretty sure you were on to something." The Columbia talk then names nine records (see Positions for the table). The epistemic rule: the exercise only works because the winners were "well known to me and pre-identified as superior investors"; chosen after the fact it fails. Observation across them: "it is extraordinary to me that the idea of buying dollar bills for 40 cents takes immediately to people or it doesn't take at all. It's like an inoculation."

- **The hamburger quiz (1997).** "If you expect to be a net saver during the next five years, should you hope for a higher or lower stock market during that period? Many investors get this one wrong." Net eaters of hamburger want cheaper beef. "there is a buyer for every seller and what hurts one necessarily helps the other." He applies it to Berkshire's own investees: 97% of Berkshire's shares are held by the same investors year to year, so owners are savers, and Coca-Cola, Washington Post and Wells Fargo bought back more ownership when prices were low.

- **Relative to a yardstick you set in advance (Partnership, 2001).** "Whether we do a good job or a poor job is to be measured against the general experience in securities" (Ground Rules, quoted in 2001). Defense against "you can't eat relative performance": See's loses money in summer yet you "can eat well throughout the year"; gaining small advantages annually must prove rewarding. His scorecard history: bogey the S&P with dividends, proxy per-share book value (45-year numbers: book value +20.3% a year, $19 to $84,487; market +22%), "That keeps us from the temptation of seeing where the arrow of performance lands and then painting the bull's eye around it" (2009).

- **Two-column intrinsic value (1999 and after).** Value Berkshire as per-share investments plus pre-tax operating earnings with investment income excluded. 1999: investments $45 (1969), $577 (1979), $7,200 (1989), $47,339 (1999); operating earnings $4.39, $13.07, $108.86, and $(458.55) in 1999 (a General Re underwriting loss). Later: $4 to $74,129 (2005, 28.0%) and $80,636 (2006, 27.5% since 1965 but 12.6% from 1995 to 2006); operating earnings $4 to $3,625 (17.9%, 31.7% for 1995 to 2006); 2015: $159,794 of investments per share and $12,304 of pre-tax operating earnings. The 2003 warning on base years: "growth-rate presentations can be significantly distorted by a calculated selection of either initial or terminal dates," so his table starts at 1968 (1964 earnings were $175,586, or 15 cents a share).

## Decision heuristics

How he actually decides under uncertainty: the questions he asks himself, what he cuts first. Dated where a position moved.

### Screening and buying

- **Four-filter buy test (1977):** a business "(1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price." Restated in 2003 as three parts: businesses that "(1) have favorable and enduring economic characteristics; (2) are run by talented and honest managers and (3) are available at a sensible price." Later compressed into the **four building blocks of value** (2017): sizable stand-alone acquisitions; bolt-ons; internal sales growth and margin improvement; investment earnings from the portfolio. The 2015 five-point successor blueprint adds repurchases at a meaningful discount and "rarely, if ever, issuing Berkshire shares."
- **The acquisition criteria list (1983):** at least $5 million of after-tax earnings; "demonstrated consistent earning power" (projections and turnarounds "of little interest"); good return on equity with little debt; management in place; "simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)"; an offering price; "We will not engage in unfriendly takeovers"; an answer "customarily within five minutes."
- **Be in businesses with tailwinds, not headwinds.** Even good management "can average only modest results" in a bad business (1977); "time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre" (1989). In 1998: "But time is the friend of the wonderful business; it is the enemy of the lousy business. If you are in a lousy business for a long time, you will get a lousy result even if you buy it cheap." Textiles, shoes and USAir were the cautionary tales; the 1983 warning on turnaround capex is the cost side: "The projections will be dazzling and the advocates sincere, but, in the end, major additional investment in a terrible industry usually is about as rewarding as struggling in quicksand" (Owner's Manual; originally 1983).
- **Price, don't time; but do hold the price discipline hard.** "We try to price, rather than time, purchases… Why scrap an informed decision because of an uninformed guess?" (1994). "Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist" (1994). The choice between a questionable business at a comfortable price and a comfortable business at a questionable price: "we much prefer the latter. What really gets our attention, however, is a comfortable business at a comfortable price" (1999). The 2019 calibration after Kraft: "You can turn any investment into a bad deal by paying too much. What you can't do is turn any investment into a good deal by paying little, which is sort of how I started out in this world."
- **Say the reason out loud.** "You really want your decision making to be by looking in the mirror. Saying to yourself, 'I am buying 100 shares of General Motors at $55 because……..' It is your responsibility if you are buying it. There's gotta be a reason and if you can't state the reason, you shouldn't buy it. If it is because someone told you about it at a cocktail party, not good enough" (U. Florida 1998, rbcpa). And: "We never buy something with a price target in mind."
- **Sometimes the numbers say no; do not demand a sure thing to pay a lot.** "The best buys have been when the numbers almost tell you not to" (U. Florida 1998). On what a near-certainty should yield (my paraphrase of the answer): do not expect a big return from something you are sure of. His words: "Why should something that is essentially a cinch to do well, offer you 40% a year? We don't have huge returns in mind, but we do have in mind not losing anything."
- **Five-minute analysis, then no haggling.** "General Re is a $18 billion deal. I have never been to their home office. I hope it is there." "Almost every business we have bought has taken five or ten minutes in terms of analysis." On negotiation: "I don't fool a lot around with negotiations. If they name a price that makes sense to me, I buy it. If they don't, I was happy the day before, so I will be happy the day after without owning it" (rbcpa 1998). Scuttlebutt, borrowed from Fisher: he asked CEOs "If you could only buy stock in one coal company that was not your own, which one would it be and why?" and "If you had a silver bullet and you could put it through the head of one competitor, which competitor and why?" ("You will find who the best guy is in the industry").
- **Do not read the seller's projections.** "why potential buyers even look at projections prepared by sellers baffles me. Charlie and I never give them a glance" (1995; horse story: "when he's walking fine, sell him"). 1999: bankers' books show earnings "projected for many years ahead" with precision, yet ask the banker about his own firm's next month and "he will go into a protective crouch." Munger's retort to the banker on the failed Scott Fetzer sale is under Acquisition stories (1999).
- **Ask the seller: "any more at home like you?"** After NFM in 1983 he asked each new partner whether there were more; the Blumkins named three furniture retailers, which led to R.C. Willey (1995), Star Furniture (1997) and Jordan's (1999) (1997, 1999 letters).
- **How the seller treats his people predicts how he treats you.** Helzberg: "Though he was certainly under no obligation to do so, Barnett shared a meaningful part of his proceeds from the sale with a large number of his associates. When someone behaves that generously, you know you are going to be treated right as a buyer" (1995). The Tatelmans paid their employees about $9 million of their own money at the Jordan's sale (1999); the Star owners did the same (1997).
- **Sellers who love the company, not only the money.** "We like to do business with someone who loves his company, not just the money that a sale will bring him"; a business auctioned with "a total lack of interest in what follows" has often "been dressed up for sale" (2000). A late-1960s counterpart: "Even if the price had been cheaper but the management had been run-of-the-mill, we would not have bought the business" (Hochschild Kohn, BPL 1966).
- **Buying what a manager has skin in:** R.C. Willey's Bill Child offered to buy the land, build the Boise store and sell it to Berkshire at cost "(without interest!)" if it worked, and "eat the loss" if it did not (2001). Buffett was "highly skeptical" of a no-Sunday format and turned out wrong three times (see Named mistakes); "the opinion of someone who is always wrong has its own special utility to decisionmakers" (2004).

### Avoiding the big error

- **Fix mistakes fast; the costliest errors are inaction.** The "cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes" (2024). His self-scored worst losses are "errors of omission, sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking" (2008). The 2005 Gen Re Securities rule on acting now is under Named mistakes. On Wells Fargo: "if you're in the top job, you've got to take action fast" (2019 meeting); "The big mistake was whatever sufficient information had come back that this is producing a counterproductive effect... That's the moment of truth" (CNBC May 2017).
- **I make mistakes when I get cash.** "I make mistakes when I get cash. Charlie tells me to go to a bar instead" (U. Florida 1998, rbcpa), about buying Salomon and USAir preferreds when he "had a lot of money around" and "it looked to me like we would never get to do anything." The pattern he names: "Buying something because you like the terms, but you don't like the business that well."
- **Never mix what should happen with what will happen.** "I never believe in mixing what I think should happen (socially) with what I think will happen in making decisions" (Partnership letter, May 1969, after workouts lost money: "in this case, we would be some millions better off if I had").
- **Never risk what you have and need for what you don't have and don't need.** "it is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need" (2017); "anything times zero is zero" (Charlie Rose 2008). The 1998 form is expected-utility, not odds: "If you risk something that is important to you for something that is unimportant to you it just doesn't make sense. I don't care if the odds you succeed are 99 to 1 or 1000 to 1." And the million-chamber gun: "If you hand me a gun with a million chambers with one bullet in a chamber and put it up to your temple and I am paid to pull the trigger, it doesn't matter how much I would be paid." Also: "If you have $100 million at the beginning of the year and you will make 10% if you are unleveraged and 20% if you are leveraged 99 times out of a 100, what difference if at the end of the year, you have $110 million or $120 million?" (rbcpa 1998).
- **Probabilities of long chains and small chances.** The mono-linked chain (see Mental models); and from 2015: "what's a small probability in a short period approaches certainty in the longer run" (a one-in-thirty annual chance gives 96.6% over a century). The one risk he says he cannot hedge: a cyber, biological, nuclear or chemical attack on the United States.
- **Read a 10-K's risk section for three things only.** "I can't remember, however, an instance when reading a 10-K's 'risk' section has helped me in evaluating a business." He wants probability, range of costs, and timing: "A threat that will only surface 50 years from now may be a problem for society, but it is not a financial problem for today's investor" (2015).
- **Red flags in other people's accounts (2002).** "There is seldom just one cockroach in the kitchen" (the footnote rule is under Accounting); "Managers that always promise to 'make the numbers' will at some point be tempted to make up the numbers."
- **Sizing for survival: be the last man standing.** "We run it so that we will be the last man standing" (CNBC 2023). Berkshire's four-drawdown table (falls of 59%, 37%, 49%, 51%) is "the strongest argument… against ever using borrowed money to own stocks" (2017). The 2005 risk law: "a long string of impressive numbers multiplied by a single zero always equals zero." On promises of safety: "We are quite willing to accept huge risks" if sized to survive.

### Process, people and communication

- **Ignore macro forecasts (with a documented exception pattern).** "We will continue to ignore political and economic forecasts, which are an expensive distraction" (1994); "I've been in business, running Berkshire for 58 years, and I've never opined an economic forecast of any use to the company" (CNBC 2023).
- **Ignore macro forecasts: the Partnership version (Ground Rule 6, BPL 1963 and 1966).** "I am not in the business of predicting general stock market or business fluctuations. If you think I can do this, or think it is essential to an investment program, you should not be in the partnership" (Ground Rule 6, 1963 and 1966), and the 1966 test: "(1) if they knew in February that the Dow was going to 865 in May, why didn't they let me in on it then; and, (2) if they didn't know what was going to happen during the ensuing three months back in February, how do they know in May?" Go "to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is" (Gretzky, quoted 1994), but the puck is business economics, not the Fed.
- **Dated exceptions to the no-forecast rule (2002 to 2006, 2019).** The exceptions: the 2002 to 2006 currency position (see Positions) and the 2019 remark "I don't think they can coexist with really low inflation rates over time. But I've been wrong on that for some time, I may be wrong for a long time in the future." Resolution (my reading): he states each macro call with error bars, in writing, and sizes it so it cannot hurt the fortress; the discipline is not to avoid holding any macro view but never to bet the company on one. Elections: "I can't think of when I've really done much about purchases or sales in connection with any election" (CNBC May 2017; the GDP-print line is under Macro).
- **Concentrate when conviction is real; act as if you have 20 punches.** "I could improve your ultimate financial welfare by giving you a ticket with only 20 slots in it… and once you'd punched through the card, you couldn't make any more investments at all" (Munger recounting Buffett, USC 1994). "if you… find five to ten sensibly-priced companies that possess important long-term competitive advantages, conventional diversification makes no sense for you" (1993). He put about 40% of the Partnership into American Express after the 1963 salad-oil scandal (1994). In 1998: "If you really know businesses, you probably shouldn't own more than six of them… Very few people have gotten rich on their seventh best idea. But a lot of people have gotten rich with their best idea."
- **Bet on the jockey; align the incentives; eat your own cooking.** "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" (2024). "We do not use options or other one-sided forms of compensation; if you lose money, so do we" (2024); "alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside. Many 'alignment' plans flunk this basic test, being artful forms of 'heads I win, tails you lose'" (1994). He adds a caution on reading people: "People are not that easy to read. Sincerity and empathy can easily be faked" (2023), and an 1863 line from Hugh McCulloch: "Never deal with a rascal under the expectation that you can prevent him from cheating you."
- **Delegate by handing over the baton.** On Lou Simpson (GEICO equities, 20.3% a year from 1980 to 2004 against 13.5% for the S&P): "When Charlie and I assign responsibility, we truly hand over the baton." "I typically learn of Lou's transactions about ten days after the end of each month. Sometimes, it should be added, I silently disagree with his decisions. But he's usually right" (2004). The 2001 admission: "I manage most of Berkshire's equity portfolio, and my results were poor, just as they have been for several years," while "Lou's performance has been far better than mine."
- **Manager span of control and "merit badges."** "If I have one person reporting to me and he is a lemon, that's one too many, and if I have managers like those we now have, the number can be almost unlimited" (1995). "We distribute merit badges, not lottery tickets: In none of Berkshire's subsidiaries do we relate compensation to our stock price" (1997). Eddie Bennett, the batboy who joined winning teams, is the model: "to be a winner, work with winners" and "My job is to stay out of their way and allocate whatever excess capital their businesses generate. It's easy work" (2002). Managers are asked to run their companies "as if these are the sole asset of their families and will remain so for the next century" (1999); no show-and-tell presentations, no budgets to be approved by headquarters (1999).
- **Reputation is the bright line / the front-page test.** Taking over scandal-hit Salomon in 1991: "Lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding; lose a shred of reputation for the firm, and I will be ruthless." The operational test: before any act, ask whether you would be willing to see it appear "on the front page of [your] local paper" read by "spouses, children, and friends, with the reporting done by an informed and critical reporter" (1991 testimony). He asked "every Salomon employee to be his or her own compliance officer."
- **Equal-information rule and Friday-night disclosure.** No earnings guidance and no one-on-one calls with large holders; "Our goal is to have all of our owners updated at the same time" (Owner's Manual). Financial data goes out late Friday or early Saturday: "There is no one more important to us than the shareholder of limited means who trusts us with a substantial portion of his savings" (2015).
- **Do not let a headline stand for the whole letter.** On the 2009 letter's one-line sentence that got half-quoted ("We are certain, for example, that the economy will be in shambles throughout 2009 – and probably well beyond – but that conclusion does not tell us whether the market will rise or fall"), the media quoted only the first half; the Dow closed the day of the letter at 7,063 and finished the year at 10,428: "I regard this as terrible journalism." The lesson for him: "Those who invest only when commentators are upbeat end up paying a heavy price for meaningless reassurance" (2009).
- **Bad-news culture.** "Just tell me the bad news; the good news will take care of itself" (Munger's dictum, quoted 1995). (His 2004 whistleblower-line admission is under Governance.) In 2017, Berkshire's hotline received "about 4,000 complaints of one sort or another," routed to the head of internal audit, anonymous with "no retribution": "a pretty good percentage of the time it lead to something pretty big."
- **Know when to shut down vs. subsidize a weak business.** "I won't close down businesses of sub-normal profitability merely to add a fraction of a point to our corporate rate of return. However, I also feel it inappropriate for even an exceptionally profitable company to fund an operation once it appears to have unending losses in prospect. Adam Smith would disagree with my first proposition, and Karl Marx would disagree with my second; the middle ground is the only position that leaves me comfortable" (1985, closing the textile mill). (The 1969 Partnership refusal to liquidate is under Pivot vs. persevere.) The Owner's Manual's statement of the exception: closed in the mid-1980s "after 20 years of struggling" only because it was "doomed to run never-ending operating losses."
- **Know when to stop playing a game you no longer understand.** The 1969 Partnership wind-down: "I am not attuned to this market environment and I don't want to spoil a decent record by trying to play a game I don't understand just so I can go out a hero." The same discipline drives his 2023 crypto abstention. The 2023 letter applies the same honesty to scale: "Outside the U.S., there are essentially no candidates."

## Positions by decision type

Documented position per concrete situation, each with a source; inference is marked. The acquisition stories and the named-mistake ledger are given in full because they are the payload.

### Pivot vs. persevere (when to kill a business)

Distinguish a temporary rough patch from a structurally doomed one; subsidize neither indefinitely.
- **Berkshire textiles (1965 to 1985).** He funded it long past sense: "I wanted the business to succeed and wished my way into a series of bad decisions… wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in business" (2012). He shut it in 1985 admitting he "should be faulted for not quitting sooner." In December 1969 he had explicitly refused to liquidate: "I have no desire to trade severe human dislocations for a few percentage points additional return per annum" (about 1,100 employees), though compulsory additional investment or sustained operating losses "might have to be" a different decision (BPL Dec 1969). In 2017 he still counted the human cost: "it wasn't any fun eventually getting rid of 2,000 people working at Berkshire Textile Mills" (CNBC May 2017), and asked about his worst trade he answered "Yeah, but I have plenty of other competitors" (meaning Berkshire itself). The exchange on textiles: "What kind of an idiot would go into textiles in New England?" (Munger) and "The guy on your right" (Buffett).
- **Waumbec Mills (1979).** He expanded the textile commitment by buying Waumbec (Manchester, NH) below working capital and "got very substantial amounts of machinery and real estate for less than nothing." "But the purchase was a mistake. While we labored mightily, new problems arose as fast as old problems were tamed." Lesson: "Both our operating and investment experience cause us to conclude that 'turnarounds' seldom turn" (see Mental models for the full quote and the dated tension with 1989 and 2023).
- **Dexter Shoe (1993, 1999, 2001, 2014, 2015).** He called shoes "a cyclical problem, not a secular one" in 1995 and was wrong; the 2001 letter lists the three decisions that hurt: "(1) buying it in the first place; (2) paying for it with stock and (3) procrastinating when the need for changes in its operations was obvious. I would like to lay these mistakes on Charlie (or anyone else, for that matter) but they were mine." In 2000 he charged off all the remaining Dexter accounting goodwill ("We may regain some economic goodwill at Dexter in the future, but we clearly have none at present"). The 2014 verdict is the dollar figure (of the stock used to pay for it: "shares I used for the purchase are now worth about $5.7 billion. As a financial disaster, this one deserves a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records") and the 2015 letter adds the human cost: "When low-cost competition drove shoe production to Asia, our once-prosperous Dexter operation folded, putting 1,600 employees in a small Maine town out of work."
- **Newspapers (2006).** The Buffalo News lost its moat; he will "stick with the News" unless he faces "an irreversible cash drain," and expects "non-economic individual buyers" to appear ("Aspiring press lords should be careful").
- **Controlled businesses in general.** He "almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems" (2024); the Owner's Manual says good businesses are never sold "regardless of price," even though this "hurts our financial performance," and the 2016 edition clarifies this is about controlled businesses, "not to marketable securities."
- **Turn distress into a deal only when the structure and the operator are fixed (Fruit of the Loom, FINOVA, 2001).** See Acquisition stories.

### How to price (your own product, underwriting, a retail margin)

Price to exposure, never to volume or recent experience.
- **Insurance.** Price to exposure, not recent experience: the 9/11 admission and the three underwriting rules are under Insurance, float and underwriting (2001). GEICO prices to pass savings through rather than to maximize margin: in 1997 its underwriting margin was 8.1% against a deliberate 4% target, so rates were cut; in 1998 the margin was 6.7%, "better than we had anticipated" (and better than he wished, my paraphrase), so rates fell 3.3% with more cuts planned (1997, 1998).
- **Retail.** Mrs. Blumkin's rule: "If you have the lowest price, customers will find you at the bottom of a river" (2003). Borsheim's gross margin was "fully twenty percentage points below that of its major rivals" (2004). See's raises prices without losing the franchise, but he worries about volume: in 1983 same-store pounds were down 0.8% and about 8% cumulatively since 1979, and he refuses to cut quality: "We regard product quality as sacred" (1983).
- **A newspaper's news hole.** He refuses to cut the Buffalo News's 50% news hole for margin (page for page it gave readers over 25% more news than the typical paper), and treats a newspaper's penetration ratio as the best measure of its franchise strength; the News ranked number one in weekday penetration among the 100 largest U.S. papers and its Sunday circulation went from 270,000 (1976) to 376,000 (1984) (1983, paraphrase).

### When to sell a winner

Almost never. "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes" (1996). Selling to lock gains forfeits the tax-deferred compounding engine; "one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable" (2023). A senior security that matures is not "sold" (1995). He names his own reverse mistakes with the price tag attached:
- **GEICO, 1951 to 1952.** At 20 he took the train to Washington on a Saturday in January 1951, pounded on a closed door until a custodian appeared, and spent "four hours or so" with Lorimer Davidson learning why direct marketing gave GEICO a cost advantage agents could not copy: "After my session with Davy, I was more excited about GEICO than I have ever been about a stock." He bought 350 shares for $10,282 (by year end worth $13,125, more than 65% of his net worth; he already had more than 50% in it), then sold all in 1952 for $15,259 to switch into Western Insurance Securities at "slightly more than one times its current earnings." "But in the next 20 years, the GEICO stock I sold grew in value to about $1.3 million, which taught me a lesson about the inadvisability of selling a stake in an identifiably-wonderful company" (1995).
- **Disney, 1966 to 1967.** In 1966 Disney's market value was under $90M, pre-tax earnings about $21M, more cash than debt, with the $17M Pirates of the Caribbean ride about to open: "a company selling at only five times rides!" The Partnership bought a significant amount at a split-adjusted $31. "In 1967 I sold out at 48 per share." And: "That decision may appear brilliant, given that the stock now sells for $66. But your Chairman was up to the task of nullifying it" (1995).
- **Capital Cities, 1993.** "don't later be as dumb as I was two years ago when I sold one-third of our holdings in Cap Cities for $635 million (versus the $1.27 billion those shares would bring in the Disney merger)" (1995). Baseline: he calls himself a "repeat offender" (1994).
- **McDonald's, 1998.** "a very big mistake… you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off to the movies during market hours" (1998). In the 1998 classroom he had judged it "not as good a business as Coke. There really hardly are any."
- **The bubble top, 2000 to 2004.** "I made a big mistake in not selling several of our larger holdings during The Great Bubble. If these stocks are fully priced now, you may wonder what I was thinking four years ago when their intrinsic value was lower and their prices far higher. So do I" (2003). A year later: "I can properly be criticized for merely clucking about nose-bleed valuations during the Bubble rather than acting on my views... I talked when I should have walked," and "Our huge positions add to the difficulty of our nimbly dancing in and out of holdings as valuations swing" (2004).
- **Selling when relative undervaluation is gone (Commonwealth Trust, 1958).** The mirror case: he sold the block at $80 when a better special situation appeared, though the quoted market was about 20% lower, because a buyer at $80 (intrinsic $135) "can expect to do quite well" but that is "quite different from a price $50 with an intrinsic value of $125" (BPL 1958).

### When to buy aggressively

When others are fearful and you have cash. Oct 16, 2008 NYT op-ed "Buy American. I Am.": "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful"; "Bad news is an investor's best friend. It lets you buy a slice of America's future at a marked-down price." Executed live: the GE $3B and Goldman $5B preferred-plus-warrants deals at the 2008 trough: "when people talk about cash being king, it's not king if it just sits there" (Charlie Rose 2008).
- **Reach for a bucket, not a thimble (2009).** Corporate and municipal bonds were "ridiculously cheap relative to U.S. Treasuries" in 2008 to 2009; Berkshire bought some. Self-criticism: "I should have done far more. Big opportunities come infrequently." Numbers: $44.3B of cash entering 2008, plus $17B of retained operating earnings, down to $30.6B at year end 2009 ($8B earmarked for BNSF). Five non-traded positions (Dow, GE, Goldman, Swiss Re, Wrigley) cost $21.1B, carried at $26.0B, yielding $2.1B a year. "A climate of fear is their best friend."
- **Junk bonds, 2002.** After avoiding them, he bought about $8 billion in 2002; by 2003 "Yesterday's weeds are today being priced as flowers." The 2002 commitments "sextupled, reaching $8.3 billion"; equity gains had exceeded losses by about 100 to 1 over 38 years but junk issuers are "overloaded with debt," so "we expect that we will have occasional large losses" (2002, 2003). Rule: "We have never purchased a newly-issued junk bond, which is the only kind most investors are urged to buy" (2001).
- **Wells Fargo, 1990** (see Margin of safety); **Level 3 euro bonds (2001)**: bought €254 million at 51.7% of par, sold at 85% in December 2004; of a $163M gain, about $85M came from the "market's revised opinion about Level 3's credit quality" and $78M from the euro (2004).
- **Washington Public Power System bonds (1984), Chrysler Financial, Texaco, RJR Nabisco** as earlier distressed-credit episodes (2001 letter).
- **In-between years:** "If stocks become significantly cheaper than entire businesses, we will buy them aggressively" and "Under any market or economic conditions, we will be happy to buy businesses that meet our standards. And, for those that do, the bigger the better" (2003). A 1974 precedent: after an insurance fraud of unknown size Berkshire "kept more funds in cash-equivalents than we normally would have" and so bought fewer stocks that were "extraordinarily cheap" (2003, citing the cost of caution).

### When to hold cash vs. deploy

Hold cash rather than overpay; "We have made a lot of money by not wanting to be fully invested at all times" (2025 meeting, [third-party transcript](https://boersenbrief.privatinvestor.de/?p=13783)). Heading into 2026 Berkshire held a record roughly $334 to $397B of cash and T-bills. He downplays it ("the great majority of your money remains in equities," 2024) and frames the pile as ballast plus optionality for a large deal.
- **Dated history of the pile.** 1998: "Cash never makes us happy. But it's better to have the money burning a hole in Berkshire's pocket than resting comfortably in someone else's" (more than $15B). 2004: $43B, "not a happy position," and "I struck out" on multi-billion acquisitions. 2017: about $95B at about 1%, no significant acquisition for about 15 months, and leveraged buyers using 1% money can outbid an all-equity buyer. 2019: $114.2B, with $1.7B of buybacks in the quarter (Reuters wire via Daily Herald; a critic's line: "This much cash is certainly a drag"). His 2019 answer on why not an index fund: "I would rather own an index fund than carry Treasury bills" on balance, yet the cash is for speed: "there are conditions under which we could spend a hundred billion dollars very, very quickly," and Munger noted Harvard plunged its cash into markets "at exactly the wrong moment."
- **The cost, in his words.** "Charlie and I mainly just wait for the phone to ring" (1998); "Lethargy bordering on sloth" (1990). The 2003 line: idle cash is "a painful condition to be in" but "not as painful as doing something stupid."

### Raise vs. bootstrap / leverage

Use debt sparingly. "We use debt sparingly and, when we do borrow, we attempt to structure our loans on a long-term fixed-rate basis" (Owner's Manual). The sharper lines from the same document: "I've never believed in risking what my family and friends have and need in order to pursue what they don't have and don't need"; "We will reject interesting opportunities rather than over-leverage our balance sheet"; "(As one of the Indianapolis '500' winners said: 'To finish first, you must first finish.')" He admits conservatism "has penalized our results."
- **Dated exceptions he carves out.** 2004 and 2005: "we believe in using borrowed money to support profitable, interest-bearing receivables." Berkshire borrowed $2B at the start of 2004 and relent it to Clayton Homes at "cost plus one percentage point" (a $7.35B balance by January 2005), because "having a rich daddy can lead to sloppy decisions" and each unit stood on its own bottom (his own image: every tub); the securitization market had "dried up" and Berkshire's balance sheet was the edge. The 2005 letter lists three narrow uses of debt (short-term repos on liquid government paper, borrowing against understood receivables like Clayton's loans or Finova, and MidAmerican's non-guaranteed debt) and prefers "better profits to better cosmetics" over securitization. "(Debt is a four-letter word around Berkshire.)" (2001).
- **Utilities and the railroad.** Debt is concentrated in utilities and BNSF, non-recourse to Berkshire, long-term fixed rate (Owner's Manual). The 2023 fortress terms: "Berkshire does not currently pay dividends, and its share repurchases are 100% discretionary. Annual debt maturities are never material."
- **Yen debt (2019 to 2023).** The Japanese trading-house stakes (Itochu, Marubeni, Mitsubishi, Mitsui, Sumitomo; about 9% each, cost ¥1.6 trillion, year-end market value ¥2.9 trillion, a $8 billion unrealized gain) were financed with ¥1.3 trillion of yen bonds. "Neither Greg nor I believe we can forecast market prices of major currencies. We also don't believe we can hire anyone with this ability" (2023). The same logic dated to 1998: "can borrow money in Japan for 10 years at one percent… all I have to do is beat one percent," but Japanese businesses "earn very low returns on equity - 4% to 5% - 6%" (rbcpa 1998), so he found nothing then.
- **Personal.** "We basically never borrow money. I never borrowed money even when I had $10,000" (rbcpa 1998). On smart people and debt: "Every smart guy is tempted by leverage" and "some of them are broken by it," with Munger the near-miss (CNBC May 2017).

### Focus vs. diversify (advice to others and to himself)

For non-experts: total diversification at minimal cost. His will instructs the trustee: "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. (I suggest Vanguard's.)" (2013), and he won a 10-year, $1M bet that such a fund would beat hedge funds-of-funds. "By periodically investing in an index fund… the know-nothing investor can actually out-perform most investment professionals" (1993). "98% - 99% who invest should extensively diversify and not trade" (rbcpa 1998). For those who genuinely know what they own, the opposite.
- **Ground Rule 7 as an expected-value calculation (BPL Nov 1965 and Jan 1966).** "We diversify substantially less than most investment operations. We might invest up to 40% of our net worth in a single security under conditions coupling an extremely high probability that our facts and reasoning are correct with a very low probability that anything could drastically change the underlying value of the investment." The reasoning: with 50 uncorrelated ideas each worth 15 points over the Dow he would put 2% in each, but "We have to work extremely hard to find just a very few attractive investment situations." Position sizing is set so any single position (or correlated group) has only a "tiny" probability of making the whole portfolio more than ten points worse than the Dow. "any portfolio encompassing one hundred stocks (whether the manager is handling one thousand dollars or one billion dollars) is not being operated logically." "Noah School of Investing - two of everything. Such investors should be piloting arks." And Billy Rose's line: "You've got a harem of seventy girls; you don't get to know any of them very well."
- **Concentration in practice: the 40% position (BPL 1966 to 1967).** He hit the 40% limit in 1966 to 1967 with one "relatively undervalued" holding (a $14,096,593 gain on about $19.5M of average capital), at the cost he accepted: "The sort of concentration we have in this category is bound to produce wide swings in short term performance," and "I am willing to give up quite a bit in terms of leveling of year-to-year results ... in order to achieve better overall long-term performance." 1965 results were "overwhelmingly the product of five investment situations."
- **The Schloss exception (2006).** Walter Schloss built his record "by investing in about 1,000 securities, mostly of a lackluster type. A few big winners did not account for his success" ("Edwin replied, 'We try to buy stocks cheap.' So much for Modern Portfolio Theory, technical analysis, macroeconomic thoughts and complex algorithms"). Buffett's own six-stock rule coexists with it: "I don't diversify personally... with the exception of Walter Schloss, Walter diversifies a lot. I call him Noah, he has two of everything" (rbcpa 1998).

### Estate, succession and giving

Don't rule from the grave; hand the money and the trust to people, re-earned and not inherited.
- **Dated evolution of the plan.** 2005: "Every share of Berkshire that I own is destined to go to philanthropies." Owner's Manual (later edition): the job splits into one CEO for operations and one or more executives for investments; the Buffett family not in management, but a non-paid, non-executive family chairman; on death "None of my stock will have to be sold" and all shares go to foundations over about a dozen years. 2006: five foundations, with Berkshire proceeds disbursed "within ten years after my estate is closed," so over "the next 25 years or so" ("Taxes, I should note, had nothing to do with my decision or its timing"); the argument against perpetuity: "These managerial attributes sometimes wane as institutions - particularly those that are exempt from market forces - age." **[CHECK, resolved]** The Nov 2025 letter supersedes the Owner's Manual text: shares redirected to four family foundations run by his three children, and a keep-the-A-shares stance "until Berkshire shareholders develop the comfort with Greg [Abel] that Charlie and I long enjoyed." The older passage describes a plan, not his current one.
- **Successor selection.** Munger flagged the successors years early: Abel and Jain would be "under-described as 'world-class'" (Munger, 2014). 2005: the board "unanimously agreed on the person who should succeed me"; three young candidates are each "much better at certain management aspects of my job"; the investment job would be split ("another person in the organization handle marketable securities").
- **Successor selection, the investment job (2006, 2023).** 2006: plan to "hire a younger man or woman with the potential to manage a very large portfolio," with the criteria "There is far more to successful long-term investing than brains and performance that has recently been good… A single, big mistake could wipe out a long string of successes. We therefore need someone genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks, including those never before encountered… Temperament is also important." On pay, he notes the retention problem: "Being able to list Berkshire on a resume would materially enhance the marketability of an investment manager." 2023: Abel "in all respects is ready to be CEO of Berkshire tomorrow." Decay clause (2005): "When their abilities ebb, so usually do their powers of self-assessment. Someone else often needs to blow the whistle."
- **The pledge, with its arithmetic (Fortune 2006, Giving Pledge 2010).** In June 2006 he told Carol Loomis he would start giving away 85% of his Berkshire fortune, most of it to the Gates Foundation (Fortune's summary), and restated the family rule he had given Fortune in 1986: "a very rich person should leave his kids enough to do anything but not enough to do nothing" (2006). The 2010 pledge: "More than 99% of my wealth will go to philanthropy during my lifetime or at death." The mechanism is the stock itself: "take a pile of Berkshire Hathaway stock certificates—“claim checks” that when converted to cash can command far-ranging resources—and commit them to benefit others who, through the luck of the draw, have received the short straws in life," because "Were we to use more than 1% of my claim checks on ourselves, neither our happiness nor our well-being would be enhanced." The justification is luck: "Both my children and I won what I call the ovarian lottery" (2010).
- **Giving through the company, and why it stopped (2001, 2003).** The 2003 letter records the end of the Designated Gifts Program (1981 to 2002, $197 million directed by shareholders): boycotts over abortion-related designations began to hurt independent associates of Pampered Chef. "Charlie and I see nothing charitable in harming decent, hard-working people just so we and other shareholders can gain some minor tax efficiencies." Berkshire now "makes no contributions at the parent company level." In 2001 he had defended it: "In neither the purchase of goods nor the hiring of personnel, do we ever consider the religious views, the gender, the race or the sexual orientation of the persons we are dealing with. It would not only be wrong to do so, it would be idiotic."

### When to acquire (policy), and the stories behind it

Prefer friendly cash purchases of whole businesses run by managers who want to keep running them, evaluated "on an all-equity basis… We also never factor in, nor do we often find, synergies" (2017). The method is passive and referral-driven: "Our acquisition technique at Berkshire is simplicity itself: We answer the phone" (2000); "At Berkshire, our carefully-crafted acquisition strategy is simply to wait for the phone to ring" (1999); "We buy to keep" and have no "exit" strategy (2002). On hostile deals, even justified ones: "We, though, will leave these 'opportunities' for others. At Berkshire, we go only where we are welcome" (2015). Because Berkshire analyzes all purchases on an all-equity basis, when LBO financing collapsed in 2000 "our evaluations did not change, which means we became considerably more competitive" (2000).

**What stock-based and auction-based deals cost (the policy side).**
- **Stock-for-stock: "when I've issued stock, I've cost you money" (1997).** The rule dates to 1982: "we will not issue shares unless we receive as much intrinsic business value as we give. Such a policy might seem axiomatic. Why, you might ask, would anyone issue dollar bills in exchange for fifty-cent pieces?" (1982). Reviewed action in 1997: every Berkshire stock-only merger (excluding Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip Stamps) left owners "slightly worse off" than no deal, while the cash deals (National Indemnity 1967, See's, Buffalo News, Scott Fetzer, GEICO) did "incredibly well." Reason: Berkshire's roster is full of ".380 hitters," so a deal adding a ".350 hitter" requires trading one. "Though it hurts me to say it, when I've issued stock, I've cost you money." A premium makes sense only if "a) its stock is overvalued relative to the acquiree's or b) the two enterprises will earn more combined than they would separately."
- **Advisers will justify any foolish acquisition (1997).** "If a CEO is enthused about a particularly foolish acquisition, both his internal staff and his outside advisors will come up with whatever projections are needed to justify his stance. Only in fairy tales are emperors told that they are naked."
- **Stock deals sell part of everything you own; the tax ranking (2005, 1999).** In 2005: "When a management proudly acquires another company for stock, the shareholders of the acquirer are concurrently selling part of their interest in everything they own," adding that he has made such deals himself and on balance they cost shareholders money. Tax ranking (1999): an asset purchase for cash is best and "the worst deal of all is a stock-for-stock acquisition"; "We have never done an inefficiently-structured deal, however, in order to make our figures look better."
- **Kissing toads: why acquirers pay double (1981).** Acquirers pay a 2X takeover price for a target the market prices at X because managers are sure their "managerial kiss will do wonders for the profitability of Company T(arget)." His answer: "investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads. If investors instead bankroll princesses who wish to pay double for the right to kiss the toad, those kisses had better pack some real dynamite." The exceptions he allowed were acquirers of businesses able to raise prices easily under inflation and to absorb large volume increases with little added capital (1981).
- **The bank and the Chihuahua (2009).** A bank Berkshire held, long barred from acquisitions by statute, got the law changed and its managers "began to behave like teenage boys who had just discovered girls." The target, a smaller similar bank, wanted nearly three times book in stock while the acquirer traded near book: "the owner of the small bank became richer, we became poorer." Munger's line: "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?" Management's rationalization: "We need to show that we are in the hunt. Besides, it's only a small deal." His fix: "Directors should hire a second advisor to make the case against the proposed acquisition, with its fee contingent on the deal not going through" and, failing that, "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut" (2009).
- **Acquirer's returns versus price paid** is in Mental models (21.7% on tangible net worth, 9.9% on carrying value, 2004).

**Berkshire-era acquisitions, in rough order (situation, action, quote, source year).**
- **Illinois National Bank (1969) and the forced sale (1979).** Bought in 1969 for the Rockford bank; the Bank Holding Company Act required divestiture by Dec 31, 1980. In 1969 Boston's First National answered his $10M request at once ("they would supply the money for our purchase and that we would work out the details later") while a New York bank never responded; in 2001 FleetBoston did the same for Berkadia: "you've got the money and we'll work out the details later" (2001). The bank "earned approximately 2.3% on average assets last year, a level again over three times that achieved by the average major bank." Gene Abegg, then 82, ran it. The Fed held that in a spin-off no Berkshire officer or director could serve at the bank.
- **Selling Illinois National: choose the buyer, accept the cost (1979).** Forced by the Bank Holding Company Act, his decision rule for the sale: "We will be most choosy about any purchaser, and our selection will not be based solely on price. The bank and its management have treated us exceptionally well and, if we have to sell, we want to be sure that they are treated equally as well." And the cost: "we do not expect to be able to fully, or even in very large part, replace the earning power represented by the bank from the proceeds of the sale of the bank. You simply can't buy high quality businesses at the sort of price/earnings multiple likely to prevail on our bank sale" (1979).
- **Blue Chip Stamps (1970), the control purchase that went to zero.** Sales $126M, about 60 billion stamps licked, a 116-page rewards catalog: "When I was told that even certain brothels and mortuaries gave stamps to their patrons, I felt I had finally found a sure thing." Sales fell to $19.4M (1980), $1.5M (1990), then "fell another 98%"; in 2006 "all of $25,920 (no zeros omitted) came from Blue Chip." "No quitter, I redoubled my managerial efforts" (2006). (Blue Chip's float funded See's, Wesco and the Buffalo News; the 1983 appendix prices the See's piece.)
- **Nebraska Furniture Mart (1983).** Bought 90% from Rose Blumkin ("Mrs. B"), then 90, who had arrived from Russia, had no formal education and no English, and started in 1937 with $500 saved from selling used clothing; sales "over $100 million" from one 200,000 square foot store, more than all Omaha competitors combined. He left 10% with the family in management and optioned 10% to key young family managers; Mrs. B stayed chairman and kept selling carpet seven days a week. The deal was "quickly made" because he had "admired both the family and the business for decades." Why the model works: "built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners." Character evidence: when early resources ran out she sold furniture from her home "to pay creditors precisely as promised," and after proving she could profitably sell carpet at a huge discount she "sold the judge $1400 worth of carpet." He quotes Pascal on being unable to stay quietly in one room and adds "Even Pascal would have left the room for Mrs. Blumkin" (1983). The 'Berkshire Weekend' sales at NFM went from $5.3M (1997) to $25.1M (2004) and $30M (2006).
- **Scott Fetzer (1985): the anti-auction.** A major banker had failed to sell it; after Buffett wrote to CEO Ralph Schey, "I had never met Ralph, but within a week we had a deal." The engagement letter still owed the bank $2.5 million and the banker offered the sales "book"; Munger answered "I'll pay $2.5 million not to read it" (1999). Net price $230 million; Schey (hired at 61) ran it 15 years and "the company distributed $1.03 billion to Berkshire" (2000).
- **GEICO (1976 to 1996).** In the early 1970s management "made some serious errors in estimating their claims costs," which "almost caused it to go bankrupt"; Jack Byrne became CEO in 1976 (1995). "Because I believed both in Jack and in GEICO's fundamental competitive strength, Berkshire purchased a large interest": $45.7M for 33.3% by year end 1980, no purchases for 15 years, and a stake that grew to about 50% through GEICO's own buybacks. In 1995 Berkshire agreed to pay $2.3B for the other half. "That is a steep price," justified by the same moat, Tony Nicely (insurance) and Lou Simpson (investments, 22.8% a year from 1980 to 1995 against 15.7% for the S&P); expense ratio 23.6% of premiums (1995).
- **GEICO's customer-acquisition economics (1999).** Marketing spend rose from $33M and 652 phone counselors (1995) to $143M and 2,162 counselors (1998) and $242M and 2,631 counselors (1999); "we would happily commit $1 billion annually to marketing if we knew we could handle the business smoothly and if we expected the last dollar spent to produce new business at an attractive cost." Advertising buys "a direct relationship with a huge number of households that, on average, will send us $1,100 year after year"; in 1999 customers added 766,256 and GEICO "gained $590 million of cash from operating earnings and the increase in float."
- **GEICO's two-variable incentive plan and the advertising ceiling (1999, 2004, 2015).** Compensation is on two variables only: policyholder growth and profit on seasoned (more than one year old) business, so, as he told associates, "we will foot the bill for new business"; profit sharing was 28.4% of salary ($113.3M) in 1999 and 24.3% ($191M) in 2004. The 1999 limit he named: "inquiries per ad fall for all of us" as advertising rises, and about 40% of people checking rates find they can save; the referral floor, "we probably could maintain our policy count by spending as little as $50 million annually." Also an insurance-industry history lesson (2004, 2015): "Though the early bird gets the worm, the second mouse gets the cheese," and "It was clear to me that GEICO would succeed because it deserved to succeed."
- **Gillette preferred (1989) and the Salomon and USAir convertibles (1987 to 1989).** See the Named mistakes ledger.
- **Helzberg's Diamond Shops (1995): acquisitions by walking around.** A week after the 1994 meeting, Buffett crossed 58th and Fifth in New York when Barnett Helzberg Jr., who owned four Berkshire shares, stopped him; Helzberg's had 134 stores in 23 states, founded in 1915, with sales of $10M (1974), $53M (1984) and $282M (1994). Barnett, 60, wanted diversification. Berkshire bought it in 1995 in a tax-free stock exchange, the only structure Barnett wanted, and "we would not have bought the business if Jeff [Comment] had not been there to run it." "Buying a retailer without good management is like buying the Eiffel Tower without an elevator." Screening rule: when people say they have a business, "it usually turns out they have a lemonade stand," so he only asked for particulars (1995). Ben Bridge Jeweler followed in 2000, agreed "by phone, prior to any face-to-face meeting" on referral from Barnett Helzberg, with same-store sales +9%, +11%, +13%, +10%, +12%, +21% and +7% over seven years (2000).
- **R.C. Willey (1995, 1999, 2001, 2004, 2005).** Irv Blumkin told Bill Child of the Blumkin family's good Berkshire experience; in early 1995 Child mentioned estate-tax and diversification reasons to sell. Child took over from his father-in-law in 1954 at sales of about $250,000; 1995 sales were $257M, over 50% of Utah furniture. Process: "Bill sent me some figures, and I wrote him a letter indicating my idea of value. We quickly agreed on a number, and found our personal chemistry to be perfect" (1995).
- **R.C. Willey's Boise and Las Vegas stores: the founder's capital at risk (1999 to 2004).** In 1997 Child wanted a Boise store and offered to build it at his own risk ($9M in the 1999 letter, $11M in the 2001 letter; the discrepancy is in the sources) and sell it at cost; Buffett offered to take the downside and "Bill said nothing doing." Opened August 1999, "immediately became a huge success"; Child transferred it at cost and "refused to take a dime of interest on the capital he had tied up over the two years." Las Vegas then "outsells all others in the R.C. Willey chain" at "twice what I had anticipated" (2001); a second store 20 minutes away that Buffett predicted would cannibalize ("In truth, I thought they were mistakes") does 26% more volume than any other store in the chain (2004). "Today, when I pontificate about retailing, Berkshire people just say, 'What does Bill think?'" (2001). NFM's new Kansas City store "will keep this store open on Sundays" (2001).
- **Star Furniture (1997) and Jordan's (1999).** Star (Houston, Melvyn Wolff and Shirley Toomin, 12 stores, family since 1924) was done "in a single, two-hour session" in New York: "I had no need to check leases, work out employment contracts, etc. I knew I was dealing with a man of integrity and that's what counted" (closed July 1, 1997). Jordan's (Tatelman brothers, family business since 1927, "shoppertainment," the highest sales per square foot of any major U.S. furniture operation) was found by the "any more at home like you?" chain. Berkshire bought assets for cash so goodwill amortizes over 15 years for tax, "a procedure that will maximize the cash produced for our shareholders but minimize the earnings we report to you" (1999).
- **International Dairy Queen (1998).** 5,792 stores in 23 countries; trigger: an owner (Luther) had died and his estate needed to sell; a cash-or-Berkshire-stock choice patterned on the FlightSafety deal and tilted toward cash (only 45% chose cash). "We have put our money where our mouth is": Buffett was a regular at the Omaha DQ (1997). Decision pattern: he buys what he uses.
- **NetJets (1998, $725M).** Buffett was a customer first: Frank Rooney recommended it; "It took Rich about 15 minutes to sell me a quarter (200 hours annually) of a Hawker 1000"; his family flew 900 hours on 300 trips; he made a testimonial ad "long before I knew there was any possibility of our purchasing the business" and asked Rich Santulli to call if he ever wanted to sell. Santulli phoned in May 1998. Moat logic: the largest operator (1,000 customers, 163 aircraft, 31% of corporate jet orders in 1997) sells multiple manufacturers' planes while two rivals "prescribe" their own house brand; FlightSafety supplies pilot training. He sold the Berkshire plane. "Alice, you aren't buying it; your heirs are" (the Aunt Alice and fur coat joke) (1998). On selection: "I viewed the selection of a flight provider as akin to picking a brain surgeon: you simply want the best" (2004); "Once a customer has tried us, going back to commercial aviation is like going back to holding hands" (2003). The later losses are in the ledger.
- **General Re (1998, $22B).** The largest U.S. property-casualty reinsurer (plus 82% of Cologne Re). Berkshire took over the investment portfolio but not underwriting; asked Gen Re to retain more of its business: "Berkshire, in contrast, happily accepts volatility, just as long as it carries with it the expectation of increased profits over time," and "we are a Fort Knox of capital, and that means volatile earnings can't impair our premier credit ratings." After closing, Gen Re dumped about 250 stock positions, incurring $935 million of taxes, because "We don't back into decisions" (1998). He bought it with minimal due diligence (the "home office" line under Decision heuristics, U. Florida talk, Oct 1998; the letter gives $22 billion at closing). **[CHECK, dated]** The 1998 optimism ("Berkshire can add absolutely nothing to the skills of General Re's and Cologne Re's managers") became, in 2001 to 2003, the admission that Gen Re was "grossly mispricing" and underreserved; see Named mistakes.
- **MidAmerican Energy (1999 to 2005).** Walter Scott Jr. introduced it at a California conference in September 1999; Buffett read public reports and "had two short meetings with Walter and David Sokol," then said he would deal "at an appropriate price." The structure was forced by the 1935 holding-company act: an 11% fixed-income security, common stock and exchangeable preferred that gave Berkshire "just under 10% of the voting power of MidAmerican but about 76% of the equity interest," about $2B in all, with Walter and family buying about $280M of stock for cash (1999). In 2003 Berkshire owned 80.5% (fully diluted), voting limited to 9.9% under PUHCA, with $1.578B of junior debt owed to Berkshire at 11%; the U.K. unit went from 6,800 employees at denationalization (March 31, 1990) to 2,539 serving the same customers (2003).
- **Northern Natural Gas (2002): the Enron pipeline bought in three days.** An Enron history (1985 InterNorth merged with Houston Natural Gas on a promise to stay in Omaha; Ken Lay renamed it Enron and moved it to Houston within a year); Enron put it up as collateral for a Dynegy loan; Dynegy wanted "a quick and certain cash sale"; call Friday July 26, contract July 29, "Dynegy phoned the right party" (2002).
- **Kern River, PacifiCorp and the turn to capital-intensive utilities (2002 to 2009).** Kern River ($1.2B expansion) and PacifiCorp (2005, $3.4B of MidAmerican stock plus $1.7B of borrowing): "You can't expect to earn outsized profits in regulated utilities, but the industry offers owners the opportunity to deploy large sums at fair returns" (2005). The 2009 shift: "In earlier days, Charlie and I shunned capital-intensive businesses such as public utilities," now accepted for the expectation of "ever-increasing amounts of cash" and "reasonable expectations of earning decent returns on the incremental sums they invest" (2009).
- **The 2000 wave: eight businesses, $8 billion, 97% cash.** CORT Business Services: a one-page fax on Nov 23, 1999 from airplane broker Bruce Cort with a Washington Post article on a failed buyout; Buffett "printed out its SEC filings" that day, met CEO Paul Arnold Nov 29, and bought through Wesco for about $386M cash: "a fine though unglamorous business, an outstanding manager, and a price (going by that on the failed deal) that made sense." U.S. Liability (introduced by Ron Ferguson of General Re, half stock half cash by phone).
- **Justin, Shaw, Benjamin Moore and Johns Manville (2000).** Justin Industries (boots and Acme brick): a stranger's May 4 fax from Mark Jones; bought for $570M cash; Acme makes over a billion bricks a year in 22 plants, about 11.7% of national output; 75% of Texans name Acme. Shaw Industries: after Buffett refused an uncapped asbestos policy for a prospective merger partner ("we would never issue a policy that lacked a cap"), the carpet maker's owners kept at least 5% and Berkshire owns 87.3%. Benjamin Moore: call from a director he knew from Salomon; "we made a $1 billion cash offer on the spot." Johns Manville: LBO financing collapsed Friday Dec 8; "The following Monday" Buffett and Munger made an all-cash offer with no financing contingencies; trustees voted the next day; about $1.8B.
- **Why sellers pick Berkshire, and the anti-synergy promise (2000).** "We like to do business with someone who loves his company, not just the money that a sale will bring him"; a Rembrandt owner prefers choosing the permanent home to having "a trust officer or uninterested heirs auction it off" (2000). The anti-synergy promise to Ben Bridge's family: "No one wanted another jewelry chain to come in and decimate the organization with ideas about synergy and cost saving (which, though they would never work, were certain to be tried)."
- **Fruit of the Loom (2001), from Graham-Newman in 1955.** Backstory: Buffett was one of five employees of Graham-Newman when Philadelphia and Reading Coal and Iron (P&R) bought Union Underwear (licensee of the Fruit of the Loom name) for $15M, with "Fully $9 million of the remaining $12.5 million due" satisfied by non-interest-bearing notes payable from 50% of any earnings above $1 million: "(Those were the days; I get goosebumps just thinking about such deals.)" Fruit later earned over $200M pre-tax. Why it failed: John Holland ran it in its best years; after he retired in 1996 "management loaded the company with debt, in part to make a series of acquisitions that proved disappointing. Bankruptcy followed."
- **Fruit of the Loom: buying the debt, then the company (2001).** Berkshire bought public and bank debt at about 50% of face early in the bankruptcy (about a 15% current return, reaching 10% of the senior debt, "probably end up returning us about 70% of face value"), then made a whole-company bid "to which we attached no financing conditions, even though our offer had to remain outstanding for many months," with the proviso that "John had to be available to continue serving as CEO after we took over. To us, John and the brand are Fruit's key assets." "What goes around truly does come around" (2001).
- **Acme Boot, P&R's second Graham-Newman-era deal (told in 2001).** Acme Boot, P&R's second deal (price $3.2M, sales $7M, also part paid in non-interest notes), became "the world's largest bootmaker," then "eventually hit the skids and never recovered"; H.H. Brown bought its inventory and trademarks for $700,000 in December 2001. Graham and Dodd quote Horace: "Many shall be restored that now are fallen and many shall fall that are now in honor."
- **FINOVA and Berkadia (2001).** Late 2000 Berkshire began buying FINOVA's obligations ($11B outstanding), 13% "at about two-thirds of face value": "We expected the company to go into bankruptcy, but believed that liquidation of its assets would produce a payoff for creditors that would be well above our cost." With Leucadia, a prepackaged plan paid creditors 70% of face plus full interest and a 7.5% note for the remaining 30%; the vehicle Berkadia borrowed $5.6B from FleetBoston and relent to FINOVA with a priority claim at a spread of about two points (90% Berkshire, 10% Leucadia); Berkshire guaranteed 90% of Berkadia's borrowing; an offer for up to $500M of new bonds was withdrawn when the NYSE closed the week of 9/11. The resulting $6B loan was oversubscribed by 17 banks: "if you ever need $6 billion, just give Chad a call - assuming, that is, your credit is AAA."
- **XTRA and Larson-Juhl (2001).** XTRA was bought by tender offer expiring September 11, 2001, with an "out" if markets closed; its CEO Lew Rubin knew Berkshire could back away while his son-in-law in the World Trade Center was unaccounted for; Berkshire completed the deal. Larson-Juhl: a December 3 call from Craig Ponzio (sales from $3M to $300M since 1981), "in ninety minutes we reached an agreement. In ten days we had signed a contract."
- **The Pampered Chef (2002).** Doris Christopher founded it in 1980, borrowing $3,000 against her life insurance policy ("all the money ever injected into the company"); the first party had $175 of sales, first-year sales $50,000, and 22 years later more than $700M and 67,000 consultants. Sheila O'Connell Cooper was CEO for two years. "It took me about ten seconds to decide that these were two managers with whom I wished to partner" (2002); Buffett attended a party himself. Entry criteria: "economic characteristics ranging from good to great, run by managers ranging from great to great," plus "a sensible purchase price."
- **Clayton Homes (2003), found through a student gift.** Prof. Al Auxier brings about 40 University of Tennessee finance students to Omaha each year; in February 2003 their thank-you gift was Jim Clayton's autobiography. He already knew Clayton was "the class act of the manufactured housing industry" from knowledge acquired "after earlier making the mistake of buying some distressed junk debt of Oakwood Homes": "At the time of that purchase, I did not understand how atrocious consumer-financing practices had become throughout most of the manufactured housing industry. But I learned: Oakwood rather promptly went bankrupt."
- **Clayton Homes: the offer made from a book (2003).** He phoned CEO Kevin Clayton, judged him "both able and a straight-shooter," and "I made an offer for the business based solely on Jim's book, my evaluation of Kevin, the public financials of Clayton and what I had learned from the Oakwood experience." The board was receptive because lenders had fled. The diagnosis: "Much of its volume a few years back came from buyers who shouldn't have bought, financed by lenders who shouldn't have lent." Full circle: Clayton agreed to buy Oakwood's assets, so Berkshire's discounted Oakwood debt likely returned a small profit. Each of the 40 students got a "PhD (for phenomenal, hard-working dealmaker)" and a B share; Auxier got an A share.
- **Clayton Homes after the purchase: financing and lending discipline (2003, 2004, 2015).** The $1.7B acquisition; Berkshire then borrowed $2B and re-lent it to Clayton at a markup (see Raise vs. bootstrap / leverage; 2003, 2004). In 2015: 100% risk retention ("At Clayton, our risk retention was, and is, 100%"), $12.8B of mortgages, average loan $59,942, 8,444 foreclosures at a cost of $157M (a 2.64% rate, 95.4% of borrowers current), 65 regulatory exams producing fines of $38,200 and refunds of $704,678; contrast with the 2008 originate-and-sell chain where "Both the originator and the packager of these financings had no skin in the game and were driven by volume and mark-ups."
- **The 2006 mortgage warning, written from inside Clayton (2006).** He warned a year before the crash about teaser and optional-payment mortgages: "A rolling loan gathers no loss" and "This is the Scarlett O'Hara scenario: 'I'll think about that tomorrow.'" (2006), while Clayton earned $513M pre-tax in an industry at "its lowest unit sales since 1962" because of lending discipline.
- **McLane (2003): $23 billion of sales at about a 1% pre-tax margin.** Banker Byron Trott told him in early spring that Wal-Mart wished to sell. One roughly two-hour meeting with Wal-Mart's CFO Tom Schoewe and a handshake; closed 29 days later: "We did no 'due diligence.'" "We knew everything would be exactly as Wal-Mart said it would be." Rationale: low margin, but CEO Grady Rosier is "superb," and retailers that shunned McLane because it was owned by a competitor may now buy (2003).
- **Forest River (2005) and Business Wire (2005).** Forest River (RV maker, $1.6B sales, Pete Liegl): a two-page fax on June 21, figures the next morning, an offer that afternoon, a handshake on June 28; Liegl's earlier LBO sale had sunk the business into bankruptcy until he repurchased it. "You can be sure that I won't be telling Pete how to manage his operation." Liegl: "It was easier to sell my business than to renew my driver's license." Business Wire: Cathy Baron Tamraz wrote after a Nov 12, 2005 WSJ article ("We run a tight ship and keep unnecessary spending under wraps"); Buffett called and "before long Berkshire had reached agreement" with founder Lorry Lokey (founded 1961, age 78) (2005). Pete Liegl's 2024 tribute appears in the 2024 letter.
- **MiTek (2001, reviewed 2006): Berkshire as "buyer of choice."** MiTek: a "heavy package" containing "an unprepossessing chunk of metal" (a roof-truss connector plate) arrived from CEO Gene Toombs: "It took me only a minute to realize that he was our kind of manager and MiTek our kind of business." Bought for $420M ($200M loan at 9% plus $198M of stock at $10,000 a share); 55 employees bought 2,200 shares for $22M at the same price ("As they would not be if they had options, all of these managers are true owners… they can't 'reprice' their stakes: What they paid is what they live with"); five years later sales tripled, the stock was valued at $71,699 per share, there were 14 acquisitions for $291M and the debt to Berkshire was repaid (2006).
- **ISCAR (2006): a 1.25-page letter from Israel.** A letter dated October 25, 2005 from Eitan Wertheimer ("I am writing to introduce you to ISCAR"), citing "generational transfer and ownership" issues, concluding "Berkshire Hathaway would be the ideal home for ISCAR"; closed July 5, 2006, 80% for $4B, family keeps 20%, Berkshire's first non-U.S. business; "It's a business without magic except for that imparted by the people who run it"; "ISCAR makes money because it enables its customers to make more money. There is no better recipe for continued success."
- **TTI (2006): a founder chooses his buyer.** Paul Andrews Jr. built it from $112,000 of sales to $1.3 billion in 35 years; at 64 he saw how disruptive a founder's death is ("What starts out as disruptive, furthermore, often evolves into destructive") and wanted a chosen owner "rather than allowing a trust officer or lawyer to conduct an auction after his death"; he rejected a strategic buyer (synergy means dismantling) and private equity ("load the company with debt and then flip it"): "That left Berkshire." Deal made "before lunch" on Nov 15 (2006).
- **BNSF (2009 to 2023).** The stock math is in Mental models. Capital: spending above depreciation, about $5.8B in 2015, "nearly three times our annual depreciation charge," the largest capex in American railroad history; BNSF moves about 17% of U.S. intercity freight with 45% more ton-miles than its closest competitor; revenue per ton-mile just under 3 cents against 4.2 to 5.3 cents at four peers (2015). The 2023 correction is in the ledger.
- **Kraft Heinz (2013 to 2019).** Berkshire owned about 53% of Heinz at a cost of $4.25B; after the Kraft merger it owned 325.4M shares (about 27%) costing $9.8B, and GAAP forced a $6.8B write-up though "we sold no Kraft Heinz shares," "an outcome only an accountant could love." The preferred ($720M a year, carried at $7.7B) was redeemed at $8.32B: "good news for Kraft Heinz and bad news for Berkshire." Philosophy contrast with 3G (Lemann, Behring, Hees): they buy bloated companies and cut fast; Berkshire buys businesses "long been run by cost-conscious and efficient managers." Munger's line used to justify non-interference: "If you want to guarantee yourself a lifetime of misery, be sure to marry someone with the intent of changing their behavior" (2015). The 2019 reckoning is in the ledger.
- **Precision Castparts (2015 to 2020).** Ex ante (2015): "PCC fits perfectly into the Berkshire model and will substantially increase our normalized per-share earning power," credit to Todd Combs, who "brought the company to my attention a few years ago and went on to educate me about both the business and Mark" Donegan; Combs and Weschler each manage about $9B: "Hiring these two was one of my best moves" (2015). Ex post (2020): the $11B write-down: "I paid too much for the company. No one misled me in any way – I was simply too optimistic." **[CHECK, dated]** Hold both: the 2015 sentence is the thesis; the 2020 sentence is his own verdict on the price.
- **Occidental (2019, 2023).** The 2019 timeline is in Mental models (Friday call, Sunday handshake). Why Berkshire: no committees or MAC clauses; "I can get a call on Friday afternoon, and they can make a date with me on Saturday, and on Sunday, it's done"; their board approved Monday 10 p.m., announced Tuesday (2019 meeting). Secrecy: plane trackers deduced the deal ("if they take a NetJets plane nobody will be able to track them") (CNBC May 2019). **Evolution:** in 2019 he described a financing deal (preferred plus warrants); in 2023 Berkshire owned 27.8% of the common plus warrants ("Berkshire has no interest in purchasing or managing Occidental") with an energy-independence rationale (U.S. production 8 million BOEPD in 1975, 5 million by 2007, more than 13 million now) and "No one knows what oil prices will do over the next month, year, or decade. But Vicki does know how to separate oil from rock, and that's an uncommon talent."
- **Japanese trading houses (2019 to 2023).** See Raise vs. leverage for the yen financing. Why he likes them: each reduced share counts "at attractive prices," low executive pay, about a third of earnings paid as dividends (paraphrase of the passage); building positions takes "a lot of patience and an extended period of 'friendly' prices. The process is like turning a battleship" (2023).
- **Apple (2016 on).** Berkshire's stake rose to 5.4% via Apple's own buybacks (2020); its look-through earnings dwarf the dividends (2021, see Mental models); by 2019 Berkshire owned "more than $50 billion" of Apple. Why Apple but not Google or Amazon (CNBC May 2017): "Apple didn't have to do a lot better in the future than they were doing at the current time"; with Google and Amazon "you're paying for the future more." Apple's pricing power observed at Nebraska Furniture Mart: price drives TV and appliance traffic, but "They don't want the cheapest product" in phones. The real moat threat: "benefit competition," not price competition. Tim Cook "has made Berkshire a lot more money than I've ever made" (2025 meeting). Munger's quip (2019): "Maybe Apple was atonement."

### Named mistakes ledger (situation, action, price tag, quote, source year)

He names his own errors by name and with the dollar figure; this ledger keeps each one and states where later sources revise earlier ones.

**Commission errors (things he did).**
- **Fifteen-year bonds (1979).** Written after rates jumped in early 1980. "You do not adequately protect yourself by being half awake while others are sleeping. It was a mistake to buy fifteen-year bonds, and yet we did; we made an even more serious mistake in not selling them (at losses, if necessary) when our present views began to crystallize." Accountability: "our mistakes should be viewed less charitably than the mistakes of those who went about their business unmindful of the developing problems." The industry argument underneath: insurers decided "that one year is too long a period for which to set a fixed price for insurance in an inflationary world," then "taken the proceeds from the sale of that six-month policy, and sold the money at a fixed price for thirty or forty years." His decision rule: "Neither a short-term borrower nor a long-term lender be" (Polonius, slightly restated) (1979). He flagged his own uncertainty: "We even may miss large profits from a major rebound in bond prices."
- **Berkshire's own insurance underwriting (1983).** Combined ratio 121 against an estimated 111 for the industry while GEICO wrote at 96: "I made some serious mistakes a few years ago that came home to roost." Insurance has "a long lead-time," so 1983 results were rooted in "operating and personnel decisions made two or more years back when I had direct managerial responsibility" (1983).
- **Unwittingly writing inadequate reinsurance (early 1970s, still billing in 1995).** "A bad reinsurance contract is like hell: easy to enter and impossible to exit." "Berkshire paid a heavy tuition for my education in the business. Unfortunately, reinsurance students can't attend school on scholarship" (1995). The 2002 statement of the same similarity: "the reinsurance and derivatives businesses are similar: Like Hell, both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit."
- **The Gillette preferred (1989).** $600M of convertible preferred into 48M split-adjusted common shares. He could "probably" have bought 60M common shares at about $10.50 in a private placement, at a discount of at least 5%. "But I was far too clever to do that." The preferred was called within two years. "If I had negotiated for common rather than preferred, we would have been better off at yearend 1995 by $625 million, minus the 'excess' dividends of about $70 million." "Our best holding has been Gillette, which we told you from the start was a superior business. Ironically, though, this is also the purchase in which I made my biggest mistake - of a kind, however, never recognized on financial statements" (1995). Overall verdict on the 1987 to 1991 preferreds: "Overall, our preferreds have performed well, but that is true only because of one huge winner, Gillette"; ex-Gillette they delivered "after-tax returns no more than equal to those we could have earned from the medium-term fixed-income issues that they replaced." By 1997 the $600M had become $4.8B.
- **Salomon and USAir preferreds (1987 to 1989), and the prediction he would like back.** Cost vs market at 12/31/95: Champion $300M/$388M; First Empire $40M/$110M; Gillette $600M/$2,502M; Salomon $700M/$728M (after a $140M redemption); USAir $358M/$215M. He had written "under almost any conditions, we expect these preferreds to return us our money plus dividends." "That's one I would like to have back. ... My assertion, however, that it was almost impossible for us to lose money on our preferreds has caused me some well-deserved heartburn" (Churchill: "eating my words has never given me indigestion").
- **The Salomon preferred: a job he never expected (1995, U. Florida 1998).** "Certainly I never dreamed I would take a new job at age 60 - Salomon interim chairman, that is - because of an earlier purchase of a fixed-income security"; "the amount of management time Charlie and I have devoted to this holding has been vastly greater than its economic significance to Berkshire"; "my decision to go with Salomon because I saw value in the conversion option must be graded as very poor" (1995). Why he did it: in September 1987 "it was a 9% security… the Dow was up 35% and we sold a lot of stuff… I took an attractive security form in a business I would never buy the common stock of" (rbcpa 1998).
- **USAir (1989 to 1998, and again in 2017).** 1995: preferred structurally good, credit weak: "On the plus side for us is the fact that our preferred is structurally well-designed ... On the minus side is the fact that we are dealing with a weak credit"; "considering my record with this investment, it's fair to say that your guess may be better than mine." 1997: "I have compiled a record that is unblemished by success. I was wrong in originally purchasing the stock, and I was wrong later, in repeatedly trying to unload our holdings at 50 cents on the dollar… Next time I make a big, dumb decision, Berkshire shareholders will know what to do: Phone Mr. Wolf." (Stephen Wolf became CEO as Berkshire left the board; the stock went from a low of $4 to a high of $73.)
- **USAir aftermath: the air-aholic line, the payoff and the 2017 verdict (1998, 2017).** 1998 talk: "I now have an 800 number I call every time I think about buying a stock in an airline. I say, 'I am Warren and I am an air-aholic'" and "We came very close to losing all our money in it." CNBC's archive summary: early 1998 US Airways repaid the $358M with common shares that sold for $574M and Berkshire collected about $240M in dividends (CNBC editorial summary; my inference of roughly $456M total gain, which is why he calls it luck, not thesis).
- **USAir in 2017: "I made it all by myself."** "I was a director of U.S. Air, it was a really dumb investment on my part. I made it all by myself. I didn't even consult Charlie. And by the time the ink was drying, I knew it was dumb. And then it got dumber." On the 2016 airline stakes the same day: "I don't think it's a suicidal business anymore. But if they get down to running at 70% capacity or something like that, it'll be suicidal again." **[CHECK, dated]** The May 2017 sentence sits three years before the 2020 sale of every airline holding (paraphrase only: his widely reported admission that the sale was his own mistake could not be verified against a raw transcript); read it as the failure-mode warning that capacity fragility, not management, breaks airlines.
- **Dexter Shoe (1993).** Price, stock as currency, and delay: see Pivot vs. persevere.
- **GEICO advertising overspend (2000).** New auto policies fell to 1,472,853 from 1,648,095 in 2000 after he had promised stepped-up advertising as "the best investment that GEICO could make": "I was wrong: The extra money we spent did not produce a commensurate increase in inquiries." Four causes: frequency overreach (the third ad in an hour is less effective), low-hanging fruit already picked, stricter underwriting, and State Farm's loss of 18% of auto premiums. Tony Nicely cut back "advertising that was not cost-effective" (2000). The 2001 letter had GEICO's policies in force falling 0.8% and him unable to "see how to" spend more than the $219M marketing budget effectively.
- **General Re: three letters, three admissions; the first (2001).** "Though our corporate performance last year was satisfactory, my performance was anything but… I allowed General Re to take on business without a safeguard I knew was important, and on September 11th, this error caught up with us"; 2001 reserve shortfall of "a staggering $800 million" and a reinsurance underwriting loss of $4,318M pre-tax.
- **General Re, the second admission (2002).** "I was dead wrong." He "had studied the operation for decades" and "detected no slippage." Gen Re "was grossly mispricing its current business," had an aggregation of nuclear, chemical and biological risk that "would have been fatal," and "the World Trade Center attack alone would have threatened the company's existence." 2002 charge: $1.31B for prior-year reserve error ($1.75B with retroactive amortization, 4.6% of float). Lines: "I wish I didn't know now what I didn't know then" (a country song) and "The natural tendency of most casualty-insurance managers is to underreserve, and they must have a particular mindset… if they are to overcome this devastating bias"; rescue: "I was lucky: Joe and Tad were on hand."
- **General Re fixed, and the dated forecast error (2003, with 1998 and 1999).** "General Re had been Berkshire's problem child in the years following our acquisition of it in 1998. Unfortunately, it was a 400-pound child, and its negative impact on our overall performance was large. That's behind us: Gen Re is fixed." Underwriting swung from $(1,393) million in 2002 to +$145 million in 2003. Also "I have failed on occasion too, particularly in not spotting Gen Re's unwitting underreserving a few years back." **[CHECK, dated]** 1998 optimism ("Berkshire can add absolutely nothing to the skills of General Re's and Cologne Re's managers") against the 2001 to 2003 record is a clean case of a forecast error; 1999 had already graded his own year: "My 'one subject' is capital allocation, and my grade for 1999 most assuredly is a D" ("Even Inspector Clouseau could find last year's guilty party: your Chairman").
- **Gen Re Securities (1998 to 2006): the dithering.** Inherited in the deal, a dealer he and Munger "didn't want, judging it to be dangerous," and they "failed in our attempts to sell the operation." Wind-down: 23,218 contracts with 884 counterparties at the start (early 2002); 14,384 contracts and 672 counterparties after ten months (pre-tax loss $173M in 2002); 7,580 and 453 (2003; pre-tax loss $99M); 2,890 (end of 2004); 741 at the end of 2005 after a $104M unwinding cost (cumulative $404M; one contract had a 100-year term); 197 in 2006 (cumulative $409M). Lines: "How can I miss you if you won't go away?" (the country song, 2003); "I'm sure I could have saved you $100 million or so, pre-tax, if I had acted more promptly to shut down Gen Re Securities." "Charlie would have moved swiftly... I, however, dithered." "I have cost you a lot of money by not moving immediately to close down" (2005). "When a problem exists, whether in personnel or in business operations, the time to act is now" (2005; Charlie calls the delay "thumb-sucking").
- **Why Gen Re Securities was dangerous, in his words (2002, 2003, 2006).** On the risk itself: "No matter how financially sophisticated you are, you can't possibly learn from reading the disclosure documents of a derivatives-intensive company what risks lurk in its positions" and Darwin: "Ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge" (2003). Why he and Munger knew: reported profits "struck us as illusory," risks "could not effectively be measured or limited," trouble would "correlate with troubles in the financial or insurance world" so the unit "would commandeer the capital and credit of Berkshire at just the time we could otherwise deploy those resources to huge advantage" (2003). Mark-to-model "degenerates into what I would call mark-to-myth" (2002). Munger's tombstone for the book in 2006: "Good Until Reached For"; "All's well that ends."
- **Oakwood Homes junk debt (confessed 2003).** See Clayton in the acquisition stories: "I did not understand how atrocious consumer-financing practices had become… But I learned: Oakwood rather promptly went bankrupt."
- **NetJets (1998 to 2009).** 2003: lost $41M pre-tax, including a $32M write-down on repurchased fractions; Europe still losing despite revenue growth of 88%, 61% and 77% in 2001 to 2003: "any company that forsakes Europe, as all of our competitors have done, is destined for second-tier status"; FlightSafety simulator obsolescence cost $37M. 2005: "I was dead wrong" (he had said it would earn money). 2009: eleven years of ownership, aggregate pre-tax loss of $157M, debt from $102M at purchase to $1.9B in April 2009, a 2009 loss of $711M: "Without Berkshire's guarantee of this debt, NetJets would have been out of business." "It's clear that I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this condition." Fix: Dave Sokol became CEO in August 2009, debt fell to $1.4B and the unit turned profitable (the Sokol affair is in Blind spots).
- **GEICO credit card (2009).** "Coming up with a brilliant insight that we should market our own credit card": pre-tax operating losses of about $6.3M, then a $98M troubled-receivables portfolio sold at 55 cents on the dollar, losing $44M more. He overruled GEICO managers who warned of "the non-cream." "I subtly indicated that I was older and wiser. I was just older" (2009).
- **Berkshire's manufacturing group (2015).** The group earned 18.4% after tax on $25.6B of net tangible assets, but "In most of these cases, I was wrong in my evaluation of the economic dynamics of the company or the industry in which it operates, and we are now paying the price for my misjudgments." Also: "Of course, a business with terrific economics can be a bad investment if it is bought at too high a price" (2015). The unnamed errors have two causes: industry economics and managers' fidelity or ability.
- **IBM (2011 to 2018).** The 2011 letter said Berkshire "should wish for IBM's stock price to languish throughout the five years" of IBM's buybacks (the share-repurchase logic); by 2015 the stake was 8.4% with a $13.8B cost against $11.2B of market value; in the May 2017 interview, on Watson experiments at GEICO and H&R Block, he said the biggest value will come "when it actually replaces human labor," and on AI fear "I've got the cure for it… I'm 86. You won't worry as much under those circumstances." The exit came in 2018 (baseline, Blind spots).
- **Energy Future (2011).** "major unforced error" (baseline).
- **Kraft Heinz (2013 to 2019).** See Acquisition stories; 2019: "We paid too much money for Kraft. To some extent, our own actions had driven up the prices." The See's calibration: about "6 billion pretax on 7 billion of tangible assets, is a wonderful business. But you can pay too much for a wonderful business." Also: "we did underestimate, not what the consumer is doing so much, but what the retailer is," because "at See's Candy, we sell directly to the consumer, but at Kraft Heinz, they're intermediaries" and "the brand is our protection against the intermediaries making all the money."
- **Kraft Heinz: retailers versus brands, and 3G (2019 meeting).** Costco's Kirkland is a "$39 billion brand" through about 775 warehouses, all of Kraft Heinz is $26 billion: the retail side "has gained in power relative to brands." Munger: "it's not a tragedy that, out of two transactions, one worked wonderfully, and the other didn't work so well." On 3G: "They have more of a taste for leverage than we do, and they probably have more of a taste for paying up, but they also are, in certain types of situations, they'd be way better operators than we would"; their agreement with Berkshire was "a one-page agreement, which I haven't even actually ever reread" (2019).
- **Precision Castparts (2016 to 2020).** The $11B write-down (see Acquisition stories).
- **BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy (2023).** "Insurance came through as expected. I erred, however, in my expectations for both BNSF and BHE." BNSF: margins "have slipped relative to all five" other major railroads; "Consequently, Berkshire is receiving an acceptable return on its purchase price, though less than it might appear, and also a pittance on the replacement value of the property." BHE: wildfire losses and regulation: "the regulatory climate in a few states has raised the specter of zero profitability or even bankruptcy (an actual outcome at California's largest utility and a current threat in Hawaii)"; "I did not anticipate or even consider the adverse developments in regulatory returns and, along with Berkshire's two partners at BHE, I made a costly mistake in not doing so." Rule: "Berkshire can sustain financial surprises but we will not knowingly throw good money after bad." A western transmission project "initiated in 2006" remains "some years from completion."
- **The 2023 scorecard behind the BNSF and BHE admission (2023).** Scorecard 2023 vs 2022: insurance underwriting $5,428M vs $(30)M; insurance investment income $9,567M vs $6,484M; railroad $5,087M vs $5,946M; utilities $2,331M vs $3,904M; operating earnings $37,350M vs $30,853M.

**Omission errors (things he did not do).**
- **Fannie Mae and healthcare stocks (1991, 1998 talk).** The 1991 letter sizes Fannie Mae at $1.4B. In 1998: "the biggest mistakes have not been mistakes of commission, but of omission," naming healthcare stocks when the Clinton plan scared the market ("We should have made a ton of money out of that because I could understand it. And didn't make it") and Fannie Mae in the mid-1980s ("Those are billion dollar mistakes or multi-billion dollar mistakes that generally accepted accounting principles don't pick up").
- **The Sinclair station.** "Back when I had $10,000 I put $2,000 of it into a Sinclair Service Station which I lost, so the opportunity cost on that money is about $6 billion right now." "It makes me feel good when my Berkshire goes down, because the cost of my Sinclair Station goes down too" (rbcpa 1998).
- **Tangible-asset bias (1983), tech (2017), Amazon and Google (2017, 2019).** See Mental models.
- **Walmart.** Munger flagged in 2014 that Berkshire's net worth "would now be at least $50 billion higher" absent errors of omission such as Walmart (baseline).
- **2008 bonds:** "I should have done far more" (2009).

**Governance omissions he names.**
- **Board hygiene (2004):** "regular meetings of directors without the CEO present. I've sat on 19 boards, and on many occasions this process would have led to dubious plans being examined more thoroughly." And "Berkshire would be more valuable today if I had put in a whistleblower line decades ago."
- **Silence on boards (2002):** "Too often I was silent when management made proposals that I judged to be counter to the interests of shareholders. In those cases, collegiality trumped independence." Over 40 years on 19 public-company boards and about 250 directors, "the great majority of these directors lacked at least one of the three qualities I value."
- **Incapacitated CEOs (2025):** "failed to act… This failure can be a huge mistake" (baseline; see Blind spots).
- **Wells Fargo.** The baseline records the passive-conduct critique. Dated additions: in May 2017 he answered a shareholder who asked why he was not "ruthless" as at Salomon: "the CEO lost his job and gave back his pay. And I was not a director." Diagnosis: "they obviously came up with an incentive system that incented the wrong things." Berkshire's hotline: "We got about 4,000 complaints." On reputation versus earning power: "the reputation has been hurt. The fundamental earning power of the bank over a period of years has not been hurt in any material way," and "I worry about getting complacent."
- **Wells Fargo in 2019: incentives, Salomon, punishment and Tim Sloan (2019 meeting).** "it looks like to me like Wells made some big mistakes in what they incentivized. And as Charlie says, there's nothing like incentives, but they can incentivize the wrong behavior"; the fake accounts "had no balance in them, that could not possibly have been profitable to Wells. So, you can incentivize some crazy things." Rule: "when you find a problem, you have to do something about it." Punishment: bank shareholders paid "many, many billions of dollars" while no one went to jail, "And that is infuriating"; the CEO of a bank that needs government help "should lose his net worth and his spouse's net worth."
- **Defending Tim Sloan, against Munger (2019 meeting).** Defense of Tim Sloan (2019): "I was 100% behind Tim Sloan... I would have never asked for the resignation," and a Wall Street hire would be "pinatas from now until the election time... They'd be poison in Washington"; he diverged from Munger, who thought predecessors (Kovacevich, Stumpf) were the problem: "I like Dick Kovacevich. Dick expresses himself very forcefully, Charlie expresses himself forcefully and maybe the two don't mix that well." Munger on the same panel: "I don't think people ought to go to jail for honest errors of judgment." **[CHECK, dated, resolved]** The baseline critique (that as the largest holder he took no action to stop it) and the 2017 self-defense ("I was not a director") are both on the record; the synthesis is that as a roughly 10% holder who spoke to the CEO about once a year he took the position that he had no inside information, and in 2019 he stated the rule for others: "if you're in the top job, you've got to take action fast."

### The Partnership years (1956 to 1969): worked situations with the numbers

The Partnership letters (compiled in the csinvesting mirror; the Market Folly and Brian Langis hosts carry the identical text) are the densest set of numbered cases. Generals, workouts and controls were the three categories; the four-way table later split generals into private-owner and relatively-undervalued, and the results in each category "are dominated by one or two investments" (BPL 1969).

- **Commonwealth Trust Co. of Union City, NJ (1958 letter, "Typical Situation").** Intrinsic value about $125 a share (conservative), earnings about $10, no dividend, price about $50: "a very well managed bank with substantial earnings power selling at a large discount from intrinsic value," about $50M of assets. A larger bank owned 25.5% and wanted a merger blocked "for personal reasons." He bought about 12% at an average of about $51 over roughly a year (a position of 10% to 20% of partnership assets), becoming the second-largest holder. "Obviously it was definitely to our advantage to have the stock remain dormant in price." About 300 holders and "two trades or so per month."
- **Commonwealth Trust: the price move, the test and the exit (BPL 1958).** Competition lifted the price to $65 ("Very small buying orders can create price changes of this magnitude in an inactive stock, which explains the importance of not having any 'Leakage' regarding our portfolio holdings"), and he stopped buying. Three-part test: "1. Very strong defensive characteristics; 2. Good solid value building up at a satisfactory pace and; 3. Evidence to the effect that eventually this value would be unlocked although it might be one year or ten years." Exit: sold at $80 when he found a special situation where the Partnership could be the largest holder (replacement about 25% of assets). His warning on reading short records: realized profit in any single year is "fortuitous," so a one-year record has "serious limitations as a basis for estimating long term results."
- **Sanborn Map Co. (1960 letter, dated Jan 30, 1961): the 35% position.** A fire-insurance-map near-monopoly (after-tax map profits over $500,000 in the late 1930s, under $100,000 by 1958 to 1959 after "carding") that had accumulated about $2.5M of securities (original cost), worth about $7M. The valuation gap: in 1938, with the Dow at 100 to 120, the stock was $110 with $20 a share of portfolio, so the map business was valued at $90; in 1958, with the Dow near 550, the stock was $45 with a $65 portfolio, so the map business "was evaluated at a minus $20 with the buyer of the stock unwilling to pay more than 70 cents on the dollar for the investment portfolio with the map business thrown in for nothing."
- **Sanborn Map: the board that would not act, and the exchange offer (BPL Jan 1961).** Governance finding: of 14 directors, nine were insurance-industry executives who together owned 46 shares of 105,000 ("the largest holding in this group was ten shares"); dividends were cut five times in eight years "although I could never find any record of suggestions pertaining to cutting salaries or director's and committee fees." "The very fact that the investment portfolio had done so well served to minimize in the eyes of most directors the need for rejuvenation of the map business."
- **Sanborn Map: the action and the result (BPL Jan 1961).** He bought the widow's block of 15,000 shares after a family dispute and grew holdings with allies to about 46,000 shares; Booz Allen's consultants also recommended separation; instead of a proxy fight he offered to take out any holder at fair value in portfolio securities (the SEC ruled the plan fair). Result: about 72% of the shares (50% of 1,600 holders) exchanged for portfolio securities; the map business kept over $1.25M in bonds and avoided a corporate capital-gains tax of more than $1M. It reached 35% of net assets at one point. Meta lesson: "Such control situations may occur very infrequently. Our bread-and-butter business is buying undervalued securities and selling when the undervaluation is corrected along with investment in special situations where the profit is dependent on corporate rather than market action."
- **Dempster Mill Manufacturing (1956 to 1963, told in the Jan 18, 1964 letter).** Situation (1956): stock $18, book $72, of which $50 a share was current assets net of all liabilities; "The qualitative situation was on the negative side (a fairly tough industry and unimpressive management), but the figures were extremely attractive." Base rate: "Experience shows you can buy 100 situations like this and have perhaps 70 or 80 work out to reasonable profits in one to three years." He bought for five years at $16 to $25 while a director; by mid-1961 about 30% (tender offers drew "poor results"); purchases at $30.25 in August and September 1961 and a tender at the same price took him past 70%. Valuation at 11/30/61: cash 100%, receivables 85%, inventory 60%, prepaids 25%, plant at estimated auction $800K, giving $35.25 a share.
- **Dempster Mill: the Harry Bottle turnaround (BPL Jan 1964).** Management: he first promoted the executive vice president "to see what he would do unfettered by the previous policies. The results were unsatisfactory," and hired Harry Bottle (April 17, 1962; in the president's chair April 23). Bottle's changes: inventory from over $4,203,000 to $890,000; administrative and selling expense from $150,000 to $75,000 a month; factory overhead burden from $6 to $4.50 per direct labor hour; five unprofitable branches closed (three kept) with distributors replacing the rest; jobbed lines eliminated; repair-part prices adjusted for about $200,000 of extra profit; cash and securities from $166,000 to $2,436,000; liabilities from $2,318,000 to $274,000.
- **Dempster Mill: the "fingers and toes" valuation and the morals (BPL Jan 1964).** Valuation by the "fingers and toes" method: the Partnership owned 71.7% at a cost of $1,262,577.27; at June 30, 1963 the company held securities worth $2,028,415.25, so its share was $1,454,373.70: "Thus, everything above ground (and part of it underground) is profit." "I find a bit more substance in this fingers and toes method than in any prayerful reliance that someone will pay me 35 times next year's earnings." Value per share: $35.25 (11/61), $51.26 (11/62), $64.81 (11/63); the late-1963 sale of operating assets near book plus portfolio gains realized about $80 a share.
- **Dempster Mill: the margin-of-safety rules it taught (BPL Jan 1964).** "Never count on making a good sale. Have the purchase price be so attractive that even a mediocre sale gives good results. The better sales will be the frosting on the cake"; "By buying assets at a bargain price, we don't need to pull any rabbits out of a hat to get extremely good percentage gains"; "Harry works the same way I do -- he likes big carrots." and "I like dealing with someone who is not trying to figure how to get the fixtures in the executive washroom gold-plated." Morals: a three-year judging minimum; no discussing current holdings ("plead the '5th Amendment'"); and "An investment operation that depends on the ultimate buyer making a bum deal (in Wall Street they call this the 'Bigger Fool Theory') is tenuous indeed" (1964). Note on tax: de-incorporating would have doubled partners' after-tax earnings but was complex.
- **Texas National Petroleum work-out (Jan 18, 1964 letter appendix).** Rumors in early 1962 of a sellout to Union Oil of California. "I never act on such information, but in this case it was correct and substantially more money would have been made if we had gone in at the rumor stage rather than the announced stage. However, that's somebody else's business, not mine." After terms were announced in April 1962 he bought $264,000 of 6.5% debentures (callable at 104.25), 64,035 shares (proxy estimate $7.42 a share on liquidation) and 83,200 warrants (about 13% of the issue; strike $3.50, a $3.92 workout).
- **Texas National: scoring the risks and the payoff (BPL Jan 1964).** Risk scoring: stockholder approval "nil," title and antitrust "virtually nil," the one open item a tax ruling delayed by USC's charitable status in the production payment. His call log: June 18 "Hoping for ruling in July," July 24 "early next week," Aug 13 all parties in Washington, Sept 18 "No news." Ruling late September; closed Oct 31, 1962; payments of $3.50 (Dec 14), $3.90 (Feb 4), 15 cents (Apr 24, 1963). Bonds: $14,446 gain on $260,773 for about 20% annualized. Stock and warrants: $89,304 realized (from $146,000 in April to $731,000 in October), about 22% annualized.
- **Work-out lessons from Texas National: delay, leverage and errors (BPL Jan 1964).** Pattern: "the deals take longer than originally projected" and "the payouts tend to average a little better than estimates." Leverage: "some borrowed money is warranted against a portfolio of workouts, but feel it is a very dangerous practice against generals." "When a workout falls through, the resulting market value shrink is substantial. Therefore, you cannot afford many errors." He calls it "typical of our bread-and-butter type of operation," not "any earth-shaking triumph." Mechanism: "We wait until we can read it in the paper."
- **Berkshire Hathaway (Nov 1962 to 1966).** Purchases began Nov 1962 at $7.60, which "partially reflected large losses incurred by the prior management in closing some of the mills"; he assumed a controlling position in spring 1965 at an average of $14.86 ("very heavy purchases in early 1965"); by Dec 31, 1965 net working capital alone was about $19 a share. In 1948 the mills had 11,000 workers and about $29.5M of pre-tax profit; at control, two mills and about 2,300 employees; he was pleasantly surprised that the remaining units "had excellent management personnel" and brought in no outsider.
- **Valuing control, and the textile verdict (BPL 1965 to 1969).** Valuation of a controlled holding (Nov 1, 1965): at a price halfway between net current asset value and book, because "Market price, which governs valuation of minority interest positions, is of little or no importance in valuing a controlling interest." The 1965 description: "While a Berkshire is hardly going to be as profitable as a Xerox, Fairchild Camera or National Video in a hypertensed market, it is a very comfortable sort of thing to own. As my West Coast philosopher says, 'It is well to have a diet consisting of oatmeal as well as cream puffs.'" By 1967: "B-H is experiencing and faces real difficulties in the textile business" and he saw "no prospect of a good return on the assets employed in the textile business"; by December 1969 it "cannot be judged a satisfactory business," so capital would be redeployed "now on a permanent basis into insurance and banking." (The 2017 interview adds that the three building blocks were Berkshire, Diversified Retailing and Blue Chip Stamps, and "the base companies of both of the other two totally failed, disappeared.")
- **Hochschild, Kohn & Co. (July 12, 1966 letter) and Diversified Retailing.** The first time the Partnership bought an entire business by negotiation: a privately owned Baltimore department store with several thousand employees, bought with two 10% partners; BPL's share was about 10% of more than $50M invested, so it should not be read as "financial importance"; "I still prefer the iceberg approach toward investment disclosure." Selection rule: "Even if the price had been cheaper but the management had been run-of-the-mill, we would not have bought the business." He admits "All of our investments usually appear undervalued to me" and that he did not learn to pronounce "HK" until the deal closed. Later, Diversified Retailing Co. (80% BPL) owned HK and Associated Cotton Shops (Ben Rosner; sales about $37.5M, net income about $1M, "about 20% on capital employed," a level only 37 Fortune 500 companies reached in 1967); DRC sold HK to Supermarkets General in December 1969 for $5.0M cash plus notes of $2M and $4.54M. National Indemnity (Jack Ringwalt) was bought in 1967 and Illinois National Bank and Trust of Rockford in early 1969 for Berkshire.
- **Generals-private owner: accumulating while the stock sleeps (1966).** Starting mid-1965 he bought a widely held security far below private-owner value, hoping to build $10M or more over two to three years; by year end 1965 he had invested $1,956,980 (value $2,358,412) when "Another party, previously completely unknown to me, issued a tender offer which foreclosed opportunities for future advantageous buying." He realized $1,269,181 of profit in February 1966 ($867,749 applicable to 1966) and regretted it: "we would have been much better off from a long range standpoint if 1966 results had been five percentage points worse." The mechanism is the same as Commonwealth: you want the stock stagnant while you accumulate. 1965's other "private owner" case: a tender offer realized $3,188,000, because "A private owner was quite willing ... to pay a price for control of the business which isolated stock buyers were not willing to pay."
- **The 1967 workout drag and the 1969 workout loss.** 1967: workouts earned $153,273 on $17,246,879 of average capital (0.89%): "The streets were filled with upset applecarts - our applecarts." 1969: "Our experience in workouts this year has been atrocious ... Our poor experience this year is 100% my fault. It did not reflect bad luck, but rather an improper assessment of a very fast-developing governmental trend." Plus: "Documenting one's boners is unpleasant business. I find 'selective reporting' even more distasteful." Feel: "I have felt like the bird that inadvertently flew into the middle of a badminton game."
- **The October 9, 1967 letter that lowers the goal.** Four named difficulties: (1) "the virtual disappearance of the bargain issue as determined quantitatively," leaving "the occasional security where I am really competent to make an important qualitative judgment"; (2) "Mushrooming interest in investment performance" produced a "hyper-reactive pattern of market behavior" ("I think we are witnessing the distortion of a sound idea," as three-year judgments shrank to monthly); (3) size (capital about $65M against "a diminishing trickle of good investment ideas"; and "if we were operating with one-tenth of our present capital our performance would be little better"); (4) personal: "When I started the partnership I set the motor that regulated the treadmill at 'ten points better than the DOW'" and "My own personal interests dictate a less compulsive approach to superior investment results than when I was younger and leaner."
- **The lowered goal and the refusal to chase fashion (BPL Oct 9, 1967).** New goal: "the lesser of 9% per annum or a five percentage point advantage over the Dow" ("if the Dow averages -2% over the next five years, I would hope to average +3% but if the Dow averages +12%, I will hope to achieve an average of only +9%").
- **Out of step with the go-go market, and the aftermath (BPL Oct 1967 to Jan 1969).** He explains the habit trap with Bertrand Russell's Lithuanian girls who kept stealing vegetables after the war. On fashion investing: "does not completely satisfy my intellect (or perhaps my prejudices), and most definitely does not fit my temperament. I will not invest my own money based upon such an approach" and "hence, I will most certainly not do so with your money." "Essentially I am out of step with present conditions" and "I will not abandon a previous approach whose logic I understand (although I find it difficult to apply) even though it may mean foregoing large and apparently easy, profits to embrace an approach which I don't fully understand, have not practiced successfully and which, possibly, could lead to substantial permanent loss of capital." "The above may simply be 'old-fogeyism' (after all, I am 37)." The aftermath: partners with about $1.6M withdrew at year end, and 1968 then produced +58.8% against the Dow's +7.7%, "a freak like picking up thirteen spades in a bridge game."
- **The Sorrows of Compounding (Jan 20, 1966).** Capital had reached $43,645,000. He reversed his earlier denial: "I feel substantially greater size is more likely to harm future results than to help them. This might not be true for my own personal results, but it is likely to be true for your results." Decision: admit no new partners (the joke: "I have notified Susie that if we have any more children, it is up to her to find some other partnership for them"). Control situations favor size ("A 'Sanborn Map' cannot be accomplished without the wherewithal") while passive holdings hurt. His 1967 admission: "For years my investment ideas were anywhere from 110% to 1000% of our capital." Assets by January: $303,726 (1957), $7,178,500 (1962), $9,405,400 (1963), $17,454,900 (1964), $26,074,000 (1965), $43,645,000 (1966), $54,065,345 (1967), $68.1M (1968), $104,429,431 (1969).
- **The 1969 wind-down mechanics.** Decided May 29, 1969: "I just don't see anything available that gives any reasonable hope of delivering such a good year and I have no desire to grope around, hoping to 'get lucky' with other people's money." Reasons: (1) opportunities for the quantitative analyst "have virtually disappeared"; (2) "$100 million of assets" rules out companies with less than about $100M of common stock at market, since commitments below about $3M cannot move results; (3) a short-term-oriented market. "I have flunked this test completely" (limited objectives did not lessen effort); "The only way to slow down is to stop."
- **Wind-down distribution: cash, Ruane and the Blue Chip block (BPL 1969 to 1970).** Distribution: Jan 5, 1970 cash of at least 64% (later about 70% after Blue Chip) of Jan 1, 1969 capital plus pro rata Diversified Retailing and Berkshire shares; he recommended Bill Ruane (disclosing Ruane's record fairly, down about 50% in 1962 and about 15% in 1969). The Blue Chip Stamps underwriting (about 7.5% of the company) collapsed when the offer price slid from $24 to $16.5 after announcement: "I intend to hold our block." Berkshire and DRC shares went out by private placement, not registration: "The more sophisticated partners might have an important edge on the less sophisticated ones." On second men: "The only real way to see how someone is going to do when running a company is to let him run it."
- **Bonds versus stocks, October 1969.** "For the first time in my investment lifetime. I now believe there is little choice for the average investor between professionally managed money in stocks and passive investment in bonds." With tax-free bonds at about 6.5% and ten-year stock expectations "probably not better than 9% overall. say 3% dividends and 6% gain in value" (about 6.5% after tax for a 40% bracket), and "less than 10% of professionally managed money" averaging two points above group expectancy, he concluded that passive tax-free bonds were "fully the equivalent of expectations from professionally managed money in stocks, and only modestly inferior to extremely well-managed equity money." Method: "the best expectable after-tax rate of return makes the most sense - given a rising, declining or stable dollar."
- **Taxes in the Partnership era (July 1963, Jan 1965).** "My net worth is the market value of holdings less the tax payable upon sale." "More investment sins are probably committed by otherwise quite intelligent people because of 'tax considerations' than from any other cause." Swap funds as a test case (Federal Street Fund -19.0% in 1962, +17.0%, +13.8%; Westminster -22.5%, +18.7%, +12.3%, against the Dow's -7.6%, +20.6%, +18.7%): "Anyone for taxes?" Policy: maximize investment gains, not minimize taxes. **[CHECK, dated]** This early stance (pay the tax at low rates and maximize gains) is the opposite of buy-and-never-sell; the later 0.1% ProPublica figure comes from Berkshire paying no dividend, rarely selling, and the Giving Pledge, which is why both Partnership-era and 2011 positions have to be read together (see Blind spots).
- **Why institutions underperform: his five-factor diagnosis (Jan 1965).** "(1) group decisions ... (2) a desire to conform to the policies and (to an extent) the portfolios of other large well-regarded organizations; (3) an institutional framework whereby average is 'safe' and the personal rewards for independent action are in no way commensurate with the general risk attached to such action; (4) an adherence to certain diversification practices which are irrational; and finally and importantly, (5) inertia." Evidence: the four largest funds averaged about 9% to 10% against the Dow's 11.1% (1957 to 1964) with advisory fees "over $8 million." Even so: "In no sense is this statement intended as criticism. Within their institutional framework and handling the many billions of dollars involved, I consider such average results virtually the only possible ones." 1957 to 1961 cumulative: four largest funds 71.4%, 76.9%, 70.8%, 77.4%; the Dow 74.9%; limited partners 181.6%. Of 38 comparable mutual funds from 1946 to 1960, 32 did poorer than the Dow.
- **Two kinds of conservatism (Jan 1962, Jan 1965, July 1966).** (a) Conventionalism: results "varying only slightly from average experience." (b) True conservatism: "subject to substantially less temporary or permanent shrinkage in value than total experience." In the cumulative declines of 1957, 1960 and 1962 the Dow fell 20.6% and funds 9.7% to 24.6% while limited partners gained 45.0%. "True conservatism is only possible through knowledge and reason."
- **Ground Rules and fee design (Jan 1963, Nov 1965, July 1961).** Rule 2: "Any year in which we fail to achieve at least a plus 6% performance will be followed by a year when partners receiving monthly payments will find those payments lowered." Rule 4: measure against the Dow "whether we are plus or minus." Rule 5: "three years is an absolute minimum." Rule 6 (see Decision heuristics). Fees: the first 6% to partners on beginning capital at market; excess split one-quarter to the general partner and three-quarters to limited partners; shortfalls carried forward but never back; the GP and his family barred from owning other marketable securities. The Buffetts' interest was $1,025,000 (1962), $2,392,900 (1964), $3,406,700 (1965) and $6,849,936 (1966), well over 90% of their net worth. A stated 1962 yardstick ("A Word About Par," see Mental models), and from 1964: "We started out with a 36-inch yardstick and we'll keep it that way. If we don't measure up, we won't change yardsticks." "Show me a good loser, and I'll show you a loser" (Casey Stengel, quoted).
- **The Joys of Compounding (Jan 1963 to 1965).** Isabella and Columbus: $30,000 at 4% to about $2 trillion by 1962; Mona Lisa 4,000 ecus (about $20,000) in 1540 at 6% to over $1 quadrillion; Manhattan $24 in 1626 at 6.5% to $42B (and at 7% to $205B) against a 1964 land value of $12.4B; $100,000 at 4%, 8%, 12% and 16% for 10, 20 and 30 years. The sorrow is the same table turned against size.
- **Buying versus selling (1962, 1964).** "I consider the buying end to be about 90% of this business" (July 1964); "Over the years our timing of purchases has been considerably better than our timing of sales" (Jan 1962). Market timing: "Who would think of buying or selling a private business because of someone's guess on the stock market?" (July 1966).
- **The "fashion" years (1967 to 1968).** The 1968 chain-letter vogue and reading list ("The Money Game" by Adam Smith): "We live in an investment world, populated not by those who must be logically persuaded to believe, but by the hopeful, credulous and greedy, grasping for an excuse to believe." "Some of the so-called 'go-go' funds have recently been re-christened 'no-go' funds" (Gerald Tsai's Manhattan Fund fell 6.9% in 1968). "Securities must be studied in a minute-by-minute program. Wow! This sort of stuff makes me feel guilty when I go out for a Pepsi" (Jan 1969).

### The nine records of Graham-and-Doddsville (Columbia, May 17, 1984)

The unifying claim (see Mental models): the nine investors search "for discrepancies between the value of a business and the price of small pieces of that business in the market," focusing on "two variables: price and value," and "practically no duplication" in their holdings rules out imitation.
- **Walter Schloss:** no college, a night course at the New York Institute of Finance; worked at Graham-Newman 1954 to 1956 with Buffett; 28¼ years: partnership 21.3% a year, limited partners 16.1%, S&P 8.4%; more than 800 issues over time, 100+ positions, about $45M managed. "He simply says, if a business is worth a dollar and I can buy it for 40 cents, something good may happen to me."
- **Tom Knapp / Tweedy, Browne:** 15¾ years, 20.0% overall (16.0% for limited partners) against S&P 7.0%; Knapp, a Princeton chemistry major turned beach bum who took Dodd's night course: "Incidentally, 35 years later I called Tom ... I found him on the beach again. The only difference is that now he owns the beach!"
- **Buffett Partnership (1957 to 1969):** partnership 29.5% annually, limited partners 23.8%, Dow 7.4%; cumulative 2,794.9% against the Dow's 152.6%. "The best thing he did was to quit in 1969."
- **Sequoia Fund (Bill Ruane):** met in Graham's class in 1951; Buffett asked him to take the partners' money in 1970; Sequoia 17.2% net (18.2% gross) against S&P 10.0%. Scale law: "Size is the anchor of performance."
- **Charlie Munger:** concentrated; partnership 19.8%, limited partners 13.7%, Dow 5.0%, with -31.9% in 1973 and -31.5% in 1974. As Buffett puts it, "he happens to be a fellow whose whole psyche goes toward concentration."
- **Rick Guerin / Pacific Partners:** IBM salesman and math major; 1965 to 1983, 22,200% overall (32.9% a year) against S&P 316.4% (7.8%); "which probably because he lacks a business school education, he regards as statistically significant."
- **Stan Perlmeter:** liberal-arts major and ad-agency partner who left in 1965; 23.0% annually (19.0% for limited partners) from August 1965 to October 1983 against the Dow's roughly 7%.
- **Washington Post Master Trust and a second pension fund** he influenced to hire value managers: first or near first in most periods.
- **Epistemic caution:** "if I had just recently searched among thousands of records to select a few names for you this morning," he would "advise you to stop reading right here." Observation: "it is extraordinary to me that the idea of buying dollar bills for 40 cents takes immediately to people or it doesn't take at all," and he has "never seen anyone who became a gradual convert over a ten-year period." Why he publishes the secret: "the secret has been out for 50 years, ever since Ben Graham and Dave Dodd wrote Security Analysis, yet I have seen no trend toward value investing in the 35 years that I've practiced it," because of "some perverse human characteristic that likes to make easy things difficult."
- **Columbia 1984 against the efficient-market textbook.** "I'm convinced that there is much inefficiency in the market"; "Can you imagine buying an entire business simply because the price of the business had been marked up substantially last week and the week before?"; "As a friend said, to a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail"; "I am baffled why academicians invest extensive time and effort to see whether it makes a difference when buying small pieces of those same businesses"; "Ships will sail around the world but the Flat Earth Society will flourish."
- **The 2006 sequel: EMT's closed eyes and the Schloss test (2006).** "Rather than opening their minds, they closed their eyes," and "a finance instructor who had the nerve to question EMT had about as much chance of major promotion as Galileo had of being named Pope"; "if you are in the shipping business, it's helpful to have all of your potential competitors be taught that the earth is flat." On Schloss: "Maybe it was a good thing for his investors that Walter didn't go to college." And the chance test: had millions of managers drawn stock names from a hat and copied Schloss's trades, "the luckiest of them would not have come close to equaling his record."

### Governance, boards and executive pay

- **Fred Futile (2005): fixed-price options are free capital.** The CEO of "Stagnant, Inc." gets ten-year fixed-price options on 1% of a company earning $1B a year on $10B of net worth (100M shares, $10 a share, no dividend, buying back stock at ten times earnings). Shares fall to 38.7M, EPS grows to $25.80, the stock appreciates 158%, and Fred makes "$158 million, despite the business itself improving not at all." Retaining earnings in 5%-return projects still pays him $63M, and even with earnings down 20% he "could have made more than $100 million." "fixed-price options give them capital that is free." His fix: options "with strike prices that are adjusted for retained earnings," which consultants ("the ever-accommodating firm of Ratchet, Ratchet and Bingo") never propose.
- **Paying the extraordinary CEO, and CEO pay as the acid test (2003, 2005).** Counterpoint: "it's difficult to overpay the truly extraordinary CEO of a giant enterprise. But this species is rare" (Jim Kilts at Gillette "earned every penny"). He had served as a director of twenty public companies and "only one CEO has put me on his comp committee." The Hank Greenberg story: "Ask for a uniform." In 2003 the acid test: "In judging whether Corporate America is serious about reforming itself, CEO pay remains the acid test," because the CEO cares more about 100,000 vs 500,000 options than the comp committee does, so "the negotiation often has a 'play-money' quality."
- **Option expensing as institutional capture (2002 and 2004).** Arthur Andersen's Professional Standards Group said in 1992 that options should be expensed; "rainmaking" partners reversed it; FASB voted 7-0; the Senate voted 88-9 against; SEC chair Arthur Levitt later called bowing to it "the act of his chairmanship that he most regrets"; "Of the 500 companies in the S&P, 498 adopted the method deemed less desirable" (2002). In 2004 the House passed a pro-CEO bill 312-111. The 2004 position: he would support options with "1) an appropriate strike price, 2) an escalation in price that reflects the retention of earnings, and 3) a ban on his quickly disposing of any shares purchased through options" for his successor.
- **Options in practice: the GEICO thought experiment and the Gen Re swap (1998).** The thought experiment: replace GEICO's $190M ad budget with ten-year at-the-market Berkshire options paid to the media: would anyone say Berkshire bore no cost? Adjustment: subtracting option value from investees' earnings "frequently cut the reported per-share figures by 5%, with 10% not all that uncommon," and has changed portfolio decisions "either to make a sale or to pass on a stock purchase" (1998). Replacing Gen Re's option plan with a matching cash plan produced a pro-forma $63M compensation expense, which he called an "Alice-in-Wonderland outcome."
- **Mutual-fund directors and the independence definition (2003, 2002).** "Year after year, at literally thousands of funds, directors had routinely rehired the incumbent management company, however pathetic its performance had been." 2002: "a monkey will type out a Shakespeare play before an 'independent' mutual-fund director will suggest that his fund look at other managers"; his daughter declined a fund directorship because the fees would have raised her income "about 50%." 2003: "boardroom atmosphere" almost invariably "sedates their fiduciary genes"; proposed two annual declarations (they looked at other managers; they negotiated a fee comparable to peers') and told shareholders to heed "the maxim": "If you don't know whose side someone is on, he's probably not on yours."
- **Independence definitions and the institutional reform lever (2002 to 2006).** Independence rules count a director getting "100% of his income from director fees" as independent while Ron Olson, with about 3% of income from Berkshire legal fees, is not: "That is nonsense." Reform lever (2002): "Twenty, or even fewer, of the largest institutions, acting together, could effectively reform corporate governance" (allies named: Jack Bogle, Chris Davis, Bill Miller). 2006: "Compensation reform will only occur if the largest institutional shareholders - it would only take a few - demand a fresh look at the whole system."
- **The Coca-Cola independence episode (2004).** Institutions argued Buffett lacked "independence" as a Coca-Cola director because McLane and Dairy Queen buy Coke products, and sought his removal from the board or audit committee. His reply: Webster's "not subject to control by others," and the arithmetic: Berkshire held "$8 billion of Coke stock," so "my heart and mind belong to the owners of Coke, not to its management." Scripture: "For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also."
- **Fee-dependent directors and the owners' three questions (2004).** The 2004 example of fee-dependent directors: directors earning about $100,000 a year in fees "scuttled" a multi-billion offer favored by management and the banker, and "At the same meeting at which the deal was rejected, the board voted itself a significant increase in directors' fees." 2004: "the least independent directors are likely to be those who receive an important fraction of their annual income from the fees they receive for board service... Yet these are the very board members most often classed as 'independent.'" Three questions owners should press: "First, does the company have the right CEO? Second, is he/she overreaching in terms of compensation? Third, are proposed acquisitions more likely to create or destroy per-share value?" (2004).
- **Berkshire's own board design (2003, 2004, 2006).** Eleven directors (2003), each with family holdings above $4 million bought in the open market and "we've never passed out options or restricted shares"; fees nominal; no D&O insurance ("After all, who ever washes a rental car?"; "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big"). He calls it "owner-capitalism"; the rarest quality is business savvy: "I would be useless on a medical or scientific board" and "I could be replaced, without loss, by a potted plant." Process in 2003: shareholders could self-nominate (more than twenty did); Charlie and he, with the incumbents, picked David Gottesman, Charlotte Guyman, Don Keough and Tom Murphy, none of whom nominated themselves.
- **Picking Berkshire directors (2006).** 2006 criteria: "owner-oriented, business-savvy, interested and truly independent"; "I have yet to hear anyone ask, 'Does he think like an intelligent owner?'"; sarcasm at diversity-first searches: "It sometimes sounds as if the mission is to stock Noah's ark." Comp rule: "We issue no lottery tickets that carry payoffs unrelated to business performance. If a CEO bats .300, he gets paid for being a .300 hitter, even if circumstances outside of his control cause Berkshire to perform poorly." He calls himself the "Typhoid Mary of compensation committees" after serving on 19 boards.
- **The 2019 board critique, set against his own board (2019, 2023).** In 2019 the critique is sharper: "the independent directors, in many cases, are the least independent"; a typical $250,000 fee means directors "just not going to upset the apple cart"; and "you don't get invited to be on boards if you belch too often at the dinner table." **[CHECK, dated]** The board-design defense (2003, 2004, 2006) is the earlier position; the 2019 "cocker spaniel" critique (baseline) is the later one; in 2023 shareholders rejected an independent-chair proposal about 10 to 1 (US News). The tension between the stated criteria and Berkshire's own long-tenured, friend-heavy board is in Blind spots.
- **Audit committees (2002).** Four questions: (1) if the auditor were solely responsible, would the statements differ? (2) would an investor have received the essential information in plain English? (3) is the company following the same internal audit procedure the auditor would if CEO? (4) is the auditor aware of any actions "that have had the purpose and effect of moving revenues or expenses from one reporting period to another?" Ask them a week before earnings; haste is "the enemy of accuracy." "Audit committees can't audit."
- **Financial CEOs after the crisis (2009).** "Collectively, they have lost more than $500 billion in just the four largest financial fiascos of the last two years." Shareholders lost "90% or more of the value of their holdings" but "The CEOs and directors of the failed companies, however, have largely gone unscathed." Remedy: "some meaningful sticks now need to be part of their employment picture as well," paid personally, "not reimbursable by the companies they've damaged nor by insurance."
- **CEO pay and disclosure reform (2025):** see Signature quotes.

### Accounting, disclosure and communication

- **"Accounting Part 2" (1998): restructuring and merger accounting.** "Son of Gresham: Bad accounting drives out good." Evidence: R. G. Associates counted 1,369 special charges totaling $72.1B in 1998, against 1997 Fortune 500 earnings of $324B; the golf analogy (a 140 followed by four 80s instead of 91, 94, 89, 94, 92); reserve padding at deal time ("uncanny how often an actuarial 'revelation' of this kind coincides with the inking of a deal"); two bank CEOs overheard on a merger, one asking "But won't that mean a huge charge" and being told the charge would be "bigger than that" because that was why they were doing the deal. Contrast: "In all of our acquisitions, we have left the loss reserve figures exactly as we found them" and "If we are to disappoint you, we would rather it be with our earnings than with our accounting." He borrows Kinsley: "The scandal isn't in what's done that's illegal but rather in what's legal." He praised SEC chair Arthur Levitt's "earnings management" speech and chided auditors who "kowtow" to managers.
- **Pro forma upside down (2002).** Berkshire's pro-forma was about $500M pre-tax lower than reported (no mega-cat year; fixed-income arbitrage gains that would not recur). "In these presentations, the CEO tells his owners 'don't count this, don't count that – just count what makes earnings fat.'" "'Except for' losses will forever be part of the insurance business, and they will forever be paid with shareholders' money." Jack Benny: "I don't deserve this honor – but, then, I have arthritis, and I don't deserve that either."
- **Pooling and goodwill (1999).** Embracing "the fiction of pooling": "So much for poetry." He sided with FASB on ending pooling but opposed mandatory goodwill amortization (see Mental models). Congress intervening would be "a truly terrible idea."
- **Regulation FD and forecasts (2000).** Selective disclosure "was corrupt behavior, unfortunately embraced by both Wall Street and corporate America." "The fact that this reform came about because of coercion rather than conscience should be a matter of shame for CEOs and their investor relations departments." On forecasts: "it is both deceptive and dangerous for CEOs to predict growth rates for their companies," with a testable wager that fewer than 10 of the 200 most profitable companies in 2000 will reach 15% annual EPS growth over 20 years; lofty predictions "corrode CEO behavior," pushing "accounting shenanigans" that "can turn fudging into fraud." The Owner's Manual: "The CEO who misleads others in public may eventually mislead himself in private" and "We will always tell you how many strokes we have taken on each hole and never play around with the scorecard."
- **Reading footnotes (2001, 2002, 2006).** "Insurance accounting is a self-graded exam" (see Insurance); "unintelligible footnotes usually indicate untrustworthy management" (2002); "We sometimes encounter accounting footnotes about important transactions that leave us baffled, and we go away suspicious that the reporting company wished it that way" (2006). The Value Capital consolidation (2003): he calls the requirement "inappropriate" because its liabilities, "usually are above $20 billion," are "in no way ours."
- **Pension and private-equity return fiction (2006, 2019).** "private pension plans can temporarily overstate their earnings, and public pension plans can defer the need for increased taxes, by using investment assumptions that are likely to be out of reach. Actuaries and auditors go along with these tactics" (2006). A 2019 anecdote: a seller wanted Berkshire to count $110 million of earnings on a $100 million earner; a private-equity email told its manager to add 15% to projections because "Buffett will discount it by 15 percent or 20 percent anyway."
- **Rumor reporting (2000).** The Wall Street Journal of Dec 29, 2000 reported Berkshire was buying Conseco and Finova bonds; "to this day neither Berkshire nor I have ever bought a share of stock or a bond of Conseco." He suggested a "Todays Rumors" column and wrote that "Journalisms First Principle should require that the media be scrupulous" (source drops apostrophes). Stance on not commenting on holdings: "Good investment ideas are rare, valuable and subject to competitive appropriation just as good product or business acquisition ideas are" (Owner's Manual; 1998: "never-comment-even-if-untrue" to protect owners from "piggybackers"; in 1997 he broke the rule for silver, zero-coupon bonds and a third position because regulators' questions about silver implied they wanted acknowledgment and the zero-coupon holding was large enough to affect net worth).
- **Who really bought, and what he says about Berkshire shares.** "Neither publicly or privately have I ever told anyone to buy or sell Berkshire shares" (1999). The 2004 rule that stories should say Berkshire buys, not Buffett buys: "The media continue to report that 'Buffett buys' this or that stock... the stories should say 'Berkshire buys.'"

### Macro, currency and the never-forecast rule in practice

- **The currency position (2002 to 2006) as a documented exception.** "Before March 2002, neither Berkshire nor I had ever traded in currencies" (2004). By 2003 about $12 billion across five currencies; by year end 2004 $21.4B across 12 currencies. Trigger: the U.S. current-account deficit ($618B in 2004, up from $263B in 1999), a wealth transfer "at the rate of $1.8 billion daily," foreigners owning net about $3 trillion of the U.S., projected near $11 trillion in a decade. Frameworks: "A budget deficit in no way reduces the portion of the national pie that goes to Americans" but current-account deficits mean "we own less and less of what we produce… the rest of the world enjoys an ever-growing royalty on American output"; "A country that is now aspiring to an 'Ownership Society' will not find happiness in – and I'll use hyperbole here for emphasis – a 'Sharecropper's Society.'" He still says "true trade… is enormously beneficial for both us and them." Disagreement: both parties' policymakers and the Fed's "soft landing" hope: "a gently declining dollar would not provide the answer" (his Nov 10, 2003 Fortune article).
- **The currency position's scorecard and exit (2003 to 2006).** Results: 2003 gains of $825M from foreign exchange; 2005 "My conviction, however, cost Berkshire $955 million pre-tax in 2005" though Berkshire was "$2.0 billion in the black" since inception; 2006: a $2.2B total gain since 2002 ($186M in 2006), led by euro $839.2M, Canadian dollar $398.3M, pound $287.2M, won $261.3M, Australian dollar $247.1M, peso $106.1M, NZ dollar $102.6M, with losses in the Taiwan dollar (45.3), yuan (12.7), Singapore dollar (2.6) and Hong Kong dollar (2.5); he ended direct positions because carry turned negative in 2005 and moved into foreign equities or U.S. stocks with big foreign earnings. In 2006: "we've used up our bank account and turned to our credit card," the U.S. investment-income account turned negative for the first time since 1915, and "to expect a 'soft landing' seems like wishful thinking." Also "Americans will live better ten or twenty years from now than they do today." In 2005: "Either Americans address the problem soon in a way we select, or at some point the problem will likely address us in an unpleasant way of its own."
- **How he reconciled it with the never-forecast rule (in his words).** 2003: "the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters. We have in fact made few macro forecasts at Berkshire." 2004: "macro-economics is a tough game in which few people, Charlie and I included, have demonstrated skill. We may well turn out to be wrong in our currency judgments"; "(Indeed, the fact that so many pundits now predict weakness for the dollar makes us uneasy.)" On reputation: "From a reputational standpoint, Charlie and I run a clear risk with our foreign-exchange commitment. But we believe in managing Berkshire as if we owned 100% of it ourselves. And, were that the case, we would not be following a dollar-only policy." Keynes: "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally." He also hedged his preference: a prompt solution "would likely cause Berkshire to record losses on its foreign-exchange contracts," since "Berkshire's resources remain heavily concentrated in dollar-based assets."
- **Macro calls after the currency book (2019, 2023).** **[CHECK, dated and resolved]** 2003 to 2005: he stated and sized a macro view with explicit error bars. 2023: he and Abel disclaim currency forecasting outright (quoted under Raise vs. bootstrap / leverage), and the yen exposure is financed with yen debt, which removes direction risk. 2019: a directional view on inflation and deficits with a record of being wrong: "I can't reconcile a five percent budget deficit in a world of law [sic] when unemployment low interest rates negative interest rates in many countries… In economics, like other things, you could never do only one thing" (CNBC May 2019, unofficial transcript). The rule he actually follows (my reading): he voices macro views in writing, labels the error bar, and never lets one drive the core portfolio.
- **Zero-coupon bonds and silver (1997).** Silver: 111.2 million ounces bought in 1997 (pre-tax gain $97.4M); oil derivatives (14.0M barrels left of a 45.7M barrel position set 1994 to 1995; 31.7M settled for a pre-tax gain of about $61.9M); zero-coupon Treasuries ($4.6B at amortized cost, unrealized pre-tax gain $598.8M when rates fell): "If rates rise, you lose heavily with zeros, and if rates fall, you make outsized gains." The accountability line: "you pay Charlie and me to use our best judgment -- not to avoid embarrassment -- and we will occasionally make an unconventional move when we believe the odds favor it." And the honest limit: "A macro-based commitment such as this never has anything close to a 100% probability of being successful." He also distinguishes: a wonderful business at an attractive price means "we are going to make money -- the only question being when"; with these, "we think that we are going to make money. But we also recognize that we will sometimes realize losses, occasionally of substantial size" (1997).
- **Valuing the market without forecasting (1997, 1999).** At the 1997 meeting (Dow 7,071, long Treasury 6.89%) the market was not overvalued if rates stayed or fell and returns on equity stayed exceptionally high; the letter then warned that "price levels… have materially eroded the 'margin of safety'." He recalled his 1979 Forbes piece "You pay a very high price in the stock market for a cheery consensus." Companions: "We have never attempted to forecast what the stock market is going to do in the next month or the next year" (1999) and "Fools give you reasons, wise men never try" (a song line, 1999).
- **Weekly data he does track (2017).** AAR railcar loadings (Wednesdays), credit-card volumes (Visa, American Express, JPMorgan), and home-related volumes (Clayton, brick, carpet, paint, brokerage); the economy is roughly a 2% grower since fall 2009; "I've never made a trade in a stock based on a GDP figure."

### Derivatives: the 2002 alarm and the 2009 counterweight

- **The alarm (2002, 2003, 2004).** "Time bombs" and "financial weapons of mass destruction" (2002, Signature quotes). Operational detail in 2002: the reinsurance and derivatives businesses "are similar: Like Hell, both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit." "mark-to-model degenerates into what I would call mark-to-myth." Contracts on "the number of twins to be born in Nebraska in 2020" illustrate how both parties' models can show profit. "I can assure you that the marking errors in the derivatives business have not been symmetrical. Almost invariably, they have favored either the trader who was eyeing a multi-million dollar bonus or the CEO who wanted to report impressive 'earnings' (or both)." Systemic mechanics: rating-downgrade collateral triggers cause a "pile-on effect" ending in a liquidity crisis; "daisy-chain risk"; LTCM 1998 as the live test; total-return swaps "make a joke of margin requirements." On reading derivative footnotes: "the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running."
- **The derivatives alarm restated (2004).** "Like Hell, derivative trading is easy to enter but difficult to leave. (Other similarities come to mind as well.)" "The market prices of derivatives, however, can be very fuzzy... It's small wonder that phantom profits are often recorded." "rapid growth sometimes masks major underlying problems (and occasionally fraud). The real test of the earning power of a derivatives operation is what it achieves after operating for an extended period in a no-growth mode. You only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out." (The swimming-naked line appears in print here and in the 2001 letter as "After all, you only find out who is swimming naked when the tide goes out"; the baseline cites CNBC 2023.)
- **The counterweight (2002, 2006, 2009): he still uses them.** 2002: "at Berkshire, I sometimes engage in large-scale derivatives transactions in order to facilitate certain investment strategies." 2006: "derivatives, just like stocks and bonds, are sometimes wildly mispriced… We currently have 62 contracts outstanding. I manage them personally, and they are free of counterparty credit risk."
- **The 2008 to 2009 derivative book in numbers, and risk control (2009).** Equity put and credit contracts with quarterly valuation swings of -$1.517B, +$2.357B, +$1.732B and +$1.052B; derivatives float about $6.3B (separate from $62B of insurance float); posting requirement of $1.7B at the 2008 low; terms on about 10% of equity puts changed (maturities shortened, strikes lowered) with no money changing hands. "We have long invested in derivatives contracts that Charlie and I think are mispriced, just as we try to invest in mispriced stocks and bonds." And: "Charlie and I believe that a CEO must not delegate risk control. ... If Berkshire ever gets in trouble, it will be my fault. It will not be because of misjudgments made by a Risk Committee or Chief Risk Officer." **[CHECK, resolved]** The consistent thread across 2002 and 2009 is structure, not instrument: unlimited counterparty chains, marked to models, collateral triggers and incentive-driven marks are the danger; long-dated, uncollateralized, personally managed positions are not.
- **The 2008 trough in his own words (Charlie Rose, 2008, and the 2008 letter):** "Beware of geeks bearing formulas" (2008 letter); "I believe in Mark to market" (disclosure honesty), versus the 2018 GAAP rule routing unrealized gains through net income, "worse-than-useless" (2023). 2023 numbers: GAAP net earnings $90B (2021), $(23)B (2022), $96B (2023) against operating earnings of $27.6B, $30.9B and $37.4B; swings from unrealized gains can "exceed $5 billion a day"; yet "Make no mistake about the significance of capital gains: I expect them to be a very important component of Berkshire's value accretion during the decades ahead."

### Taxes, social contract, productivity and politics

- **Tax statements across 48 years.** 1998: "Writing checks to the IRS that include strings of zeros does not bother Charlie or me" (Berkshire and Gen Re paid $2.7B for 1998, "all of the U.S. Government's expenses for more than a half-day"; capital gains taxed at 35%, 75% higher than an individual's). 2003: Berkshire paid $3.3B, about 2.5% of all U.S. corporate income tax against about 1% of corporate market value; "if only 540 taxpayers paid the amount Berkshire will pay, no other individual or corporation would have to pay anything"; he rebutted Treasury's Pamela Olson, who had said he "has played the tax code like a fiddle"; corporate income taxes had fallen from 32% of federal receipts (1952) to 7.4%: "If class warfare is being waged in America, my class is clearly winning."
- **Taxes, the paperwork and the 2017 House bill (1944 to 2017).** His first 1040 at 13 (1944): "$35 for a bicycle" and "my tax bill was $7." Berkshire's 2002 return ran 8,905 pages. 2006: $4.4B "for more than half of one day" of federal spending, 9,386 pages. 2015: a 30,400-page federal return and 3,530 state returns. 2011 and ProPublica are in Signature quotes and Blind spots. 2017 (CNBC): "Based on the House bill, I'm a lot healthier now than I was a week ago financially" (about $680,000 saved on a federal bill of just under $4 million from repeal of the 3.8% net-investment-income tax), and "if there's one clear-cut message that comes out of that bill, it is we're going to cut the hell out of income taxes for the rich on investment income." Corporate tax is "2% of GDP" while health care "has gone from 5% of GDP to 17% of GDP," so health care, not corporate tax, is the bigger competitive burden. "I am very suspicious of dynamic scoring"; he is skeptical of repatriation holidays because "that encourages more investment abroad." On single payer: "I personally do" agree.
- **Productivity and the people who pay for it (2015, 2017).** The engine of prosperity: farming went from 11 million of a 28 million workforce in 1900 (40%) to about 3 million of 158 million; corn yield from 30 to over 150 bushels an acre; railroads 1947 to 2014: workforce 1.35 million to 187,000, ton-miles 655 billion to 1.85 trillion, real price per ton-mile down 55%, saving shippers about $90B a year; BNSF 1996 to 2015: ton-miles +71% while employees rose 4%; BHE Iowa: 3,700 to 3,500 employees while output rose from 19 to 29 million megawatt-hours, "without a rate increase for 16 years" while industry rates rose 44% (2015). The flip side: Dexter's Maine workforce (see Named mistakes); New Bedford textile workers "had no Plan B." Remedy: a reformed and expanded Earned Income Tax Credit: "The price of achieving ever-increasing prosperity for the great majority of Americans should not be penury for the unfortunate." And "(It was fortunate that horses couldn't vote.)"
- **Productivity and its casualties, in the 2017 and 2019 interviews.** In 2017: "if we had not changed any ways we did business, we would be living as we lived in 1776"; "productivity gains are the only way that consumption gains come"; "we have to take care of people who are the roadkill in better output for all the rest of us"; on 3G, they "have followed the standard capitalist formula" of "trying to do business with fewer people," "it's a painful process"; Coca-Cola's 1,200 headquarters job cuts: "prosperous companies tend to be sloppier than companies that are in tougher businesses," and of Berkshire, "a certain amount of slop in them," "that's a defect of mine." 2019: "I'm a card-carrying capitalist"; "capitalism does involve regulation. It involves taking care of people who are left behind"; "I generally think the private sector does a better job than the public sector in most things"; the healthcare venture with J.P. Morgan and Amazon because spending rose from 5% to 17 or 18% of GDP.
- **Capital-light giants (2017).** "close to 10% of the market value perhaps of the United States in five extremely good businesses that essentially take no capital": the five largest U.S. companies "could run those five businesses with no equity capital." Steel, railroads, utilities, Exxon and autos took heavy capital. He would "love it" if Berkshire had more of these but "I just haven't been able to build" many (See's is his example). Implication: arguments about capital shortages are "built on this conception of how business was 50 years ago."
- **Political neutrality (2017).** "I do not make investment or business decisions based on who is president or who I think is going to be president"; "I have never called the president in my life." "Berkshire Hathaway parent has never, to my knowledge, employed a lobbyist, and certainly has never made a contribution in 52 years to a political candidate, from the dog catcher up to the president of the United States." He had been "100% for Hillary and did a lot of fundraising." He recalled his father storing sugar in the basement when Democrats won; "it isn't a question of being in the market, it's a question of owning businesses" and "if I wanted to own farms I wouldn't keep buying and selling 'em based on some election result."
- **Climate (2006, 2015, 2023).** 2006: "Were the terrible hurricane seasons of 2004-05 aberrations? Or were they our planet's first warning that the climate of the 21st Century will differ materially from what we've seen in the past?" and "it would be a huge mistake to bet that evolving atmospheric changes are benign in their implications for insurers." 2015: "It seems highly likely to me that climate change poses a major problem for the planet," framed with Pascal's Wager and "Noah's Law": "If an ark may be essential for survival, begin building it today, no matter how cloudless the skies appear." But "when you are thinking only as a shareholder of a major insurer, climate change should not be on your list of worries," because one-year policies reprice: "Increased possibilities of loss translate promptly into increased premiums." Super-cat rates had fallen so Berkshire "backed away from that business." **[CHECK, dated]** 2006 treats it as a risk to insurers; 2015 separates the planetary question from Berkshire's insurance economics; the 2023 vote against TCFD-aligned proposals (US News, baseline) is consistent with the 2015 framing but not with a transparency-first view.
- **The fortress and the casino (2023).** "markets can – and will – unpredictably seize up or even vanish as they did for four months in 1914 and for a few days in 2001"; "think back to September 2008." "markets now exhibit far more casino-like behavior than they did when I was young. The casino now resides in many homes and daily tempts the occupants." On Wall Street: "what truly causes its denizens' juices to flow is feverish activity. At such times, whatever foolishness can be marketed will be vigorously marketed – not by everyone but always by someone." The pledge: "One investment rule at Berkshire has not and will not change: Never risk permanent loss of capital." "Extreme fiscal conservatism is a corporate pledge we make to those who have joined us in ownership of Berkshire," "akin to an insurance policy on a fortress-like building thought to be fireproof," and Berkshire's role in a crisis is "to function as an asset to the country… and to help extinguish the financial fire rather than to be among the many companies that, inadvertently or otherwise, ignited the conflagration."
- **Size limits the upside (2023).** Berkshire's GAAP net worth was $561 billion against $8.9 trillion for the other 499 S&P companies (2022), "nearly 6% of the universe"; "Doubling our huge base is simply not possible within, say, a five-year period"; "Those days are long behind us; size did us in"; the goal is "somewhat better prospects" and "materially less risk of permanent loss of capital" ("Anything beyond 'slightly better,' though, is wishful thinking").
- **Bertie, the model reader (2023).** "In visualizing the owners that Berkshire seeks, I am lucky to have the perfect mental model, my sister, Bertie." "She is sensible – very sensible – instinctively knowing that pundits should always be ignored. After all, if she could reliably predict tomorrow's winners, would she freely share her valuable insights and thereby increase competitive buying? That would be like finding gold and then handing a map to the neighbors showing its location." Her record: active until 1980, then, at 46 and "independent of any urgings from her brother," she kept only the mutual fund and Berkshire and "made no new trades during the next 43 years. During that period, she became very rich, even after making large philanthropic gifts (think nine figures)." The target audience is "investors who trust Berkshire with their savings without any expectation of resale," entitled to "both the good and bad news, delivered directly from their CEO and not from an investor-relations officer or communications consultant forever serving up optimism and syrupy mush."

### Career, character and advice to others (U. Florida 1998, Salomon, annual meetings)

- **The classmate exercise.** Buy 10% of a classmate's lifetime earnings: the winner is not the highest IQ but the one you "admire the most": "generous, honest and who gave credit to other people for their own ideas." The short pick is egotistical, greedy, "cuts corners, who is slightly dishonest." "The chains of habit are too light to be felt until they are too heavy to be broken." "you will be the one you want to buy 10% of when you are all through. And the beauty of it is that you already own 100% of yourself and you are stuck with it." Pete Kiewit's rule: integrity, intelligence and energy; "if they don't have integrity, you want them dumb and lazy" (rbcpa 1998).
- **Work and money.** "take a job that if you were independently wealthy that would be the job you would take"; "isn't that a little like saving up sex for your old age?"; "I only work with people I like." 2019: "delayed gratification is not necessarily an unqualified course of action under all circumstances"; "I always believed in spending two or three cents out of every dollar I earn and saving the rest"; "if you aren't happy having $50,000 or a hundred-thousand dollars, you're not going to be happy if you have 50 million or a hundred million." 2015: "Much of what you become in life depends on whom you choose to admire and copy. Start with Tom Murphy, and you'll never need a second exemplar." 1999: "Though 'working' means nothing to me financially, I love doing it at Berkshire for some simple reasons: It gives me a sense of achievement, a freedom to act as I see fit and an opportunity to interact daily with people I like and trust."
- **On learning.** "I'd be crazy if I went around people like Charlie and Bill and wasn't learning" (2017); "you really do learn a lot about human behavior" with age; "I don't think you can get to be an expert on human behavior at all by reading books" (2019). "We disagree on things a lot but we don't have arguments about it" (U. Florida 1998, on Munger). In 1998 on Gates: "Gates is the best businessman I have ever run into," though he will not value Microsoft, Oracle or Lotus.
- **How to start a fund (2019, on the 1956 partnership).** He showed seven people the agreement, told them "You don't need to read this," and wanted people "in sync with me" so they would not panic: "you ought to have your own ground rules as to what your expectations are," including when investors should praise you and "when they should throw bricks at you"; no institutions, "because institutions meant committees."
- **Arbitrage and real estate.** About 300 arbitrage situations over time (the first in 1955, cocoa beans) contribute about "½ - ¾ a percentage point of our return a year" (1998). Real estate: "If there was a LTCM situation translated to real estate, we would be open to that," and a REIT manager who says the stock is cheap at $28 and then sells at $28 less commission has "a disconnect" (rbcpa 1998). Alternatives: "I would not get excited about so-called alternative investments" (2019); LTCM people "do the same thing again."
- **Bitcoin (2019).** Munger's happy-hour quip aside, Buffett recalled his 1952 Las Vegas honeymoon: the players knew the game was "mathematically dumb" and said "We are going to make a lot of money"; "bitcoin has rejuvenated that feeling in me." (The 2023 "gambling token" quote is in Signature quotes.)
- **Capital markets as a decision about hamburgers (1998):** "If you are a net eater of hamburger over the next ten years, you want hamburger to go down unless you are a cattle producer." "The NYSE is one big supermarket of companies." "The stock doesn't know you own it." Graham's chapters 8 and 20 are "the two most important essays ever written on investing."

- **Gold and sterile assets.** "You can fondle the cube, but it will not respond" (2011).
- **Crypto.** "something like Bitcoin, you know, it is something it's a gambling token. And doesn't have any intrinsic value… but that doesn't stop people from wanting to play a roulette wheel" (CNBC 2023); "I've always wanted to get rich slow… if I were to get into gambling, I would wanna own the roulette wheel, and not be a roulette player" (CNBC 2023).
- **Size and honesty about returns.** "A fat wallet, however, is the enemy of superior investment results" (1994); "For anyone deploying capital, nothing recedes like success" (1997); "Berkshire's capital base is now simply too large to allow us to earn truly outsized returns. If you believe otherwise, you should consider a career in sales but avoid one in mathematics" (1998; a 1983 version ends "pursue a career in sales, but avoid one in mathematics").
- **Patience.** "Our elephant gun has been reloaded, and my trigger finger is itchy" (2010); "In allocating capital, activity does not correlate with achievement" (1998).
- **The preening duck.** "In a bull market, one must avoid the error of the preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world." "Our appraisal of 1997's performance, then: Quack" (1997).

**On risk, leverage and the crowd**
- **Leverage.** "to make money they didn't have and didn't need, they risked what they did have and what they did need. That is just plain foolish" (LTCM, U. Florida 1998); the geeks-bearing-formulas and anything-times-zero lines are under Derivatives and Decision heuristics.
- **On the crowd.** "we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful" (1986); "when the tide goes out you learn who's been swimming naked… we actually ran into a nudist colony here… in terms of banks" (CNBC 2023).
- **Noah.** "Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does" (2001, his "Noah rule," stated while admitting he had broken it).

**On character, reputation and his own record**
- **Luck, named.** "Charlie and I happily acknowledge that much of Berkshire's success has simply been a product of… 'The American Tailwind'… We are lucky – gloriously lucky – to have that force at our back" (2018).
- **Mistakes in his own voice.** "I talked when I should have walked" (2004); "I subtly indicated that I was older and wiser. I was just older" (2009). (The 2002 line "I wish I didn't know now what I didn't know then" is a country-song lyric he quotes, not his own.)
- **A borrowed rule he lives by.** Tom Murphy's "praise by name, criticize by category" (2024; Munger's bad-news dictum is under Decision heuristics).
- **The exemplar.** "When I say that I like to be associated with managers whom I would love to have as a sibling, in-law, or trustee of my will, Murph is the exemplar of what I mean" (1995, of Tom Murphy).
- **Farewell register.** "Kindness is costless but also priceless"; "Decide what you would like your obituary to say and live the life to deserve it"; "Keep in mind that the cleaning lady is as much a human being as the Chairman"; Father Time "is undefeated; for him, everyone ends up on his score card as 'wins'" (Nov 2025).

## Voice & style

Folksy, plain-spoken, self-deprecating, teacherly: a brilliant analyst who deliberately talks like your smartest uncle so the lesson lands. He reaches for homely analogies (Mr. Market, the castle-and-moat, the gold cube, an elephant gun, robins and spring, the preening duck, the batboy Eddie Bennett, the Gotrocks family and its helpers, Fred Futile, the hamburger quiz, Aesop's birds, "jumbo shrimp" and "lady mudwrestler" for institutional-investor oxymorons) and Midwestern understatement. He teaches by parable (Aesop; the Li'l Abner compounding tables; the golfer who "restructures" a 140 into four 80s; the commuter who cheers higher gas prices because his tank is full; the Scarlett O'Hara line on teaser mortgages; the rented suit at the funeral; the horse who is "walking fine" and gets "peddled as Secretariat") and quotes a fixed cast: Graham, Keynes ("I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong"), Mae West ("Too much of a good thing can be wonderful"), Ben Franklin, Churchill, Ronald Reagan, Jack Benny, Pascal, Polonius, Horace, Casey Stengel, Billy Rose, and Munger.

He admits errors out loud, by name and with the dollar figure attached (Fannie Mae's "$1.4 billion," Dexter's "$5.7 billion," Precision Castparts' "$11 billion," Gillette's $625 million, NetJets' $157 million, the GEICO credit card's further $44 million loss) and grades himself on a letter scale (a "D" for 1999; "Quack" for 1997). He is blunt about fees, derivatives, EBITDA, executive pay, forecasters, and his own omissions; he hedges on macro and on precise valuations (intrinsic value is "fuzzy," two honest people will differ). He explains the cost of his virtues rather than hiding it: "just standing there, day after day, with my bat on my shoulder is not my idea of fun" (1997). His sentences tend to set up a general rule, turn it on a vivid concrete case, and close on a one-line aphorism; he states what would change his mind (price, not narrative), and he credits managers by name, always.

Note the register shift over time: the Partnership letters are precise and contractual (the numbered Ground Rules, asset tables to the dollar, "old-fogeyism (after all, I am 37)"); the 1980s letters are parable-driven and appendix-heavy (the See's goodwill math); the 1990s and 2000s letters are pedagogical and crusading on accounting and governance (options, pro-forma, mutual-fund directors) with long lists of acquisition origin stories; the 2010s are statistical and patriotic (GDP per capita, productivity); the Nov 2025 farewell is a memoirist's voice, openly mortal, still wry ("I'm 'going quiet.' Sort of"), closing on aphorism rather than arithmetic.

The CNBC interviews (2017, 2019, 2023) are looser and more candid than the letters: asked why he did not buy Amazon, he answers "Stupidity." Distinguish him from Munger: Munger is terser and more caustic ("If something is not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well," which Buffett relays as something "Charlie regularly reminds me," 1994; "Are we supposed to applaud because the dog that fouls our lawn is a Chihuahua rather than a Saint Bernard?"; "I feel like a horse's ass for not identifying Google better"), and it was Munger who supplied the strategic pivot ("buy wonderful businesses at fair prices") that Buffett then spent decades narrating, with, as Buffett says, "much back-sliding."

## Blind spots

- **His alpha may be a factor bet, not magic.** *Buffett's Alpha* (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, NBER w19681) finds that controlling for Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk "drives the alpha of Berkshire's public stock portfolio down to a statistically insignificant annualized 0.3%," estimates "Buffett's leverage is about 1.6-to-1 on average," and concludes the returns are "reward for the use of leverage combined with a focus on cheap, safe, quality stocks." Read charitably this validates his framework; read critically the edge is systematizable and partly cheap leverage (float), not unique genius.
- **Tax hypocrisy, by the numbers (and the Partnership-era contrast).** ProPublica's "Secret IRS Files" found that 2014 to 2018 Buffett's wealth rose **$24.3 billion** while he paid **$23.7 million** in tax, "a true tax rate of 0.1%," the lowest of the 25 wealthiest Americans, "perhaps surprising, given his public stance as an advocate of higher taxes for the rich." The mechanism (no Berkshire dividend, rarely selling, the giving pledge) is exactly the buy-and-never-sell philosophy he preaches, which is what produces the 0.1% and the tension with "Stop Coddling the Super-Rich" (2011). His own sources show the earlier stance ("maximize investment gains, not minimize taxes," Partnership 1963 and 1965) and the 2017 remark that under the House bill's repeal of the 3.8% net-investment-income tax "I'm a lot healthier now than I was a week ago financially," and the 2003 sentence "my class is clearly winning." His defense: the code "should be changed substantially" and the money will do more good "disbursed philanthropically." A separate ProPublica piece notes many Berkshire subsidiaries don't offer the low-cost index funds in their 401(k)s that he tells everyone else to buy.
- **Kraft Heinz.** "We overpaid for Kraft" (CNBC 2019; the 2019 meeting version is in the Named mistakes ledger), tied to the 3G cost-cutting partnership and a $15.4B brand writedown (and a $3B writedown on the 26.7% stake in Feb 2019): his quality-at-a-fair-price test misfiring on a business whose "moat" (packaged-food brands against retailers) eroded faster than he priced. He conceded "we did underestimate, not what the consumer is doing so much, but what the retailer is."
- **Wells Fargo: governance rhetoric vs. passive conduct.** His sharpest documented blind spot: the 1990 "best managers in the business" bet became a fraud (millions of fake accounts, ~$185M in fines, retaliation against whistleblowers). Buffett diagnosed it precisely ("You have to be very careful what you incentivize"; "the CEO has to act," Quartz 2017) yet, as the largest (~10%, ~$26B) shareholder, took no public action to stop it and sold no shares at the time. His 2017 reply ("I was not a director"), his 2019 defense of Sloan, and Munger's contrary view are all dated above; the 2019 "cocker spaniel" board-independence critique sits in direct tension with his own conduct here, and the 2005 line that he called Wells "a company that Dick Kovacevich runs brilliantly" is the pre-scandal high-water mark.
- **The Sokol affair (2011).** David Sokol, then a leading internal succession candidate, bought Lubrizol shares shortly before pitching the Lubrizol acquisition to Buffett; Berkshire bought Lubrizol, Sokol's stake gained ~$3M, and he resigned in March 2011. Berkshire's audit committee found he had violated company insider-trading and ethics standards, and Buffett was widely criticized for an initially soft public response before the committee's harder finding. *(This is a matter of public record, Berkshire's own 2011 press release/audit-committee report and contemporaneous SEC coverage, summarized here from that record; it was not independently re-verified against a primary URL in this rebuild, so treat the framing as public-record context, not a quote.)* The 2009 letter shows Sokol had been installed as NetJets CEO in August 2009 to fix a unit where, in Buffett's words, "I failed you in letting NetJets descend into this condition."
- **Decades-long tech avoidance was costly (and he says why).** Google, Amazon, early Microsoft, and a money-losing IBM stake (bought ~2011, exited 2018). He concedes the misses; Munger's 2014 estimate of what omissions such as Walmart cost is in the Named mistakes ledger. The very discipline that built the record kept Berkshire underweight the basket that won the 2010s to 2020s. (Apple, bought 2016+, is the late correction, and the 2017 anchoring confession is the behavioral mechanism: having seen X, buying at 10X is hard.) 1999 is the first documented case: he stood aside from tech in the year he graded himself a D.
- **The airlines round-trip.** After decades of shunning airlines as commodity/capital-intensive ("It's a job I don't want, running an airline," 2017), he bought ~10% of the big four (~2016), then sold the entire stake at the 2020 trough, by his own account his mistake and his decision alone (2020 meeting; paraphrase, exact transcript wording could not be verified, see Provenance). The stocks recovered substantially within ~18 months. USAir (1989, $358M) had already shown the failure mode; the 2017 interview names it as his own mistake: "I made it all by myself."
- **Derivatives: rules for thee?** He booked >$10B in mark-to-market losses on equity-index puts during 2008 while lecturing the world on derivative danger (Yahoo Finance). On his own reading it is structure, not hypocrisy (long maturities, no collateral triggers, positions he manages himself, 2006 and 2009), but the optics were real and his counterparties bore Berkshire-credit risk.
- **Macro and currency: the exception to his own rule.** The 2002 to 2006 currency book was a macro view held by a man who says "macro-economics is a tough game"; it cost $955M pre-tax in 2005 even if it finished about $2.2B ahead by 2006. He disclosed the reputational risk himself (2004). The 2003 to 2006 insistence that the U.S. current-account position would end badly, and the 2019 belief that low inflation could not coexist with policy, both sit next to his admission that "I've been wrong on that for some time." In 1979 he held 15-year bonds and did not sell: a duration call that turned out wrong as yields peaked in 1981 (my inference from the 1979 letter's own account).
- **Governance and transparency gaps he champions elsewhere.** Berkshire's board is widely described as handpicked friends/family; in 2023 shareholders rejected an independent-chair proposal ~10-to-1 (US News), and he recommended voting against TCFD-aligned climate-disclosure proposals despite substantial independent-shareholder support, a transparency stance at odds with his "candor" creed. The 2003 to 2006 letters define "independence" as owning stock bought in the open market and not relying on director fees; that is a standard Berkshire meets, but the board is still friend-heavy by any outside measure. Berkshire also has no D&O insurance and keeps directors' downside above shareholders'. He conceded in 2002 that for years he sat silent on other boards ("collegiality trumped independence"), and in 2004 that he should have introduced executive sessions and a whistleblower line decades earlier.
- **Philanthropy critique, from inside the family.** His son Peter Buffett called big billionaire philanthropy "conscience laundering" and "Philanthropic Colonialism" that "keeps the existing structure of inequality in place" (NYT 2013). Warren's own delegation logic ("What can be more logical, in whatever you want done, than finding someone better equipped than you are to do it? Who wouldn't select Tiger Woods to take his place in a high-stakes golf game?", Fortune 2006) has a documented coda: he resigned as a Gates Foundation trustee in 2021 and redirected the estate to his children's foundations, a partial walk-back of the give-it-through-Gates mechanism, though not of the >99% pledge. His 2006 argument against perpetual foundations is an opinion with no outcome data behind it.
- **Scale now caps returns, by his own admission, and has since 1966.** "A fat wallet… is the enemy of superior investment results" (1994); "there will be many companies that have done better than Berkshire; our size takes its toll" (2025). The record: per-share investments compounded 27.5% from 1965 to 2006 but only 12.6% from 1995 to 2006 (2006 letter), and Berkshire trailed the S&P over much of the past decade. Munger's 1965 instruction says Graham's method "works but only when practiced at small scale": the cigar-butt and workout methods that built the Partnership (Dempster, Sanborn, Texas National) do not scale, which also means a founder or small investor copying the Berkshire-scale quality-at-a-fair-price rule may be applying the wrong regime.
- **The cash drag, from outside.** In 2019 a fund manager called the roughly $114B pile "certainly a drag" (Reuters via Daily Herald) while saying the Apple and Amazon purchases "are a good sign." The 2017 interview shows him accepting the cost: "it's selling at a 100 times earnings." The 2025 pile (about $334B to $397B) raises the same question.
- **Insurance results lean on luck, and he says so.** "To date, we have benefitted from good luck on this volatile book" (1999); "Mother Nature, bless her heart, went on vacation… Enjoy the view, because you won't soon see another like it" (2006). "Because we were lucky in our super-cat insurance business… our 1997 operating earnings were much better than we anticipated" (1997).
- **A rule he mis-specified for 26 years.** The five-year rolling retained-earnings test in the Owner's Manual (from 1983) failed whenever a five-year market stretch was negative; he corrected it only after a question at the 2009 meeting.
- **Deal-sourcing depends on networks and structures that no longer exist.** The best Berkshire deals arrived through friends (Roach, Newman, Blumkin, Robertson) and, in the 1955 Fruit of the Loom story, structures (non-interest-bearing notes, tax-loss shelters) he says belong to a past era ("Those were the days"). Founders without that network get less of the model.
- **Key-man / succession risk.** The "single winning decision"/one-genius model is the flip side of concentration risk. The handoff to Abel completed Jan 1, 2026; he writes that "My children are already 100% behind Greg as are the Berkshire directors," but the fact that he keeps A-shares "until Berkshire shareholders develop the comfort with Greg" (2025) tells you the market's worry that capital-allocation skill and culture were personal to Buffett and Munger. The 2006 letter shows the investment side was known to be "not as well-prepared" (a 2006 plan to hire a younger portfolio manager), a gap later filled by Combs and Weschler.
- **A recurring governance failure he names himself.** "a wonderful and loyal CEO… will succumb to dementia, Alzheimer's or another debilitating and long-term disease. Charlie and I encountered this problem several times and failed to act. This failure can be a huge mistake" (2025), a rare admission of a *repeated* weakness, not a one-off. The 2005 letter had already written the principle ("Someone else often needs to blow the whistle").
- **Survivorship in the Graham-and-Doddsville evidence.** The 1984 nine were pre-selected in advance by Buffett, and he warned that after-the-fact selection would defeat the argument; the nine hold small and illiquid names (Schloss's Hudson Pulp & Paper, Jeddo Highland Coal, New York Trap Rock), and the tables show overall and limited-partner columns but say little about fees or transaction costs. The orangutan test still leaves the question of how many Graham students never became superinvestors.

In this rebuild's batches the 1998, 2001, 2002 and 2003 URLs were stub pages pointing to PDFs (1998pdf.pdf, 2001pdf.pdf, 2002pdf.pdf, 2003ltr.pdf), 1999 came from final1999pdf.pdf, and 1983 required a `?x=1` suffix. - **Documented evolutions, not contradictions (dated).** Mark-to-market: pro in 2008 (Charlie Rose, "I believe in Mark to market"), against the 2018 GAAP rule routing unrealized gains through net income ("worse-than-useless," 2023). "Franchise" (1991) is the earlier vocabulary for "moat" (1993 to 2007). Deficit-doom preaching (retracted in 2018) became "fiscal policy… scares me more than monetary policy" (CNBC 2023).
