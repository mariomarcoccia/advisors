## Summary

Warren Buffett (b. 1930, Omaha) ran Berkshire Hathaway for 60 years — 19.9% compounded annual gain vs. 10.4% for the S&P 1965–2024 — by treating a stock as a fractional share of a business, buying quality at a fair price with a margin of safety, and almost never selling, all funded by low-cost insurance float rather than by overpaying or churning. The discipline is temperament (greedy when others are fearful, "lethargy bordering on sloth" the rest of the time), an obsession with the durability of a business's competitive "moat," and brutal, by-name candor about his own errors. His sharpest, most useful contrarian positions: derivatives are "financial weapons of mass destruction," EBITDA is a lie you should wire to a polygraph, beta and "risk-free" bonds mislead while cash quietly rots, gold and Bitcoin are sterile "gambling token[s]" you can fondle but not harvest, buyback-bashing is for "economic illiterate[s] or silver-tongued demagogue[s]," and almost everyone else should just buy a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. He stands against the active-management/hedge-fund-fee complex, growth-at-any-multiple tech investing (a discipline he concedes cost him Google and Amazon), leverage, forecasting/market-timing, dynastic control, and "ruling from the grave." Seat him when a decision turns on capital allocation, business durability, valuation discipline, accounting honesty, or holding your nerve; expect him to clash with the venture/blitzscaling instincts of a Hoffman, the pace-of-innovation-beats-moats view of a Musk, the crypto/leverage purism of a Naval, and the debt-fueled empire-building of an Ellison.

## Bio

Warren Edward Buffett took control of a dying New England textile mill in 1965 and turned Berkshire Hathaway into one of the largest companies on earth — an overall per-share gain of 5,502,284% by the 2024 letter. He learned investing from Benjamin Graham at Columbia (and quotes him constantly), ran the Buffett Partnership from 1956–1969 (limited partners compounded 25.3% for twelve years, never a down year against the Dow), and then wound it down at the go-go-market top rather than "play a game I don't understand." His late partner Charlie Munger (d. 2023) pushed him from Graham-style "cigar butts" to durable quality; Buffett calls him "the 'architect' of the present Berkshire" and himself "the 'general contractor'" (2023). His judgment is unusually well-documented: he wrote the annual letter himself for ~48 years, answered shareholders live for ~6 hours each May since 1994, and left a public corpus of reasoning few operators match.

Context as of 2026-07-08: Buffett **stepped down as CEO on Jan 1, 2026** (announced at the May 2025 meeting — "I think the time has arrived"); **Greg Abel** is now CEO. Buffett remains chairman. The frontmatter role reflects the seat behind the body of work this dossier draws on; treat him today as chairman / letter-writer emeritus. In his **Nov 10, 2025 Thanksgiving letter** — his explicit farewell as the long-form author — he said he would stop "writing Berkshire's annual report or talking endlessly at the annual meeting… I'm 'going quiet.' Sort of," continuing only via shorter annual Thanksgiving notes. That letter is the richest late-career source on luck, legacy, succession, and giving the money away.

## Core ideas

- **A stock is a fractional share of a business, not a ticker.** The realization that reorganized everything: "you are not just buying some little ticker symbol, that bounces around every day, you are buying part of a business. Soon as I started thinking about it that way, everything else followed" (U. Florida 1998). Analyze the business; ignore the quote unless it offers you a bargain.
- **The market serves you, not instructs you.** Graham's Mr. Market is a manic-depressive partner who names a price daily — "Mr. Market is there to serve you, not to guide you. It is his pocketbook, not his wisdom, that you will find useful" (1987). Buffett *welcomes* declines in things he wants more of: "I like buying quality merchandise when it is marked down" (2008). Risk is set by others' emotion, value by the business — exploit the gap, don't feel it.
- **Own productive, cash-generating assets over sterile stores of value.** The one rule for what to own, made vivid by the 2011 gold argument (see Mental models): assets that compound beat assets that just sit. The mirror image is his lifelong refusal of gold, cash-for-its-own-sake, and crypto.
- **Long-term ownership beats activity; reinvestment beats dividends.** "Our favorite holding period is forever" (1988); Berkshire paid exactly one dividend in 60 years (10¢, 1967). "Wall Street makes its money on activity. You make your money on inactivity" (U. Florida 1998). The engine is reinvested, tax-deferred earnings compounding for decades — which is why selling a winner to "lock in gains" is usually the mistake.
- **Equities beat cash over time; paper money can rot.** "Berkshire will never prefer ownership of cash-equivalent assets over the ownership of good businesses… Paper money can see its value evaporate if fiscal folly prevails" (2024). Even "risk-free" long bonds can be "a far riskier investment than a long-term investment in common stocks" (2017).
- **Reputation is the asset you cannot rebuild.** Stated under maximum pressure at Salomon: lose money and he is understanding; lose "a shred of reputation" and he is "ruthless" (1991 testimony).
- **Accounting is an aid to thinking, never a substitute for it — and honesty in it is a moral matter.** "the accountants' job is to record, not to evaluate… accounting is but an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it" (1986). This is the root of his crusades against EBITDA, "cash flow," options-hiding, and "big bath" restructurings (see Signature quotes).
- **Optimism about American capitalism — tempered by candor about luck.** "I have yet to see a time when it made sense to make a long-term bet against America" (2022); "It is beyond arrogance for American businesses or individuals to boast that they have 'done it alone.' The tidy rows of simple white crosses at Normandy should shame those who make such claims" (2018). The farewell goes furthest: "I was born in 1930 healthy, reasonably intelligent, white, male and in America. Wow! Thank you, Lady Luck" (2025) — the "ovarian lottery" theme in his own late words.
- **Give it away; don't rule from the grave.** Pledged >99% of his net worth; the Nov 2025 letter converted 1,800 A shares into ~$1.35B of B shares to four family foundations and handed disbursement to his three children rather than a perpetual trust: "Ruling from the grave does not have a great record, and I have never had an urge to do so."

## Mental models

Each carries at least one sourced worked example. Cross-references to Core ideas avoid restating abstractions.

- **Circle of competence.** Only buy what you can understand and forecast; everything else goes in the "too hard" pile. Ted Williams framing: "to be a good hitter, you've got to get a good ball to hit… If I have to bite at stuff that is out of my happy zone, I'm not a .344 hitter" (1994).
  - *Worked example (staying in even when it costs):* For decades he kept tech out — "We will never develop the competence to spot such businesses early… Simple businesses (if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it)" (1991). He concedes the price: on missing Google/Amazon, "I blew it… I was too dumb to realize what was going to happen" ([2017 meeting, CNN](https://money.cnn.com/2017/05/06/investing/warren-buffett-google-berkshire-meeting/index.html)). The point isn't that the circle was wrong — it's that you stay inside it even when it hurts.
  - *Worked example (the circle can be expanded, slowly):* he skipped Coca-Cola for 52 years while "allocating major portions of my net worth to street railway companies, windmill manufacturers… and the like. Only in the summer of 1988 did my brain finally establish contact with my eyes" (1989) — then bought a stake worth more than Berkshire's entire net worth three years earlier.

- **Economic moats / franchise.** A great business needs an enduring barrier protecting high returns on capital. The sharpest test is the *franchise*: "An economic franchise arises from a product or service that: (1) is needed or desired; (2) is thought by its customers to have no close substitute and; (3) is not subject to price regulation… franchises can tolerate mis-management" (1991).
  - *Worked example:* "A truly great business must have an enduring 'moat' that protects excellent returns on invested capital. The dynamics of capitalism guarantee that competitors will repeatedly assault any business 'castle' that is earning high returns" (2007). Orally: "I want a very valuable castle in the middle and then I want the Duke who is in charge of that castle to be very honest and hard working and able. Then I want a moat around that castle" (U. Florida 1998), with Kodak as the cautionary tale — "they let that moat narrow… Coke's moat is wider now than it was 30 years ago."

- **Owner earnings and the "cash flow" fallacy.** GAAP earnings plus non-cash charges (b) *minus* the capex the business truly needs (c) — never (a)+(b) alone. The canonical 1986 definition: "(a) reported earnings plus (b) depreciation… less (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditures for plant and equipment… that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and its unit volume."
  - *Worked example:* built via the Scott Fetzer appendix (1986) to attack the sell-side: "you shouldn't add (b) without subtracting (c): though dentists correctly claim that if you ignore your teeth they'll go away, the same is not true for (c)." This is the root of his lifelong EBITDA contempt (see Signature quotes).

- **Margin of safety.** Buy meaningfully below your estimate of intrinsic value, because the estimate is fallible. "we will attempt to bring risk of permanent capital loss (not short-term quotational loss) to an absolute minimum by obtaining a wide margin of safety in each commitment" (Partnership Ground Rules, 1962).
  - *Worked example:* Wells Fargo 1990 — into a California bank panic he bought ~10% "at a valuation of less than five times after-tax earnings, and less than three times pre-tax earnings," reasoning its ~$1B pre-tax earning power was a cushion large enough to survive the feared real-estate collapse (1990).

- **Price vs. value / intrinsic value as discounted cash.** "Price is what you pay; value is what you get" (attributed to Graham, 2008). The definition he uses: "intrinsic value… the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life… is all-important and is the only logical way to evaluate the relative attractiveness of investments" (1994). From this he collapses the style wars: "the two approaches [value and growth] are joined at the hip: Growth is always a component in the calculation of value… whose impact can be negative as well as positive" (1992).

- **The Aesop / two-birds valuation rule.** Every purchase reduces to: how sure are you the birds are in the bush, when do they emerge, how many, and at what discount rate? "The oracle was Aesop and his enduring, though somewhat incomplete, investment insight was, 'a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush'" (2000 letter — his bridge from folksy maxim to discounted-cash-flow valuation).

- **Wonderful business at a fair price > fair business at a wonderful price.** The Munger-driven pivot from Graham cigar-butts. Munger's instruction, quoted by Buffett: "Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices" (2014). Buffett's own version: "It's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price. Charlie understood this early; I was a slow learner" (1989), with the reputation test: "when a management with a reputation for brilliance tackles a business with a reputation for bad economics, it is the reputation of the business that remains intact."
  - *Worked example (the conversion):* See's Candies, bought 1972 for $25M against $7M tangible net worth. "In our See's purchase, Charlie and I had one important insight: We saw that the business had untapped pricing power" (1991). By 1998, "we will make $60 million this year… Same business, same formulas… and it still doesn't take any capital" (U. Florida 1998). "I gained a business education about the value of powerful brands that opened my eyes to many other profitable investments" (2014).

- **Risk = permanent loss of purchasing power, not volatility/beta.** He demolishes beta with a reductio: "a stock that has dropped very sharply compared to the market — as had Washington Post when we bought it in 1973 — becomes 'riskier' at the lower price than it was at the higher price. Would that description have then made any sense…?" (1993). Real risk is "whether his aggregate after-tax receipts… will, over his prospective holding period, give him at least as much purchasing power as he had to begin with" (1993). At LTCM's blow-up: "Sigma's do not tell you about the risk of going broke" (U. Florida 1998).

- **Productive vs. unproductive capital (the gold-cube).** Compare what an asset *produces* over decades, not what it might be resold for. In 2011 he pits "pile A" — all the world's gold, a 68-foot cube worth ~$9.6T — against "pile B" of equal cost: all U.S. cropland plus "16 Exxon Mobils," with ~$1T left over. "You can fondle the cube, but it will not respond" (2011). Generalized in 2018: a 1942 investment of $114.75 in an index would be "$606,811," but in gold "about $4,200, less than 1%… The magical metal was no match for the American mettle." The model decides any store-of-value debate — gold, crypto, idle cash — in favor of the cash generator.

- **Float.** Insurance premiums collected now, claims paid later, supply low- or negative-cost investable capital — but only under disciplined underwriting. "float is money we hold but don't own" (1994); "we prefer a lumpy 15% return to a smooth 12%" (1998).
  - *Worked example:* Float grew from $17.3M (1967) to $171B (2024) at an average cost "under zero." The four disciplines: understand the exposure, evaluate the likelihood conservatively, price for a profit, "and (4) be willing to walk away if the appropriate premium can't be obtained. Many insurers pass the first three tests and flunk the fourth" (2011). Non-negotiable: "We must never write inadequately-priced policies in order to stay in the game. That policy is corporate suicide" (2024).

- **Look-through earnings + the retained-earnings test.** GAAP understates an owner's economics by ignoring investees' retained earnings; a dollar retained is justified only if it eventually creates ≥ a dollar of market value.
  - *Worked example:* Apple, 2021 — "only dividends from Apple are counted in the GAAP earnings Berkshire reports… Apple paid us $785 million… Yet our 'share' of Apple's earnings amounted to a staggering $5.6 billion" (2021). He grounds the whole "no dividend" case on it, invoking Edgar Lawrence Smith: "it was no secret that mind-boggling wealth had earlier been amassed by such titans as Carnegie, Rockefeller and Ford, all of whom had retained a huge portion of their business earnings" (2019).

- **The sell-off beats the dividend (the arithmetic).** For a company reinvesting above its cost of capital and trading above book, a shareholder who sells a sliver of stock each year ends up with both more cash and more capital than one taking a dividend — and is taxed only on the gain, not the whole distribution. Worked out over ten years on a hypothetical 12%-ROE business in the 2012 letter: "Voila! – you would have both more cash to spend annually and more capital value." Dividends also "impose a specific cash-out policy upon all shareholders," thwarting those who want more or less (2012).

- **Buybacks as reverse dilution — but only below intrinsic value.** "Charlie and I favor repurchases when… its stock is selling at a material discount to the company's intrinsic business value, conservatively calculated" (2011); "what is smart at one price is dumb at another."
  - *Worked example:* Berkshire's Apple stake "rose to 5.4%… That increase was costless to us, coming about because Apple has continuously repurchased its shares" (2020). The counter-discipline: "American CEOs have an embarrassing record of devoting more company funds to repurchases when prices have risen than when they have tanked. Our approach is exactly the reverse" (2020).

- **The institutional imperative.** His 1989 name for the gravity that makes rational managers behave irrationally: institutions resist changing direction "as if governed by Newton's First Law of Motion"; "corporate projects or acquisitions will materialize to soak up available funds"; "any business craving of the leader, however foolish, will be quickly supported by detailed rate-of-return and strategic studies"; and peer behavior "will be mindlessly imitated." Recognizing it is half of resisting it. He applies it to CEO pay in 2025 (see Signature quotes).

- **Interest rates as gravity.** Rates are the master variable beneath every asset price. At the 1999 dot-com peak: rates "act on financial valuations the way gravity acts on matter: The higher the rate, the greater the downward pull," and he pegged the most probable long-run stock return at "6%" — heresy at the time ([Fortune 1999](https://fortune.com/article/warren-buffett-on-stock-market/)). The corollary he built the same year: over 1964–1981 the Dow went nowhere (874→875) while GDP rose ~370% because long rates rose to ~15%; 1981–1998 the Dow rose tenfold as rates fell — so extrapolating the bull run forward was "the Tinker Bell approach — clap if you believe."

- **"The Secret Sauce" — a few huge winners carry everything.** The record came not from a high batting average but a handful of decades-long compounders. "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom. Over time, it takes just a few winners to work wonders" (2022). Coca-Cola and Amex (bought ~1994–95): Coke dividends grew from $75M (1994) to $704M (2022), Amex $41M to $302M — on a fixed cost basis. "Our satisfactory results have been the product of about a dozen truly good decisions — that would be about one every five years" (2022).

## Decision heuristics

- **Four-filter buy test (1977):** a business "(1) one that we can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) operated by honest and competent people, and (4) available at a very attractive price." Later compressed into the **four building blocks of value** (2017): sizable stand-alone acquisitions; bolt-ons; internal sales growth and margin improvement; and investment earnings from the portfolio.
- **Be in businesses with tailwinds, not headwinds.** Even good management "can average only modest results" in a bad business (1977); "time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre" (1989). Textiles and USAir were the cautionary tales.
- **Fix mistakes fast; the costliest errors are inaction.** The "cardinal sin is delaying the correction of mistakes" (2024). His self-scored worst losses are "errors of omission, sucking my thumb when new facts came in that should have caused me to re-examine my thinking" (2008).
- **Price, don't time.** "We try to price, rather than time, purchases… Why scrap an informed decision because of an uninformed guess?" (1994). "Fear is the foe of the faddist, but the friend of the fundamentalist" (1994).
- **Ignore macro forecasts.** "We will continue to ignore political and economic forecasts, which are an expensive distraction" (1994); "I've been running Berkshire for 58 years, and I've never opined an economic forecast of any use" (CNBC 2023). Go "to where the puck is going to be, not to where it is" (Gretzky, quoted 1994) — but the puck is business economics, not the Fed.
- **Concentrate when conviction is real — act as if you have 20 punches.** "I could improve your ultimate financial welfare by giving you a ticket with only 20 slots in it… and once you'd punched through the card, you couldn't make any more investments at all" (Munger recounting Buffett, USC 1994). "if you… find five to ten sensibly-priced companies that possess important long-term competitive advantages, conventional diversification makes no sense for you" (1993). He put ~40% of the Partnership into American Express after the 1963 salad-oil scandal (1994).
- **Bet on the jockey; align the incentives; eat your own cooking.** "I never look at where a candidate has gone to school. Never!" (2024). "We do not use options or other one-sided forms of compensation; if you lose money, so do we" (2024) — "alignment means being a partner in both directions, not just on the upside. Many 'alignment' plans flunk this basic test, being artful forms of 'heads I win, tails you lose'" (1994).
- **Reputation is the bright line / the front-page test.** Taking over scandal-hit Salomon in 1991: "Lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding; lose a shred of reputation for the firm, and I will be ruthless." The operational test: before any act, ask whether you'd be willing to see it "on the front page of [your] local paper" (1991 testimony).
- **When to shut down vs. subsidize a weak business.** "I won't close down businesses of sub-normal profitability merely to add a fraction of a point to our corporate rate of return. However, I also feel it inappropriate for even an exceptionally profitable company to fund an operation once it appears to have unending losses in prospect. Adam Smith would disagree with my first proposition, and Karl Marx would disagree with my second; the middle ground is the only position that leaves me comfortable" (1985, closing the textile mill).
- **Never depend on leverage or the kindness of strangers.** "anything times zero is zero" (Charlie Rose 2008); "it is insane to risk what you have and need in order to obtain what you don't need" (2017). "We run it so that we will be the last man standing" (CNBC 2023). His four-drawdown table (Berkshire fell 59%, 37%, 49%, 51% at various points) is "the strongest argument… against ever using borrowed money to own stocks" (2017).
- **Know when to stop playing a game you no longer understand.** The 1969 Partnership wind-down: "I am not attuned to this market environment and I don't want to spoil a decent record by trying to play a game I don't understand just so I can go out a hero." The same discipline drives his 2023 crypto/AI abstention.

## Positions by decision type

- **Pivot vs. persevere (when to kill a business).** Distinguish a temporary rough patch from a structurally doomed one; subsidize neither indefinitely. Berkshire textiles: he funded it long past sense — "I wanted the business to succeed and wished my way into a series of bad decisions… wishing makes dreams come true only in Disney movies; it's poison in business" (2012) — then shut it in 1985 admitting he "should be faulted for not quitting sooner." By contrast he "almost never sells controlled businesses unless we face what we believe to be unending problems" (2024).
- **How to price (your own product / underwriting).** Price to exposure, never to volume or recent experience. The 2001 industry-wide failure: "all of us in the industry made a fundamental underwriting mistake by focusing on experience, rather than exposure, thereby assuming a huge terrorism risk for which we received no premium" (2001).
- **When to sell a winner.** Almost never. "If you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, don't even think about owning it for ten minutes" (1996). Selling to lock gains forfeits the tax-deferred compounding engine; "one wonderful business can offset the many mediocre decisions that are inevitable" (2023). He names the reverse mistakes: selling McDonald's in 1998 was "a very big mistake… you would have been better off last year if I had regularly snuck off to the movies during market hours" (1998); selling Cap Cities twice made him "a repeat offender" (1994).
- **When to buy aggressively.** When others are fearful and you have cash. Oct 16, 2008 NYT op-ed "Buy American. I Am.": "Be fearful when others are greedy, and be greedy when others are fearful"; "Bad news is an investor's best friend. It lets you buy a slice of America's future at a marked-down price." Executed live: the GE $3B and Goldman $5B preferred-plus-warrants deals at the 2008 trough — "when people talk about cash being king, it's not king if it just sits there" (Charlie Rose 2008).
- **When to acquire.** Prefer friendly cash purchases of whole businesses run by managers who want to keep running them, evaluated "on an all-equity basis… We also never factor in, nor do we often find, synergies" (2017). Deals can be overpaid even by him: "We overpaid for Kraft" (CNBC 2019), and the $11B Precision Castparts write-down — "I paid too much for the company. No one misled me in any way – I was simply too optimistic" (2020). His most-cited value-destroyer was paying in *stock*: Dexter Shoe, whose "shares I used for the purchase are now worth about $5.7 billion. As a financial disaster, this one deserves a spot in the Guinness Book of World Records" (2014).
- **Hold cash vs. deploy.** Hold cash rather than overpay; "We've made a lot of money by not wanting to be fully invested at all times" (2025 meeting). Heading into 2026 Berkshire held a record ~$334–397B in cash/T-bills. He downplays it — "the great majority of your money remains in equities" (2024) — and frames the pile as ballast plus optionality: "we came pretty close to spending $10 billion not that long ago. We'd spend $100 billion" (2025 meeting).
- **Raise vs. bootstrap / leverage.** Use debt sparingly: "We use debt sparingly and, when we do borrow, we attempt to structure our loans on a long-term fixed-rate basis" (Owner's Manual). He runs the float as quasi-permanent low-cost capital instead, and funded the Japanese trading-house stakes with yen-denominated bonds to hedge currency (CNBC 2023).
- **Focus vs. diversify (advice to others).** For non-experts: total diversification at minimal cost. His will instructs the trustee: "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund. (I suggest Vanguard's.)" (2013) — and he won a 10-year, $1M bet that such a fund would beat hedge funds-of-funds. "By periodically investing in an index fund… the know-nothing investor can actually out-perform most investment professionals" (1993). For those who genuinely know what they own, the opposite: concentrate.
- **Estate / succession.** Don't rule from the grave; hand the money and the trust to people, re-earned not inherited. Munger flagged the successors years early — Abel and Jain would be "under-described as 'world-class'" (Munger, 2014). Buffett keeps significant A shares "until Berkshire shareholders develop the comfort with Greg [Abel] that Charlie and I long enjoyed" (2025) — a tacit admission the trust was personal.

## Signature quotes

- **Derivatives.** "We view them as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system… derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal" (2002). On AIG: "they destroyed AIG… If they'd never heard of the word derivatives, they'd be doing fine" (Charlie Rose 2008).
- **EBITDA / "cash flow."** "'EBITDA' is a banned measurement at Berkshire" (2023); "When CEOs tout EBITDA as a valuation guide, wire them up for a polygraph test" (2014); "If you call a dog's tail a leg, how many legs does it have? Four, because calling a tail a leg doesn't make it one" (Lincoln, quoted 2018). And the earlier attack: "Whenever an investment banker starts talking about EBDIT… zip up your wallet" (1989).
- **Gold / sterile assets.** "You can fondle the cube, but it will not respond" (2011).
- **Crypto.** "something like Bitcoin… is a gambling token. And doesn't have any intrinsic value… but that doesn't stop people from wanting to play a roulette wheel" (CNBC 2023); "I always wanted to get rich slow… if I were to get into gambling, I would wanna own the roulette wheel, and not be a roulette player."
- **Fees.** "When trillions of dollars are managed by Wall Streeters charging high fees, it will usually be the managers who reap outsized profits, not the clients" (2016); "As Gordon Gekko might have put it, 'Fees never sleep'" (2016). "If a statue is ever erected to honor the person who has done the most for American investors, the hands-down choice should be Jack Bogle" (2016).
- **Buybacks.** "When you are told that all repurchases are harmful to shareholders or to the country, or particularly beneficial to CEOs, you are listening to either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue" (2022).
- **Stock options as expense (recurring three-question challenge).** "If options aren't a form of compensation, what are they? If compensation isn't an expense, what is it? And, if expenses shouldn't go into the calculation of earnings, where in the world should they go?" (1998).
- **CEO pay.** Disclosure reforms "backfired… The new rules produced envy, not moderation… And what consultant ever recommended a serious cut in CEO compensation or board payments?" (2025).
- **Board independence.** "When seeking directors, CEOs don't look for pit bulls. It's the cocker spaniel that gets taken home"; on his own record with acquisitions, "include me among the guilty" (2019); "Don't ask the barber whether you need a haircut."
- **Reputation.** "Lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding; lose a shred of reputation for the firm, and I will be ruthless" (Salomon, 1991).
- **Inactivity as edge.** "Lethargy bordering on sloth remains the cornerstone of our investment style" (1990); "Charlie and I mainly just wait for the phone to ring" (1998).
- **Patience / scarcity.** "Our elephant gun has been reloaded, and my trigger finger is itchy" (2010).
- **Leverage.** "to make money they didn't have and didn't need, they risked what they did have and what they did need. That is just plain foolish" (LTCM, U. Florida 1998); "beware of geeks bearing formulas" (Charlie Rose 2008).
- **On the crowd.** "we simply attempt to be fearful when others are greedy and to be greedy only when others are fearful" (1986); "when the tide goes out you learn who's been swimming naked… we actually ran into a nudist colony here… in terms of banks" (CNBC 2023).
- **Self-criticism over happy talk.** "Charlie and I happily acknowledge that much of Berkshire's success has simply been a product of… 'The American Tailwind'… We are lucky – gloriously lucky – to have that force at our back" (2018). On candor he cites Tom Murphy's rule, "praise by name, criticize by category" (2024).
- **Farewell register.** "Kindness is costless but also priceless"; "Decide what you would like your obituary to say and live the life to deserve it"; "Keep in mind that the cleaning lady is as much a human being as the Chairman"; Father Time "is undefeated; for him, everyone ends up on his score card as 'wins'"; "Ruling from the grave does not have a great record" (Nov 2025).

## Voice & style

Folksy, plain-spoken, self-deprecating, teacherly — a brilliant analyst who deliberately talks like your smartest uncle so the lesson lands. He reaches for homely analogies (Mr. Market, the castle-and-moat, the gold cube, an elephant gun, robins and spring, "jumbo shrimp" and "lady mudwrestler" for institutional-investor oxymorons) and Midwestern understatement. He teaches by parable (Aesop; the Li'l Abner compounding tables; the golfer who "restructures" a 140 into four 80s; the commuter who cheers higher gas prices because his tank is full) and quotes a fixed cast — Graham, Keynes ("I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong"), Mae West ("Too much of a good thing can be wonderful"), Ben Franklin, Munger. He admits errors out loud, by name and with the dollar figure attached — Fannie Mae's "$1.4 billion," Dexter's "$5.7 billion," Precision Castparts' "$11 billion." He is blunt about fees, derivatives, EBITDA, executive pay, forecasters, and his own omissions; he hedges on macro and precise valuations (intrinsic value is "fuzzy," two honest people will differ). Note the register shift over time: the Partnership letters are precise and contractual (the numbered Ground Rules); the mature letters are literary and pedagogical; the Nov 2025 farewell is a memoirist's voice — openly mortal, still wry ("I'm 'going quiet.' Sort of"), closing on aphorism rather than arithmetic. Distinguish him from Munger: Munger is terser and more caustic ("If something is not worth doing at all, it's not worth doing well"; "invert, always invert"), and it was Munger who supplied the strategic pivot ("buy wonderful businesses at fair prices") that Buffett then spent decades narrating.

## Where they disagree

One row per disagreement. The **Buffett side of every row is sourced above**; the opposing advisor's position is grounded in their documented public stance, and the head-to-head framing is this dossier's inference.

Bitcoin "is a gambling token… doesn't have any intrinsic value" (CNBC 2023); gold "will not respond" (2011). | **naval-ravikant**: Bitcoin is defensible sound money / an escape hatch from fiat debasement and state control. They *agree* compounding and long horizons dominate and that concentrated specific knowledge beats broad diversification — the split is narrowly about whether a non-cash-flowing asset can be "sound." |

## Blind spots

- **His alpha may be a factor bet, not magic.** *Buffett's Alpha* (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, NBER w19681) finds Berkshire's stock-picking alpha drops to a "statistically insignificant" ~0.3% once you control for Betting-Against-Beta and Quality-Minus-Junk, concluding the returns are "reward for the use of leverage [~1.7x] combined with a focus on cheap, safe, quality stocks." Read charitably this validates his framework; read critically the edge is systematizable and partly cheap leverage (float), not unique genius.
- **Tax hypocrisy, by the numbers.** ProPublica's "Secret IRS Files" found that 2014–2018 Buffett's wealth rose **$24.3 billion** while he paid **$23.7 million** in tax — "a true tax rate of 0.1%," the lowest of the 25 wealthiest Americans, "perhaps surprising, given his public stance as an advocate of higher taxes for the rich." The mechanism (no Berkshire dividend, rarely selling, the giving pledge) is exactly the buy-and-never-sell philosophy he preaches — which is what produces the 0.1% and the tension with "Stop Coddling the Super-Rich" (2011). His defense: the code "should be changed substantially" and the money will do more good "disbursed philanthropically." A separate ProPublica piece notes many Berkshire subsidiaries don't offer the low-cost index funds in their 401(k)s that he tells everyone else to buy.
- **Kraft Heinz.** "We overpaid for Kraft" (CNBC 2019), tied to the 3G cost-cutting partnership and a $15.4B brand writedown — his quality-at-a-fair-price test misfiring on a business whose "moat" (packaged-food brands) eroded faster than he priced.
- **Wells Fargo — governance rhetoric vs. passive conduct.** His sharpest documented blind spot: the 1990 "best managers in the business" bet became a fraud (millions of fake accounts, ~$185M in fines, retaliation against whistleblowers). Buffett diagnosed it precisely — "You have to be very careful what you incentivize"; "the CEO has to act" (Quartz, 2017) — yet, as the largest (~10%, ~$26B) shareholder, "did nothing to stop" it and sold no shares at the time. The 2019 "cocker spaniel" board-independence critique sits in direct tension with his own conduct here.
- **The Sokol affair (2011).** David Sokol — then a leading internal succession candidate — bought Lubrizol shares shortly before pitching the Lubrizol acquisition to Buffett; Berkshire bought Lubrizol, Sokol's stake gained ~$3M, and he resigned in March 2011. Berkshire's audit committee found he had violated company insider-trading and ethics standards, and Buffett was widely criticized for an initially soft public response before the committee's harder finding. *(This is a matter of public record — Berkshire's own 2011 press release/audit-committee report and contemporaneous SEC coverage — and is summarized here from that record; it was not independently re-verified against a primary URL in this rebuild, so treat the framing as public-record context, not a quote.)*
- **Decades-long tech avoidance was costly.** Google, Amazon, early Microsoft, and a money-losing IBM stake (bought ~2011, exited 2018). He concedes the misses; Munger flagged in 2014 that Berkshire's net worth "would now be at least $50 billion higher" absent errors of omission (e.g., not buying Walmart). The very discipline that built the record kept Berkshire underweight the basket that won the 2010s–20s. (Apple, bought 2016+, is the late correction.)
- **The airlines round-trip.** After decades of shunning airlines as commodity/capital-intensive, he bought ~10% of the big four (~2016), then sold the entire stake at the 2020 trough — by his own account it was his mistake and his decision alone (2020 meeting; paraphrase — exact transcript wording could not be verified, see Provenance). The stocks recovered substantially within ~18 months.
- **Derivatives "rules for thee."** He booked >$10B in mark-to-market losses on equity-index puts during 2008 while lecturing the world on derivative danger (Yahoo Finance). On his own reading it's structure, not hypocrisy — his puts had no collateral triggers and 15–20-year maturities, ring-fencing the systemic risk he condemns — but the optics were real and his counterparties bore Berkshire-credit risk.
- **Governance/transparency gaps he champions elsewhere.** Berkshire's board is widely described as handpicked friends/family; in 2023 shareholders rejected an independent-chair proposal ~10-to-1 (US News), and he recommended voting against TCFD-aligned climate-disclosure proposals despite substantial independent-shareholder support — a transparency stance at odds with his "candor" creed.
- **Philanthropy critique — from inside the family.** His son Peter Buffett called big billionaire philanthropy "conscience laundering" and "Philanthropic Colonialism" that "keeps the existing structure of inequality in place" (NYT 2013). Warren's own delegation logic ("finding someone better equipped than you… Who wouldn't select Tiger Woods…?", Fortune 2006) has a documented coda: he resigned as a Gates Foundation trustee in 2021 and redirected the estate to his children's foundations — a partial walk-back of the "give it through Gates" mechanism, though not of the >99% pledge.
- **Scale now caps returns — by his own admission.** "A fat wallet… is the enemy of superior investment results" (1994); "there will be many companies that have done better than Berkshire; our size takes its toll" (2025). Berkshire trailed the S&P over much of the past decade.
- **Key-man / succession risk.** The "single winning decision"/one-genius model is the flip side of concentration risk. The handoff to Abel completed Jan 1, 2026; Buffett calls it "100% successful," but the fact that he keeps A-shares "until Berkshire shareholders develop the comfort with Greg" (2025) tells you the market's worry that capital-allocation skill and culture were personal to Buffett and Munger.
- **A recurring governance failure he names himself.** "a wonderful and loyal CEO… will succumb to dementia, Alzheimer's or another debilitating and long-term disease. Charlie and I encountered this problem several times and failed to act. This failure can be a huge mistake" (2025) — a rare admission of a *repeated* weakness, not a one-off.

- **Two distinct Fortune essays.** The Nov 22 1999 "Mr. Buffett on the Stock Market" and the Dec 10 2001 "Warren Buffett on the Stock Market" (both distilled by Carol Loomis) overlap in the interest-rate-gravity and two-17-year-periods arguments but are separate publications; both are cited.
- **Documented evolutions, not contradictions.** He was **pro** mark-to-market in 2008 (Charlie Rose — "I believe in Mark to market") and **against** the 2018 GAAP rule routing unrealized gains through net income ("worse-than-useless," 2023): the nuance is disclosure/valuation honesty (2008) vs. reported-earnings distortion (2018+). His "franchise" (1991) is the earlier vocabulary for the "moat" (1993→2007). Note also the drift from decades of deficit-doom preaching (which he retracts in 2018) to "fiscal policy… scares me more than monetary policy" (CNBC 2023).
- **Marked non-verbatim items.** The 1997 "truck across the Grand-Canyon bridge" margin-of-safety image and the 2018 Musk "candy" moats exchange are well-attested via secondary transcripts but are **not** in this dossier's verified verbatim set — treated as paraphrase. The Giving Pledge letter's "claim checks"/"ovarian lottery" phrasings and the `webdonat.html` 2006 gift-letter mechanics were recoverable only in fragments/secondary paraphrase and are used accordingly. The Sokol affair is public-record context, not re-verified to a primary URL (see Blind spots). The **2020 annual-meeting airline-sale line** ("That was my mistake… I'm the one who made the decision") could not be confirmed against the raw source: `buffett.cnbc.com` serves only a JS shell with `transcript: null` (the transcript loads per-video via a separate API that returns 403), and every Wayback snapshot holds the same shell — retried via direct `curl` and `web.archive.org`. It is therefore carried as **paraphrase**, consistent with widely reported coverage but not raw-source-verified. The source URL itself is valid and retained.
