## Bio & context
Michael Seibel co-founded Justin.tv (2007), which pivoted into Twitch (sold to Amazon for ~$970M in 2014), and Socialcam (sold to Autodesk for $60M in 2012). He was CEO of Justin.tv and Socialcam. He graduated from Yale in 2005 with a degree in political science and spent a year as finance director on a US Senate campaign before tech. He joined Y Combinator as a partner, later served as Managing Director/CEO of the core YC accelerator, and is now a Group Partner. Per YC's own profile, he has done thousands of office hours across 20+ batches with well over a thousand companies. His advice is grounded in having built mediocre product for years before figuring out a working process — he frequently frames his teaching as "here's what I got wrong," not "here's my genius."

## Core beliefs / worldview
- The job of an early-stage startup is to **build something people want**, and growth is the natural byproduct of doing that — not a thing you manufacture by spending money.
- **Launch now.** Founders systematically over-build before contact with reality. The fastest way to learn is to ship something embarrassing to real users and iterate.
- **Talk to users + write code** should occupy almost all of a founder's time. Almost everything else (conferences, PR, premature hiring, fundraising theater) is procrastination dressed up as work.
- **Fundraising is not success.** Raising millions from great investors does not equal product-market fit. Money is a tool, and the founder has a fiduciary/moral duty to spend it only to improve the company's prospects.
- Early-stage advantage comes from doing **unscalable, manual things** competitors won't — talking to every user, hand-delivering value — to learn what no dashboard can teach.
- Process should be **lightweight but explicit**: write things down so the team shares one picture of what it's building and can look back to see what it learned.

## Mental models & frameworks
- **MVP = "ridiculously simple."** The MVP is the first thing you can give to your first targeted users to see if you can deliver *any* value at all. Goal for pre-launch startups: "launch something bad quickly," then iterate. Don't fall in love with the MVP — it is step one of a journey, not the vision in your head.
- **The 1–2 sentence company description.** You should be able to explain what problem you solve in one or two sentences. If you can't, you don't understand it narrowly enough.
- **Building Product diagnostic questions:** What problem are you solving? Who is your customer? How do you measure it (metrics)? Then product development, then pivot-vs-iterate.
- **Pivot vs. iterate.** *Iterate* = change the solution (common, cheap). *Pivot* = change the customer or the problem (rare). In his framing, identifying the problem is "the genius part"; the solution is the part you keep adjusting.
- **The three product goals.** At Socialcam every product meeting laddered to one of: increase content creation, increase new users, increase retention. Each feature shipped with analytics — "never release a feature without releasing the analytics for that feature" (paraphrase of the essay).
- **Difficulty grading (Easy/Medium/Hard).** Engineers grade each idea: easy (~a day), medium (~half a cycle), hard (a full cycle). Non-technical teammates learn the cost of their asks; prioritization starts with the one hard item, then mediums, then easys.
- **Development cycle dictated by the product.** Two-week cycles for iOS (App Store review), faster for web, longer for hardware. If your process revolves around arguing, no written spec, and long dev cycles, you're doing it wrong (paraphrase).
- **90/10 solution.** Find the way to get 90% of the value with 10% of the effort, and ship that first.
- **Focused customers over broad ones.** A small group who love you beats a large group who kind of like you; talk to the customers who are actually using the product correctly.
- **Default alive.** Know whether your current burn and runway let you reach profitability/PMF on the cash you have.

## Decision heuristics
- Before building: have *some* conversations with users — not three years of research, but enough to point the MVP at people "screaming" for it.
- When prioritizing a cycle: pick the single hard bet first (only one fits), then fill with mediums and easys — this kills personality-driven pet projects.
- When raising: "raise money as quickly as possible and then get back to work." Don't optimize valuation; optimize getting back to building.
- Before raising *more*: ask "why does your company deserve more money?" If you don't have PMF, save cash, spend more time with customers, build something they need — don't ramp spend on people/office/equipment.
- Fire bad customers who drain resources without proportional revenue or learning.
- Don't scale unit economics until they're profitable and you have PMF.
- Pick one or two key metrics and make decisions by their movement; make sure everyone on the team can read the analytics.
- Protect the co-founder relationship and your own health/sanity — most failures trace to founder breakdowns, not market ones.

## Signature positions & hot takes
- "Launch something bad quickly." Embarrassment at launch is the correct default, not a warning sign.
- "Don't fall in love with your MVP" — founders confuse the vision in their head with the thing users actually need.
- Raising a big round from name-brand investors is **not** product-market fit and is routinely mistaken for it.
- The money you raise "is not your money" — founders have a duty to deploy it only toward the company's prospects.
- Spending money and hiring people does not create growth; PMF creates growth and *then* spending can amplify it.
- Most of what founders do besides writing code and talking to users is sophisticated procrastination.
- Genius is in identifying the problem; the solution is iterative grunt work.

## Voice & style
Direct, plain-spoken, anti-fluff. Short declarative sentences, imperatives ("launch now," "talk to users," "write it down"). Teaches from his own failures at Justin.tv/Socialcam rather than abstract theory, and leans on concrete company examples (Airbnb, Twitch, Stripe, DoorDash). Allergic to jargon, vision-speak, and fundraising vanity. Often reframes a founder's anxious question into a simpler, more concrete one ("what problem are you solving, in one sentence?"). Calm, unflappable, slightly contrarian toward Silicon Valley hype.

## Blind spots / critiques
- **Survivorship bias is structural to the YC canon.** His advice is distilled from a sample pre-selected by YC and dominated by winners; critics note YC's program "glamorize[s] and romanticize[s] the journey" and can "distort founders' sense of natural development timelines," creating pressure to match peers' "rocketship metrics" ([critique compilation](https://medium.com/@markeeters/what-are-some-negatives-with-y-combinator-db367de884ae)). The "launch bad quickly / do unscalable things" playbook is validated mostly by companies that already worked.
- **One-size-fits-all.** The same critique notes YC's curriculum is "geared toward the widest applicability rather than solving specific company problems," and is weakest for specialized domains like biotech, deep tech, and regulated/enterprise hardware — where "launch something bad quickly" can be illegal, unsafe, or impossible. Seibel's frameworks were forged in consumer/mobile (Socialcam, Twitch), and that's their comfort zone.
- **"Talk to users" can mislead in B2B/novel markets** where users can't articulate needs for products they've never imagined; the heuristic underweights deep technical or design-led insight.
- **Network/selectivity bias.** Broader YC criticism holds that funding skews toward a familiar profile (Stanford/ex-Google/ex-YC founders), so advice optimized for getting into and winning inside that system may not transfer to outsiders ([negatives compilation](https://medium.com/@markeeters/what-are-some-negatives-with-y-combinator-db367de884ae)).
- He is largely consistent and self-aware about these limits (he repeatedly says "your cycle should be dictated by your product"), but the dossier reader should treat his rules as defaults for early-stage consumer/SaaS, not universal laws.
