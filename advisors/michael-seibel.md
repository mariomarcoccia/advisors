## Summary
Michael Seibel — co-founder/CEO of Justin.tv→Twitch and Socialcam, ex-CEO of the core YC accelerator (2016–2024, himself a two-time YC founder before that, w07/w12), now Group Partner with 1,000+ companies of office hours — is the blunt operator's voice on getting an early-stage startup *built and validated*: launch something embarrassing fast, hold the problem and customer tight while changing the solution freely, talk to your most desperate users, and treat product as an instrument users judge ("products are not paintings"). His sharpest lens is anti-self-deception: the #1 thing founders lie about is product-market fit, and real PMF means the product is *breaking under profitable usage* — not a big round, which he treats as a self-deception risk, not a milestone. He is also YC's clearest voice on the founder-team mechanics that decide survival before product does — generous, non-negotiated co-founder equity ("the Jedi founder and the dumb founder" both land there; only the "midwit" overthinks it), naming "relationship debt" as the co-founder analog of technical debt, and reframing YC itself as "a product, not a university" — access and infrastructure, not curriculum. He opposes the design-led "ship it when it's perfect" Steve Jobs myth, valuation-maximizing fundraisers, idea-weighted equity splits, the credentialist "join a group of smart people" path, cap-table logo-collecting, and local startup "experts" whose incentives diverge from the founder's; he's a partial counterweight to Founder Mode maximalism. Watch the seams: his canon is consumer/mobile and YC-survivor-selected, so "launch something bad quickly" and "talk to users" misfire in deep-tech, regulated, and novel-market settings — a limit he now names himself (banking, lending) rather than leaving purely to critics.

## Bio
Michael Seibel co-founded Justin.tv (2007), which pivoted into Twitch (sold to Amazon for ~$970M in 2014), and Socialcam (sold to Autodesk for $60M in 2012); he was CEO of Justin.tv and Socialcam. He graduated from Yale in 2005 in political science after an unusually extracurricular, disengaged college run — he was "invited to leave for a year" after his own admitted "sabotage" of his first senior semester, and found Yale classes "somewhat easier" than expected because students there seemed "more interested in getting the A than actually... engaging" ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689)). He spent a year as finance director on a US Senate campaign before tech, having originally planned to become a lawyer and eventually a Supreme Court justice — a plan he abandoned as he learned more about the profession. He had a pre-startup life script — "In my 20s, I'd focus on making money. In my 30s, I'd focus on having a family. In my 40s, I would do politics. And in my 60s, I would retire and teach" — and frames his YC career as "attempting to combine the 30s plan and the 60s plan" ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689)). He joined Y Combinator first as a **founder himself**, twice — Justin.tv/Twitch went through YC's w07 batch and Socialcam through w12 — before becoming a partner, then Managing Director/CEO of the core accelerator (2016–March 2024), and is now a Group Partner ([Partner Emeritus transition post, written by Garry Tan](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/michael-seibel-partner-emeritus)). Per YC's profile he has done thousands of office hours across 20+ batches with well over a thousand companies. He diagnoses his own career pattern bluntly: "It turns out I volunteer for jobs that nobody wants" — and stuck with the institutional climb because he came to see YC as something that "could be an institution that lasts for 100 years" the way "nobody knows who runs Yale right now" ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689)). His authority is grounded in building mediocre product for years before finding a working process — he teaches from "here's what I got wrong" and carries a standing disclaimer that he broke the rules he now preaches. The Justin.tv years were lean: "We were each given five hundred dollars a month walking around money... I slept in the living room and sometimes on the balcony." ([Building Product transcript](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product))

## Core ideas
- The job of an early-stage startup is to **build something people want**; growth is the byproduct, not something you manufacture with spend.
- **Products are instruments, not art.** "Products are not paintings. They're not art. If users don't find products useful, then the products are by definition not useful." ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product)) Usefulness, measured by users, is the only verdict.
- **Launch now.** Founders over-build before contact with reality; the fastest way to learn is to ship something embarrassing and iterate. And the launch matters far less to users than to you: "Launching is nowhere near as significant an event to your users as it is to you." ([Biggest Mistakes, via Moguldom](https://moguldom.com/221628/solving-a-problem-you-dont-care-about-some-of-the-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make-with-michael-seibel/)) Concretely, in the fuller talk transcript: "Do you remember the day Snapchat launched? Do you remember the day Instagram launched? Do you remember the day that WhatsApp launched? Remember the day that Uber or Lyft launched? Most likely, you don't." ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Talk to users + write code** should be ~80–90% of a founder's waking hours; audit last week, and if it's nearer 20% "you're probably doing something very, very wrong." ([Wharton/Dalton & Michael, via Startup Archive search](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/michael-seibel-on-why-you-need-to-analyze-the-frequency-and-intensity-of-your-customer-s-problem)) The alternative is what he calls **prioritizing sizzle over steak** and **"cargo-culting" a startup** — "press and hiring, conferences, investors... You are pretending to be a startup, but the actual work of startups is pushing product, getting it in the users' hands, and seeing if they like it." ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Self-deception is the master failure mode.** "The number one thing they lie about is whether they have product-market fit. The great founders, limit the amount they lie." ([Invest Like the Best EP.190](https://podcastnotes.org/investors-field-guide/lessons-from-thousands-of-startups-michael-seibel-on-invest-like-the-best-with-patrick-oshaughnessy/)) He catalogs the specific rationalizations he hears founders use to talk themselves into believing they have PMF: "famous investors invested in me. I must have product market fit... I got... YC I must have product market fit... I grew 50% this month. I must have product market fit... We have a million ARR. I must have product market fit. Or I need to say I have it to raise the next round[,] you start to believe your own fumes." ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689))
- **Fundraising is not success.** A big round from great investors does not equal PMF; money is a tool to be spent only to improve the company's prospects. His flattest statement of it, essay-length: "Success isn't the same as raising a round of financing. Quite the opposite: raising a round should be a byproduct of success," because "a financing round is not a milestone. It is literally cash." He extends the point past the individual founder to the ecosystem: using fundraising as a benchmark "encourages a culture of optimizing for short term showmanship instead of making something people want and creating lasting value." ([Fundraising Rounds Are Not Milestones](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/fundraising-rounds-are-not-milestones)) Counterintuitively, he argues raising *more* than you need can *lower* your odds: it resets other investors' bar for what "enough traction" looks like, and even disciplined founders reliably spend more once they have more in the bank. ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds))
- **YC is a product, not a curriculum.** "It's not like a school where we're like, 'Here's our syllabus.'... It's more like you're in the club and you get access to the resources... the bigger your ambition is, the more doors are gonna open." He concedes this is partly a marketing failure of his own making: "a bunch of the stuff we do is meant to be secret. I don't know how you market that." ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **Start from a problem you care about, not an idea.** "Starting with ideas is tricky because people immediately want to grade your idea. It's a lot easier to start with a problem." ([Startup Archive: get and test ideas](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/michael-seibel-on-how-to-get-and-test-startup-ideas)) Picking a problem you don't personally care about is the first fatal first-timer mistake — founders "lose motivation" mid-journey, with "no deep connection to the problem." ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Startups suit a specific temperament, not a wealth motive.** "There is a certain type of person who only works at their peak capacity when there is no predictable path to follow, the odds of success are low, and they have to take personal responsibility for failure." He won't sell it on money: "I cannot promise that doing a tech startup will make you rich (in fact the odds are against you becoming rich)." ([Why Should I Start a Startup?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-should-i-start-a-startup))
- **Opportunity, not talent, is the binding constraint on who builds.** "It's not a talent issue, tons of minority talent — it's an information and access issue." "The opportunity to start the next Google should be open to all." ([Black Founders AMA](https://medium.com/one-percent/everything-you-need-to-know-from-black-founders-ama-with-y-combinator-partner-michael-seibel-fd5bb42d829b))
- Process should be **lightweight but explicit**: write things down so the team shares one picture and can look back at what it learned.

## Mental models
- **MVP = "ridiculously simple."** The first thing you can give your first targeted users to see if you can deliver *any* value at all; "launch something bad quickly," then iterate. *Worked example:* he tells founders an MVP can be sub-software — "we see startups just start with a landing page and a spreadsheet," buildable "in weeks, not months"; and "Don't fall in love with your MVP... It's just step one in a journey." ([How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp))
- **Order of operations for starting up** ([One Order of Operations](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/one-order-of-operations-for-starting-a-startup)) — *inverts* "find a co-founder, pick an idea": (1) "Is there any particular problem that you are passionate about?"; (2) brainstorm solutions with friends; (3) turn those partners into co-founders — "this is the perfect time to ask them to work with you"; (4) "work together to build and launch a minimal viable product (MVP)"; (5) "Once you have your first users of any kind, you have a startup." Caveat: "you still need someone with the technical skills to ship the MVP." Problem and passion first; idea and team emerge *from* the problem.
- **Hold problem and customer tight, solution loose — the screwdriver/mechanic metaphor.** "Hold the problem you're solving tightly, hold the customer tightly, hold the solution you're building loosely." *Worked example (verbatim, from the live talk):* "the screwdriver is not actually good at screwing in anything, but I wonder what other problems it could solve... No, the problem was I need to screw something in. The user was like a mechanic. And if your screwdriver doesn't help the mechanic solve the problem, keep the mechanic. Keep the problem... Fix the [expletive] screwdriver." ([How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp)) This is the operational form of pivot-vs-iterate.
- **Pivot vs. iterate.** *Iterate* = change the solution (common, cheap). *Pivot* = change the customer or the problem (rare). Identifying the problem is "the genius part"; the solution is what you keep adjusting. *Worked example:* his own Justin.tv→Twitch arc. The fuller version of this story is *not* just "same broadcasting problem, different product" — it's user-love-driven: "we were kind of excited by the general idea of democratizing live video... but we weren't actually terribly in love with the people who chose to use the platform. As it continued to grow, and only when Emmett kind of really refocused the company around video game streaming did we regain the love for our users... that was really one of the core drivers of Twitch becoming a successful company." ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Grade customers by desperation, not by ease of access — the "hair on fire" / brick test.** "Whose business is going to go out of business without using you? Which people out there are not going to be able to get to work or to watch their kids?" *Worked example:* he redefines "easy" customers as the *most desperate* ones — they tolerate a bad MVP because they need the outcome. ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product)) *Extended, later framing of the same test:* "you want to build your first version for customers who have their hair on fire... imagine that you are a person and your hair is on fire right now... I'm a Founder I've got an MVP what I'm selling is a brick... you would buy that brick and you would hit yourself on the head with the brick to smother the fire... you are in so much pain as a customer you will use a non-perfect solution to solve your problem." Heuristic that falls out of it: "for customers who are not desperate you can wait you don't have to go after them now — just go after the desperate ones first." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **Filter your early users hard.** "The best startups very heavily filter the people who are able to use the initial product and make sure that they're the right type of initial customer." *Worked example:* restricting who can touch v1 is a feature — it concentrates feedback on people who actually have the problem. ([Get and test ideas](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/michael-seibel-on-how-to-get-and-test-startup-ideas)) Restated as a closing maxim elsewhere: "it's far better to have a hundred people love your product than a hundred thousand who kind of like it... it's totally okay to do things that don't scale and recruit those initial customers one at a time." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **The Jedi / dumb founder / midwit meme — applied twice, to two different decisions.** A meme he leans on repeatedly: two very different people (a deep expert and a naive first-timer) converge on the same simple right answer, while the person in the middle overthinks it into the wrong one.
  - *Applied to equity:* "the Jedi founder and the dumb founder... they're both generous with co-founder Equity. The midwit... they're thinking about skill sets and contributions and time commitments and the network and various things... trying to do a really complicated calculation. I'm going to argue today that's not required." ([Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
  - *Applied to MVP scoping:* "the founder who's doing all the best things and knows all the best things and the idiot the first time founder... many times these two Founders will actually come to the right decision before the founder who is really smart and is trying to work really hard and do everything right." The smart-seeming wrong path: "100 surveys and 600 user interviews and contact every single one of the competitors and spend you know a year fundraising and hire 100 people." The Jedi/idiot-converging answer: "launch something quickly and iterate." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **Restate hard ideas as easy ones.** "Most hard ideas can be restated as an easy idea. If you just understand what bits of your hard idea are both useless and hard, and most of the time there are useless and hard bits." Strip the parts that are simultaneously hard *and* not load-bearing. ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product))
- **The three product goals → one top KPI.** *Worked example:* "At Socialcam, the top level KPI was DAU... the three ways we thought we contributed to DAU was new users, retention of users, and new content created." Every product meeting laddered to one of those three. ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product))
- **Measurement is part of the spec.** "Building measurement is part of a product spec. So when you spec out a product, you better spec the stats you expect to be tracking." Not using analytics is a named first-timer mistake. ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product))
- **The scientific method for startups — the parent framework behind "measurement is part of the spec."** The loop is literally "hypothesize > a/b test > conclude > repeat," and running it "requires having measurement in place... pre-existing analytics measuring what your users are doing every day." The failure mode it kills is treating intuition as fact — "you cannot have the attitude: 'This is obvious, why would we measure it?'" — and its cultural precondition is a founder "willing to use data to back up their decision making" who doesn't "believe that standards or aesthetics are rules written in stone." He frames the whole enterprise as a two-mode balance, not pure rationalism: "executing a startup is a balance between creativity/intuition/instinct and the scientific method" — inspiration finds the problem; the method decides which solution actually works. ([The Scientific Method for Startups](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-scientific-method-for-startups))
- **Difficulty grading (Easy/Medium/Hard).** Engineers grade each idea so non-technical teammates learn the cost of their asks; prioritize the one hard item, then mediums, then easys. *Worked example, exact thresholds from his team:* "Hard meant it would take one engineer most of the dev cycle to build. Medium typically meant two to three days. Easy means we could do multiple in a day." ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product))
- **Heavy MVP vs. lean MVP.** A coinage he dates live in the talk itself: "heavy MVP... I just invented that term heavy MVP when I made this presentation two days ago. So, maybe [it] becomes a thing." *Worked example:* for regulated or hard-tech industries (insurance, banking, drones, rockets, biotech, tunneling) where you can't launch a full product in weeks, the heavy-MVP fallback is "a simple website that explains what you do," buildable "in days, not weeks," used as a reference point in conversations with early users and regulators. ([How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp))
- **Development cycle dictated by the product.** Two-week cycles for iOS (App Store review), faster for web, longer for hardware. If your process is arguing, no written spec, and long dev cycles, you're doing it wrong. (Paraphrase, [Product Development Cycle Fundamentals](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/product-development-cycle-fundamentals))
- **Time-box, write down, and ruthlessly cut the spec.** Three mechanical hacks, stated across two talks: (1) time-box to a fixed ship date; (2) write the spec down, specifically to stop scope drift when an offhand comment nudges the plan ("your three-week plan turns into a three-month plan" if unwritten); (3) cut mid-sprint against a single filter — "is there a truly desperate customer need" for this feature — and don't fall in love with the MVP, fall in love with the customer. Momentum favors you once something ships ("the momentum to keep anything going is extremely strong") and works against you if nothing has ("very easy to just delay, delay, delay"). ([How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp); [How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **90/10 solution.** Get 90% of the value with 10% of the effort and ship that first.
- **Default alive.** Know whether current burn and runway let you reach profitability/PMF on the cash you have.
- **Fear as a signal, not a stop sign.** Two complementary framings. *Operational:* "your company doesn't actually die right like it doesn't die tomorrow... ask like well what would actually happen like imagine the worst case scenario... it's not bad to feel the fear but it is bad to act on it." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp)) *Diagnostic:* "Fear is the most useful tool in a startup if it's used correctly... look at your to-dos today. Which of the items makes your stomach hurt? You should probably do this." ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689))
- **Innovate on the product, copy everyone else on the rest.** Innovation budget is scarce; spend it only on differentiated product/customer value, adopt boring proven defaults (stack, office, hiring, org chart, corporate structure) everywhere else. Named, in the podcast delivery, as **"innovation juice"** — a finite resource founders burn by stacking multiple low-probability bets (a novel legal structure *and* a novel tech stack *and* a contrarian location) on top of the one real miracle they need. Cited anti-patterns (paraphrase, no official transcript — treat as summary of the episode, not verbatim): YC applicants who pick a non-standard incorporation as a "voluntary red flag"; OpenAI's nonprofit-owns-for-profit structure as a cautionary example even by its own architects' later admission; Digg's stack of risky infrastructure bets against Reddit's plain one. Once you've made one startup work, you've "earned" the right to spend energy on idiosyncratic bets on your *next* one. ([Stop Innovating on the Wrong Things](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/stop-innovating-on-the-wrong-things))
- **"What's only possible now?"** Test for AI startups: build the thing that was impossible until the new capability existed, not the obvious wrapper. *Worked example:* it was obvious maps would come to the phone; it was *not* obvious Uber would. The non-obvious second-order use is where defensibility lives. ([Dalton & Michael on AI](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/dalton-and-michael-on-ai))
- **The layman test.** "A founder who truly understands what they're working on is able to explain it to a layman." Inability to explain simply signals the founder hasn't narrowed the problem enough. ([Invest Like the Best EP.190](https://podcastnotes.org/investors-field-guide/lessons-from-thousands-of-startups-michael-seibel-on-invest-like-the-best-with-patrick-oshaughnessy/))
- **Product brain vs. business brain.** "Are you using your product brain or using your business brain?... your product brain tells you you don't like the peanut butter you just bought... Your business brain's job is to figure out whether that problem can build a billion dollar business... What I've noticed is that everyone's business brain sucks... before you have product market fit, it does." *Worked example — Brex:* the founders "applied to YC with some crazy weird business brain idea... something with VR... While being at YC... they couldn't get a credit card even though we had fun[ded] them and had a business... they noticed that all the other young founders and international founders in the batch couldn't get credit cards either... That's like thinking from your product brain." A second worked example he cites approvingly: "Mark Andreessen didn't invent the mosaic browser with his team at University of Illinois because he thought there was a market for browsers... It was like he's using the internet[,] it doesn't work for him. He needs to make the internet useful for him." ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689))
- **Do things that don't scale = software edition.** With Dalton Caldwell he extends PG's essay to operational unscalable work — onboard clients by hand, do the opposite of well-funded competitors. *Worked example referenced:* the "Collison installation" — when someone agreed to try Stripe, the founders said "give me your laptop" and set them up on the spot rather than emailing a signup link. ([Dalton & Michael: Things That Don't Scale](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/If-dalton-michael-what-does-it-really-mean-to-do-things-that-don-t-scale)) A related worked example: Stripe's own day-one product, before it even had its name — "back then it didn't even have the name stripe it was called slash depth payments... they had no fancy Bank deal they were working with a tiny Bank... every night file manual paperwork... the first version of stripe was so basic that even us back in the day at twitch couldn't use it." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **Three real-world fast-MVP proof points, told first-person.** Airbnb's 2008 MVP had no online payments (guests paid hosts in person), no map view, a part-time engineer, and "only worked for conferences." Twitch/Justin.tv day one was a single 24/7 channel of Justin's own life, low-resolution, no games, streamed on an unbuilt, expensive-to-rent video system. Stripe's day-one build is above. Pattern: "all of these products were fast to build... they all had very limited functionality... all these products appealed to a small set of users." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **"Fake Steve Jobs" syndrome.** The perfectionist who thinks "I know what the perfect product is and I know it's going to take a year to build, why would I build shitty versions of it." *Rebuttal, with worked counter-evidence:* "the iPhone started without an app store... you couldn't take video with the first iPhone... the first iPhone only had 2G and not 3G... the first version of the iPod had like an actual physical scrolling device where like [it] would get stuck into it and it would break all the time. Even the great Steve Jobs iterated his products over time." ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **Real PMF is the product breaking, under profitable usage — not a feeling.** Corrects a common misconception: real PMF requires *both* (1) usage outgrowing what you built for scale (technical or operational breakage) *and* (2) good unit economics on the users you're getting ("not paying $1,000 for a user who's only going to ever give you $100"). Causes of *fake* PMF: raising from impressive people ("if these people chose their company, that means that they must have product market fit"); raising a Series A too early (shifts focus from product to "company building"); "magical thinking" (not tracking churn/payback); weak technical talent (avoiding the hard truth the product needs work). Preventive measures: pick one honest KPI (usually revenue) and don't swap it when it stops looking good; **"cap your burn"** pre-PMF and only exceed the cap with revenue, not raised capital; the **"three-month essential rule"** for hires — calendar a check three months out, and if you wouldn't be devastated to lose this hire, they weren't essential. ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)) Cross-references the essay-level definition already in the corpus ([The Real Product Market Fit](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit)) but adds the causes→signs→fixes machinery and the two concrete preventive rules.
- **Turning your investor into your boss.** Named failure mode: founders let fear and the false belief in "100% repeatable paths to victory" (true of law/medicine/apprenticeship models, false in startups) create a vacuum that investor opinion fills instead of customer contact. Closing heuristic: "you're the one who gives investors power over your business... all they can do is use words." ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds))
- **Relationship debt.** Named, explicitly analogized to technical debt: "the more and more relationship debt you get, just like technical debt, the harder and harder it is to execute... your relationship with the co-founder falls over if you have too much relationship debt." Causes include unrealistic expectations imported from TechCrunch fundraising envy ("This company that sucks raised $10 million on TechCrunch... why can't we raise $10 million?"). Preventive measure: the **"level three conversation"** — a scheduled, safe-space, non-crisis conversation to pay the debt down honestly, plus an explicit roles-and-responsibilities talk. ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)) *Lived worked example, not just theory:* by the founding team's own later account, Justin.tv ran on exactly this dysfunction for years — "Michael was the CEO but... he wasn't making the decisions like a CEO because we wouldn't let him," decisions were settled by "a constant war of attrition," and the team joked about deliberately never writing decisions down "so debates could be endlessly re-opened." ([Twitch Co-Founders Reunion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vG-Sxk-c2k)) His own teaching material reads, in this light, as a direct correction of his own company's first four years.
- **Ordinary vs. extraordinary.** "The failure rate at startups is so high that being average amongst smart people isn't enough. You've got to be extraordinary... many standard deviations better than the other people who are doing startups around you." Framework: **"the people around you are the floor and not the ceiling."** Preventive measures: assemble a **"Jedi council"** of advisors more extraordinary than you; set goals ~15% above what you think you can hit; treat extraordinary as trainable — he cites *Atomic Habits* (James Clear) by name and a personal disclosure of his own weight struggle, corrected by the book's reframing: "the people who are more effective than you learn how to do hard things... it's that they've trained themselves to be better at doing things that are hard." ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)) He also builds directly on a framing he credits to Dalton Caldwell: "the average YC company dies. So please don't strive to be average" — extending it himself with the school-vs-startup contrast (being average among smart people works in school, fails in startups) and a worked example: Zapier came into YC already profitable on a $1.2M demo-day raise "they've never spent," which he now regrets not funding — "I really should have given them some money." ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689))
- **The "cap-table cake" fallacy.** Against treating investor names as a product ingredient: "you're not baking a cake where you sprinkle in three different investors and then a cake pops out. Like, that's like the stupidest thing I've ever heard." ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **The "sit down and shut up" skill — a self-incriminating origin story.** From a two-hour, company-splitting argument with co-founder Emmett Shear over whether to show AM/PM next to chat timestamps: "After the first 30 minutes of violent argument Justin left the room... at hour 2 Emmett screamed 'Fuck it! I'm going to do it the way I want!'" Weeks later, after a brutal day of firefighting, the same two "couldn't help but laugh at our 'epic debate.' Of all the decisions we had to make, AM/PM could have been the most inconsequential ever." His stated lesson: "pre-launch it is very tempting to assume that you can make your product perfect. This feeling pushes people to turn small decisions into productivity killing arguments. Even worse, these urges are a classic trap for non-technical co-founders because without the ability to write code — argument feels like the only tool in the tool-belt. Thankfully I learned the skill of sitting down and shutting up." ([The AM/PM Debate](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-am-pm-debate-or-how-not-to-be-an-idiot-ceo)) Heuristic: when a decision is inconsequential and you're the non-technical co-founder, defer to whoever has to build it rather than spend scarce pre-launch energy arguing.
- **"YC as compounding software."** YC's equity price (7%) is fixed but the "software" (alumni network, Bookface, Series A program, Growth Program) keeps shipping new features for free, so the deal's *effective* value rises every year post-investment: "I think of YC as an amazing piece of software that gets better every year and remains the same price." *Worked example, hard numbers:* standard deal $20K→$150K for the same 7%; typical valuation $3.5M (2007) vs. $6–18M (2019); Demo Day investor count 50 → 600–800 in the room plus 1,000+ online. ([YC Has Changed](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/yc-has-changed))
- **Seed pitch structure (six elements).** (1) what you do in two plain sentences + one vivid example; (2) team slide of specific *accomplishments*, not titles; (3) traction *with timeframes* ("0→1,000 users in 8 weeks") — investors fund momentum, not absolute numbers; (4) a non-obvious insight about the problem; (5) market size with transparent bottom-up math (approach beats the number); (6) a direct ask tied to 18–24-month milestones. Governing rule: "better to be 80% accurate and 100% clear." ([SaaStr 2025](https://www.saastr.com/the-y-combinator-guide-to-perfectly-pitching-your-seed-stage-startup-with-michael-seibel/))
- **Nostalgia-adjusted survivorship bias.** People assume "early YC" was better because Airbnb/Dropbox are now household names; he counters it "often takes 10 years or more" for that fame to arrive, and names 2013–2016-batch companies (Coinbase, DoorDash, Flexport, Gusto, Instacart, Rappi, Zenefits) that hadn't yet reached that recognition as of writing but were comparable bets. ([YC Has Changed](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/yc-has-changed))

## Decision heuristics
- Before building: have *some* conversations with users — not three years of research, just enough to point the MVP at the most desperate people.
- Identify the most desperate customer and talk to them first; that's what makes a "hard" launch easy.
- **Your first users must come from your own network, not from digging.** "It's interesting to me that you chose a problem where you don't know anyone who has the problem, including yourself... where I get my first two users or one user or three users or five users? That should come from people you know or you already have identified through some other way." If you can't name a single person with the problem, that's diagnostic — you likely picked the wrong problem. Only the jump from 5 to 100 or 1,000 needs real search. ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- When prioritizing a cycle: pick the single hard bet first (only one fits), then fill with mediums and easys — this kills personality-driven pet projects.
- When you hit a wall: ask whether the screwdriver is wrong before assuming the mechanic or the problem is. Iterate the solution; change customer/problem only as a last resort.
- Treat a customer's polite "no" as a real no: "we really love your tech, but we can't pick it up today because X... we'll be in touch soon" means "they don't want your tech." Be a dispassionate scientist — hear "no," don't dwell, move to the next prospect. ([Successful Founders Are OK With Rejection](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/DV-dalton-michael-successful-founders-are-ok-with-rejection); co-stated with Dalton Caldwell, search-extracted — verify exact wording before public quoting.) The "dispassionate scientist" register isn't incidental to his rejection advice — it's his stated operating philosophy: a startup is a "hypothesize > a/b test > conclude > repeat" loop, and a prospect's "no" is just a failed test to log and move past, not a verdict on you. ([The Scientific Method for Startups](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-scientific-method-for-startups))
- When raising: "raise money as quickly as possible and then get back to work." Optimize getting back to building, not valuation.
- Before raising *more*: ask "why does your company deserve more money?" Without PMF, save cash and spend time with customers — don't ramp spend on people/office/equipment. ([Why Does Your Company Deserve More Money?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-does-your-company-deserve-more-money))
- Stay lean and keep burn low until you are genuinely drowning in demand — don't read a big round or headcount as PMF. ([The Real Product Market Fit](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit))
- **Cap your pre-PMF burn**, and only exceed the cap using revenue, never using capital you raised for exactly this reason. Put a calendar reminder three months out on every hire and ask whether losing them would devastate you — if not, they weren't essential (**the "three-month essential rule"**). ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds))
- Fire bad customers who drain resources without proportional revenue or learning — and refuse the ones who pull the roadmap off-target in the first place. He names the specific type: "hijackers" who "try to hijack your product to solve a problem you didn't intend to solve... these hijackers are users you don't want," because serving them "is a consulting business and if you are doing a tech startup - you don't want to be in the business of solving one off problems for users." The discipline is saying no politely: "being good at customer service doesn't mean serving every potential customer... you shouldn't feel bad telling them that you're focused on solving another problem." The cost of not filtering: "If you let any user that walks in the door steer the product roadmap you're going to end up doing a shitty job at half-solving a lot of problems." ([Users You Don't Want](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/users-you-don-t-want))
- Don't scale unit economics until they're profitable and you have PMF.
- For an AI idea, ask "is this only possible now?" — if it could have shipped two years ago, expect commoditization.
- When pitching, lead with clarity over precision and keep it short — the longer you talk, the more you risk.
- Pick co-founders you already know well, and have the uncomfortable conversations early: "It's nice to have a pre-existing relationship with your cofounder so you have a sense of how you're going to get through the hard times together" — and that relationship can be "a friendship... a co-worker... someone in your school you did a school project with." ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Name the recurring co-founder conflict triad up front — Performance, Goals, Rules.** "Is my co-founder working as hard as I am? Are we trying to accomplish the same goal? Whose job is it to do engineering? Whose job is it to do product? Whose job is it to talk to customers?" Surfacing these three explicitly, early, is cheaper than letting relationship debt accrue. ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- When asking anyone for help: skip the coffee/dinner preamble. "Just send an email that can be read in 30 seconds or less and ask for the advice you want." ([How to Ask for Advice](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-ask-for-advice)) Applied specifically to investor outreach, a **three-sentence cold-email framework**: (1) what you do, jargon-free; (2) sell the excitement — market size, growth, notable founders, nothing about your resume or personal story; (3) a direct ask — advice, investment, or an intro, never a call or meeting ("Let me escalate things"). *Worked example, verbatim, given as the model email:* "Hey Michael, My name is Tim and I'm building Twitch for cooking. I previously ran programming at the Food Network and my technical co-founder is a college friend. We're working on our mvp and were wondering whether we should build out private messaging in addition to group chat or just group chat alone. Thanks, Tim." Named common mistakes: walls of text, chasing warm intros instead of just emailing, complimenting the investor instead of getting to the point, not using an @company.com address. Quantified stakes: "Every day I receive hundreds of emails. Of those I probably need to read 250 and respond to 50." ([How to Email Early Stage Investors](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-email-early-stage-investors))
- Don't trust local/regional startup "experts" reflexively: their incentives (jobs, local economy) may diverge from yours.
- **Distrust advice that is 100% teardown and 0% alternative.** "If you ever see someone making an argument that's one hundred percent ripping something else down and zero percent explaining why their thing is good... maybe that's your business model is selling fear. Not the kind of investor I'd want on my cap table." A general-purpose filter for evaluating any advice-giver, not just anti-YC critics. ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **Don't anchor your fundraise expectations on other companies' press.** Named failure mode, "TechCrunch anchoring": "One of the classic mistakes we see from later-stage YC companies is they base how easy or hard they think their fundraise will be based on TechCrunch articles they read of other people raising... garbage in and garbage out." ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **Apply earlier and cleaner rather than "get traction first."** Worked contrast: a company that "raise[d] a pre-seed round and sold thirty percent to some random investor, ha[d] some co-founder who lasted a year, and then... fire[d] them, and now [has]... a bunch of equity gone through three pivots and burned half a million dollars" vs. "the brand new company that started a month ago... the cap table is completely clean" — "who do you think is more likely to succeed?" Directly rebuts the folk theory that more traction beats a clean start. ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **On a co-founder split (departing or fired, pre-cliff):** a departing/fired founder should retain no more than ~5% (2–5% depending on timing) if they leave before the one-year cliff, resign the board, and sign a release, typically granting proxy to those staying. A fired founder taking 1–3 months' severance is reasonable; a founder who simply leaves getting severance is "not typical." Reject "I already agreed to it," "I started six months earlier," "I raised the money first," or "I was hired post-launch" as valid reasons for a large equity gap — in every case "99% of the work is still left to be done." ([Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
- **Separate salary and equity as two different levers, and never trade one for the other.** "Salary is the money that someone needs to live... Equity is what's going to motivate them to work extremely hard... I never like to think about reducing someone's Equity because they need more salary." ([Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
- **Retain the explicit right to fire an underperforming co-founder, regardless of equity split**, as a stated condition of the CEO role: "if you're not willing to join a team under these circumstances... maybe you should go start a company and be the CEO." ([Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
- **Diagnose engineer exploitation with a five-axis self-check**, run informally with Dalton Caldwell: equity size/justification; a real seat at the decision-making table; relative effort vs. non-technical co-founders; honesty about whether the thing is actually working; and the opportunity cost of leaving. Worked example used as the show's running callback: the technical co-founder reads the analytics, asks "our launch bombed, what's the plan?" and is told "don't worry about it, this is my department... stop asking questions" — named explicitly, by one host, as **gaslighting**: "I never used the term gaslighting but it is... it's actually that." Remediation heuristic for someone who realizes they're underpaid or excluded: ask for a seat at the table before quitting; if that fails, accept that a career move can go backward before it goes forward — "sometimes you have to take a step backwards to take two steps forward." ([How To NOT Get Screwed As A Software Engineer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fcfVjd_oV1I))
- **On whether to trust your gut vs. seek outside validation ("commit or validate")** — co-stated with Dalton Caldwell, attribution between the two hosts is not reliably separable in this source (no speaker diarization); treated here as joint. Founders with real domain expertise (built the exact tool inside a company, worked the job for years) should trust their gut *more* than they think — self-check: "make something that would impress you... is this solving a problem I have or a problem I've solved before." Founders without that depth, whose instincts run "very mid" / "50th percentile," should lean toward *building* expertise inside the startup rather than assuming they're *bringing* it. *Worked A/B example:* two identical mortgage-underwriting startups — a founder with 10 years inside a mortgage company "probably know[s] way more than the average bear" and should trust their gut; a founder with only personal house-buying experience "should probably trust their gut a little bit less," though "not [that] these folks can't still be successful." Named failure mode — **inverted confidence**: the expert founder gets fearful and chases a fundraising narrative (rebranding a correct instinct as "AI" to raise money) while the inexperienced founder over-plans (headcount models, market-size reports, a two-year build) instead of shipping fast. Explicit non-endorsement: "we're not trying to give quote-unquote permission to take 10 years to build the magical thing." ([When Should You Trust Your Gut?](https://creators.spotify.com/pod/profile/ycombinator/episodes/When-Should-You-Trust-Your-Gut---Dalton--Michael-Podcast-e2ihi0m))

## Positions by decision type
- **Pivot vs. persevere.** Persevere on the problem and customer; iterate the solution relentlessly. Pivot (change customer/problem) only when the problem itself proves wrong — "Hold the problem tightly. Hold the customer tightly. Hold the solution loosely." Justin.tv→Twitch is his canonical case, and he's explicit the real driver was regaining love for a specific user group (gamers), not just a broadcasting-technology change. ([How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp); [Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make))
- **Build vs. over-build (the "Steve Jobs defense").** Ship a rough MVP and iterate; reject perfectionism dressed up as taste. He rebuts founders who cite Jobs with two independent versions of the same counter-evidence: "Real Steve Jobs released a shitty MVP that was revolutionary, but still fairly shitty and every year iterated it" ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product)), and, naming the perfectionist type explicitly as "fake Steve Jobs," the iPhone's missing app store/video/3G and the iPod's failure-prone scroll wheel ([How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp)).
- **How to price (and the pre-sale trap).** Charge enough not to lose money; don't discount to win early commitments. "The number one mistake I see with pre-sales is discounting... especially hardware founders will misunderstand how much they need to charge so they don't lose money and so their pre-sale becomes their death." ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product)) A distinct discount trap on the demand side, credited to YC partner **Paul Buchheit** and extended with his own example: selling something worth a dollar for 75 cents manufactures fake demand — his own illustration is a cupcake shop that sells at 80% off via Groupon, gets swamped, and wrongly concludes "I must have the best cupcakes in the world," when the actual signal is bargain-seeking, not product quality. He ties this explicitly to easy-money eras: "amplified in a world with cheap money... it's easy to buy growth, scale negative margins and not really understand why customers are using your product." ([Pattern Breakers](https://podcasts.apple.com/us/podcast/michael-seibel-the-godfounder-discusses-justin-tv/id1488560647?i=1000653884689))
- **When to fire (or refuse) a customer.** Two distinct cuts. First, fire customers who consume support/resources out of proportion to the revenue or learning they return (consistent with grading customers by value/desperation). Second, and earlier, *refuse to onboard* the "hijacker" — the user who wants you to solve an adjacent problem you didn't set out to solve. Taking them on quietly converts a product company into a consulting shop ("solving one off problems for users") and, at scale, "half-solving a lot of problems" instead of fully solving one. The tell that you've drifted: your roadmap is being steered by whoever walked in the door rather than by your most desperate target user. ([Users You Don't Want](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/users-you-don-t-want))
- **Raise vs. bootstrap / how to raise.** Raise to remove the constraint, then stop and build — fast close over high valuation. He frames a fat round as a *risk* of self-deception about PMF, not a milestone ([Why Does Your Company Deserve More Money?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-does-your-company-deserve-more-money)), and generalizes it with a counterintuitive claim: "all things being equal, raising more money than you need translates into less success, not more" — because it raises the next round's bar for what "enough" traction looks like, and even talented founders reliably spend whatever they have. ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)) *Lived worked example of near-death forcing the right pivot:* Justin.tv, early 2010 — "50 employees and 8 months of runway... we had users (30m monthly actives) and revenue ($4-6m revenue run rate) but weren't growing so I tried to fundraise and buy ourselves more time. I failed." Cutting spend instead ("within 2 months we went from burning $250k a month to making $100k a month") freed the team to notice two organic side-experiments already living inside the company — Socialcam (sold for $60M, 2012) and Twitch (sold for $1B, 2014) — both of which outlived the parent. ([Why Does Your Company Deserve More Money?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-does-your-company-deserve-more-money))
- **How to know you have PMF.** Not a round, not headcount. Borrowing Andreessen, it's when "customers are buying the product just as fast as you can make it." His fuller definition, stated as a correction of a common misconception: the product must be *breaking* under usage growth **and** carry profitable unit economics simultaneously — either one alone is fake PMF. Signals of the fake version: rapid headcount growth, more business people than engineers, no metrics dashboard, "too many nice things" (offices, trips, dinners), and swapping your KPI when the old one stops looking good. ([The Real Product Market Fit](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/the-real-product-market-fit); [5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds))
- **How to split founder equity.** Equal or near-equal, decided early. "Founders tend to make the mistake of splitting equity based on early work." "It takes 7 to 10 years to build a company of great value. Small variations in year one do not justify massively different founder equity splits in year 2-10." He rejects idea-origination as a basis ("Startups are about execution, not about ideas") and, in a fuller live version of the same thesis, names the specific fallacy of rewarding tenure: pointing to Bezos-at-Amazon and Zuckerberg-at-Facebook as the naive intuition ("you would think that means that those people should deserve vastly more Equity"), then rebutting it — "those first four to six years is where a lot of value is being created and those first four to six years is when most companies die... those co-founders actually got you the energy of activation your company needed to even be in the game." ([How to Split Equity Among Founders](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-split-equity-among-founders); [Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid)) All equity should carry standard vesting and a cliff — "not something that you should be innovating on" — precisely so a founder can leave, or be let go, without destroying the cap table; he similarly rejects performance-based/dynamic equity as unworkable pre-PMF, when goals are too fuzzy to set fairly. ([Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
- **Focus vs. diversify (customers).** Focus on a small group who love the product over a large group who kind of like it; filter early users hard. ([Get and test ideas](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/michael-seibel-on-how-to-get-and-test-startup-ideas))
- **How to pitch investors vs. how to pitch customers — two structurally different pitches.** A customer already feels the problem, so jargon builds credibility and the goal is to get them talking; "the investor might not have the problem that you're trying to solve at all and might be only interested in the solution in so much as they think it can build a big company," so the same jargon "is fluff that an investor has to filter out." *Worked example, self-contrasted:* "Google organizes the world's information" tells an investor nothing, versus the mechanistic version — "Google's a new type of search engine. With Google, you actually go to our website, you type what you want to know into a search box, you click enter, and we report in an ordered list websites that we think can help you with the information you're looking for. What makes it different is that those websites are ranked by how other websites link to them." Investor-pitch checklist, in his own six questions: what you do, how far along, market size, pricing plan, a non-obvious insight, and whether the team can build it. Exception: consumer products (Yelp, Uber) pitch closer to the customer version, because the investor is plausibly also a user. Diagnostic he's observed near-universally at YC: founders spend their first month trying to merge the two pitches, and only after failing repeatedly accept they need two. ([How Pitching Investors Is Different Than Pitching Customers](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/7u-how-pitching-investors-is-different-than-pitching-customers))
- **Where to locate (time-stamped).** In 2019: "I think it is so much easier to do tech here [the Bay Area] — I'd say move." ([Black Founders AMA](https://medium.com/one-percent/everything-you-need-to-know-from-black-founders-ama-with-y-combinator-partner-michael-seibel-fd5bb42d829b)) Reaffirmed in a separate, later essay in a more hedged form: "my best advice is to move to a tech hub (preferably the Bay Area) and work for a tech company until you can save the money, make friends with the right potential teammates, or discover the problem that you are passionate about. It usually takes at least 10 years to build a large and impactful company. It's fine to delay your start date." ([Three Paths in the Tech Industry](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/three-paths-in-the-tech-industry)) Flag as time-stamped; see Blind spots.
- **Pitch length.** Short. "A lot of people practice 10-minute, 30-minute, hour-long pitches. I think that's all garbage." "I think you can get all of your points across in two minutes." "The more you talk, the more you have an opportunity to say something that people don't like." And never open with origin story. ([How to create a great startup pitch](https://www.startuparchive.org/p/michael-seibel-on-how-to-create-a-great-startup-pitch))
- **What to evaluate in a startup (his order, as an investor).** (1) the team's ability to build and launch v1; (2) evidence of forward motion since starting; (3) evidence of a strong co-founder relationship. ([Invest Like the Best EP.190](https://podcastnotes.org/investors-field-guide/lessons-from-thousands-of-startups-michael-seibel-on-invest-like-the-best-with-patrick-oshaughnessy/))
- **Whether to go all-in in your 20s.** Yes — fewer responsibilities (no kids, no mortgage) make it the cheapest decade to take startup risk. He qualifies this for underrepresented founders already serving as family safety nets. ([This Is What Young Founders Should Focus On](https://creators.spotify.com/pod/profile/y-combinator/episodes/This-Is-What-Young-Founders-Should-Focus-On-e2vgiin))
- **Whether to start at all.** Only if you "enjoy being the underdog," "seek hard challenges others avoid," and "thrive under personal responsibility" — not for money or for an interesting technical problem (both available at large companies). ([Why Should I Start a Startup?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-should-i-start-a-startup))
- **Founder vs. executive vs. employee — the three legitimate career paths.** He explicitly excludes investor as a fourth path ("the best path to being an investor... starts with being successful (or failing) at one of these three first") and, unusually for an advice-giver, flags his own bias up front: "Often when they get advice, people tell them to follow the path that they followed (as a YC partner and former founder, I'm very guilty of this)." *Founder path* has a four-item prerequisite checklist ranked by importance, with idea deliberately last: (1) teammates with the technical skills to build the MVP; (2) a financial plan; (3) a problem you and they are passionate about; (4) "the least important (but still required)" — an idea for how to solve it. Named self-sabotage pattern: "trying to hustle around a missing prerequisite instead of solving the underlying issue... If your team doesn't have the technical skills to build your MVP, don't work with a dev shop. Make friends with people who do." He explicitly downgrades experience: "Experience is over-valued (not completely unimportant – but massively over-valued)... you will learn most of what you need to know about your problem, your customer, and the best solution after you start your company." *Executive path* has two named routes with worked examples — ride one fast-growing company up (his example: "one of the first 100 people at Facebook" who stayed ten years), or move diagonally between name-brand companies ("start out of college at Google, get hired at Dropbox as a team lead, move to Yahoo to become a director, move back to Google") — with an embedded critique: "producing results isn't necessarily how you move up the corporate ladder. Internal politics are usually as important, if not more so." *Employee path*: less management overhead, more direct impact on the customer, "much easier to optimize... as a software developer." Closing heuristic on multi-year exploration in your 20s: "if it takes 5-10 years to truly get good at something and you spend 10 years discovering what you want to get good at... it's not that you shouldn't explore, it's that you need to understand the costs of that exploration and plan accordingly." ([Three Paths in the Tech Industry](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/three-paths-in-the-tech-industry))

## Voice & style
Direct, plain-spoken, anti-fluff. Short declarative sentences and imperatives ("launch now," "talk to users," "write it down," "fix the screwdriver"). Profanity for emphasis when puncturing a myth ("a shitty MVP," "that's all garbage," "fix the [expletive] screwdriver"). Teaches from his own failures at Justin.tv/Socialcam rather than abstract theory, and leans on concrete company examples (Airbnb, Twitch, Stripe, DoorDash, Brex, Zapier) — e.g. on Airbnb he flips to the supply side: hosts "intensely want their house to always be booked and are willing to pay 12% fees" ([Invest Like the Best EP.190](https://podcastnotes.org/investors-field-guide/lessons-from-thousands-of-startups-michael-seibel-on-invest-like-the-best-with-patrick-oshaughnessy/)). His default teaching schema, recurring across at least five separate frameworks in one talk alone, is clinical: name the failure mode, then causes → signs → preventive measures — he says as much himself, mid-lecture: "causes, I feel like I'm a doctor" ([5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds)). Allergic to jargon, vision-speak, and fundraising vanity — he even names MVP itself as an instance of the jargon problem he otherwise rails against ("we always yell at founders to not use jargon. Yet we have this whole set of stupid startup jargon and MVP is one of them," [How to Plan an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/6f-how-to-plan-an-mvp)). Characteristic move: reframe a founder's anxious question into a simpler concrete one ("what problem are you solving, in one sentence?"; "who are your most desperate customers?"), or coin a term live and flag that he's doing it ("heavy MVP... I just invented that term two days ago"). Often pre-empts the survivorship critique against himself, sometimes with hard numbers instead of just a disclaimer. Uses vivid physical metaphors (the screwdriver and mechanic; the hair-on-fire brick; "rip that blanket off"). Self-identifies, in a joking taxonomy with his Justin.tv co-founders, as "the teacher" of the founding group — "the people you're teaching are... smarter than you, have more horsepower than you, but... they're not ready to kind of harness it, and your job is to make sure the class just kind of stays somewhat functional" ([Twitch Co-Founders Reunion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vG-Sxk-c2k)) — a good gloss on his advisory register generally: less "here is the answer" than "here is the constraint you're not naming." Calm, unflappable, slightly contrarian toward Silicon Valley hype, but candid about his own draw to chaos: "god point a gun at my head and we'll get through it — but god forbid we're safe, ah, this is kind of boring" ([Twitch Co-Founders Reunion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vG-Sxk-c2k)). In the Dalton & Michael format he plays the more operational/founder-experience voice to Dalton's investor-pattern voice, and several of the show's sharpest lines are not reliably separable between the two hosts in un-diarized transcripts — treat lines marked "joint" in this dossier as co-stated, not solo Seibel.

## Where they disagree
- **vs. the "Steve Jobs" perfectionist myth (design-led founders).** Where a design-led founder ships only when it's right, Seibel ships rough and iterates in public, naming the type "fake Steve Jobs" and citing the iPhone's missing app store and the iPod's failure-prone scroll wheel as counter-evidence. ([Building Product](https://podcasts.happyscribe.com/happy-scribe-s-favorites/transcript-of-michael-seibel-building-product); [How to Build an MVP](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/Io-how-to-build-an-mvp))
- **vs. Paul Graham / Brian Chesky on "founder mode."** Seibel reads the Chesky talk and Graham essay and lands moderate: be a chief-product-officer-level expert in your own product, but don't let "founder mode" become a euphemism for micromanagement. He sits between Chesky's "stay in the details" maximalism and the "hire great people and get out of their way" orthodoxy. ([What Founder Mode Really Means](https://creators.spotify.com/pod/profile/y-combinator/episodes/What-Founder-Mode-Really-Means-e2r2h96); his reading of their debate, not an original coinage.)
- **vs. Peter Thiel (convergence by a different route).** His contrarian-insight test — "Something you know that you think is right and other people think is wrong is normally an idea that works" ([Frederick AI](https://www.frederick.ai/blog/michael-seibel-twitch)) — is the same *shape* as Thiel's "what important truth do very few people agree with you on?" but reached via problem-passion and customer desperation, not Thiel's monopoly/secrets lens. Flag as agreement reached differently, not a disagreement.
- **vs. local/regional startup "experts," accelerators, and growth gurus.** He names a class of advisor whose incentives diverge from the founder's: local ecosystems exist to create jobs and stimulate the local economy, so they won't tell you to leave — which pairs with his "I'd say move [to the Bay Area]" stance. A sharp disagreement with the build-where-you-are school. (Position documented; "The Problem With Startup Experts" wording lower-confidence.)
- **vs. the credentialist / "join a group of smart people" path.** Against optimizing for prestige: "they perform average... we tell them, you got to rip that blanket off." ([Wharton FinTech](https://medium.com/wharton-fintech/y-combinators-michael-seibel-dalton-caldwell-lessons-from-5000-entrepreneurs-be77ba6a6970)) Cuts against advisors who counsel safe, high-status career moves.
- **vs. valuation-maximizers (and many VCs).** Against treating a high valuation/large round as the win, he treats fast-close-then-build as the win and a fat round as a self-deception risk, and generalizes it into a counterintuitive claim that over-raising *reduces* the odds of success. He rejects the premise at its root — "raising a round should be a byproduct of success," not success itself, and treating it as a milestone breeds "short term showmanship instead of making something people want" — which sets him against the fundraise-announcement culture of much of the VC/press ecosystem, not just individual over-raisers. ([Fundraising Rounds Are Not Milestones](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/fundraising-rounds-are-not-milestones); [Why Does Your Company Deserve More Money?](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/why-does-your-company-deserve-more-money); [5 Things That Kill Startups Post-Seed](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/8a-the-5-things-that-kill-startups-post-seed-rounds))
- **vs. idea-credit equity splits.** Against founders who hand the idea-originator or the longest-tenured founder a dominant stake, he argues for equal splits because "startups are about execution, not about ideas," rebutting the Bezos/Zuckerberg tenure intuition directly. ([How to Split Equity Among Founders](https://www.michaelseibel.com/blog/how-to-split-equity-among-founders); [Co-Founder Equity Mistakes to Avoid](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/LP-co-founder-equity-mistakes-to-avoid))
- **vs. cap-table-as-magic-ingredient investors and founders.** Rejects any framing that treats collecting famous-investor logos as a substitute for building — "the cap-table cake" fallacy. ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **vs. the "get more traction before applying to an accelerator" school.** He argues a clean, one-month-old company beats a messier one with more traction but a damaged cap table and burned relationship capital — earlier and cleaner over "more proof." ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **vs. "neg-only" anti-YC critics.** Reads a 100%-teardown, 0%-alternative argument as revealing the critic's own incentive (fear-selling), not a genuine, actionable concern — a general filter he applies to advice-givers broadly, not only to YC's critics. ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator))
- **vs. Ryan Bethencourt / IndieBio (institutional, on regulated industries).** Bethencourt's on-record critique — "YC wants to fund tech people that do biotech, rather than embracing what it takes to do biotech... YC doesn't have the expertise to tackle an established industry with regulatory hurdles" — targets the YC model under Sam Altman's presidency (2017), not Seibel by name; but it lands inside his tenure as the core accelerator's CEO and is the sharpest named external counter-position to his "launch fast" doctrine as applied to regulated markets. ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/06/30/yccombinator-biotech-partner-added.html)) Seibel's own later carve-out (regulated industries as an explicit exception, see Blind spots) is a partial, unprompted concession to exactly this critique.

## Blind spots
- **Survivorship bias is structural to the YC canon.** His advice is distilled from a YC-pre-selected sample dominated by winners; critics note YC "glamorize[s] and romanticize[s] the journey" and can "distort founders' sense of natural development timelines," creating pressure to match peers' "rocketship metrics" ([critique compilation](https://medium.com/@markeeters/what-are-some-negatives-with-y-combinator-db367de884ae)). He pre-empts this himself repeatedly — "we broke many, if not all of the rules" and, more precisely, "I bet you can name a startup that has made every one of these mistakes and has still been successful... those tend to be exceptions" ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)) — cite his own disclaimers as the honest framing.
- **One-size-fits-all, though he now names the boundary himself.** The same external critique notes YC's curriculum is "geared toward the widest applicability rather than solving specific company problems," weakest for biotech, deep tech, and regulated/enterprise hardware, where "launch something bad quickly" can be illegal, unsafe, or impossible ([critique compilation](https://medium.com/@markeeters/what-are-some-negatives-with-y-combinator-db367de884ae)). This is corroborated by a named, on-record competing-accelerator critique: Ryan Bethencourt (IndieBio) argued in 2017 that "YC doesn't have the expertise to tackle an established industry with regulatory hurdles, pricing and reimbursement complexities," and that the model "haven't changed... enough to reliably build therapeutics companies" — aimed at YC's institutional strategy under Sam Altman's presidency, not at Seibel by name, though it falls inside his tenure as accelerator CEO ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/06/30/yccombinator-biotech-partner-added.html)). What upgrades this from an inferred to a partly self-acknowledged limit: Seibel himself now names the exception plainly — "in some extremely regulated markets like banking or lending, it's just really hard to launch. You actually have to get approval done before you're even allowed to get customers" ([Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/66-biggest-mistakes-first-time-founders-make)). His frameworks were still forged in, and generalize best to, consumer/mobile (Socialcam, Twitch).
- **"Talk to users" can mislead** in B2B/novel markets where users can't articulate needs for products they've never imagined; the heuristic underweights deep technical or design-led insight.
- **Network/selectivity bias.** Broader YC criticism holds funding skews toward a familiar profile (Stanford/ex-Google/ex-YC), so advice optimized for winning inside that system may not transfer to outsiders ([negatives compilation](https://medium.com/@markeeters/what-are-some-negatives-with-y-combinator-db367de884ae)).
- **Internal tension on bias.** His "bias... is that the thing that's gonna prevent you from making your company work? No" ([Wharton FinTech](https://medium.com/wharton-fintech/y-combinators-michael-seibel-dalton-caldwell-lessons-from-5000-entrepreneurs-be77ba6a6970)) sits in tension with his own "access not talent" thesis — readable as downplaying structural barriers even while championing access. He also notes the real cost some carry ("Imagine being a 25 year old and being the safety net for your family?"), which complicates his "go all-in in your 20s" default. Surface as internal tension, not external scandal.
- **"Move to the Bay Area" (2019, reaffirmed in a later essay) has aged unevenly** against remote/distributed reality; treat as a repeated, not merely one-off, position rather than current universal doctrine.
- **The "YC's door is default open" claim sits next to his own admission the program's mechanics are deliberately opaque.** "A bunch of the stuff we do is meant to be secret. I don't know how you market that." ([Dalton & Michael: The Truth About Y Combinator](https://www.ycombinator.com/library/IU-dalton-michael-the-truth-about-y-combinator)) Open access to apply is not the same as legible mechanics once inside — a real, self-stated tension rather than a contradiction resolved.
- **Batch-size critique falls inside his tenure but is not attributed to him by name.** Reporting on YC's "identity crisis" documents batch sizes growing from a combined 42 companies across both 2009 cohorts to 400 in the single Winter 2022 batch, with named critics (Arpita Agnihotri; Masha Bucher of Day One Ventures) saying "the core base of Y Combinator has diluted" and batches feel "overwhelming." The reporting credits the *fix* to Garry Tan as the incoming CEO and never quotes or names Seibel — the Winter 2022 run-up falls within his 2016–2024 tenure as accelerator CEO by outside dating only; treat any link to Seibel personally as inference, not a documented critique of him. ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91279425/y-combinator-startup-accelerator-identity-crisis))
- **His own company lived the co-founder dysfunction he now teaches against.** By his co-founders' own account, Justin.tv ran for years on undocumented decision-making, no clear roles, and a "constant war of attrition" over authority — precisely the "relationship debt" pattern he now names and coaches founders to pay down early. His teaching material reads as post-hoc correction of his own lived failure, not abstract theory. ([Twitch Co-Founders Reunion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vG-Sxk-c2k))
- **Self-named psychological quirk, not a universal prescription.** He names his own attraction to chaos and crisis as a personality trait he doesn't fully endorse generalizing — "I don't know that most people would want that... why is that attractive" ([Twitch Co-Founders Reunion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2vG-Sxk-c2k)) — worth flagging when his "do things that don't scale" and "put yourself in the fire" material is applied to founders who don't share that temperament.
