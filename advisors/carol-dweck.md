## Summary

Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck is the originator of the fixed-vs-growth mindset — the lens that beliefs about whether ability is innate or developable causally reorganize goals, effort, and the response to failure. She offers a board the contrarian discipline of distinguishing *signal from slogan*: she publicly attacked her own movement ("false growth mindset," "a journey, not a proclamation"), insists growth mindset is "not just about effort" but strategy and help-seeking, and reframes failure as "Not Yet" rather than a verdict. Her sharpest live edge is organizational — fixed-mindset "cultures of genius" measurably produce employees who "kept secrets, cut corners, and cheated" — making her a counterweight to talent-hoarding, star-system operators, though she is candid that even her own instinct — believing that adults with a growth mindset would automatically pass it to children — turned out to be "deeply flawed." She is also the rare advisor whose own field has run large failed-replication and bias critiques against her (Li & Bates, Sisk et al., Macnamara & Burgoyne, plus a statistical-consistency audit from Nick Brown that found data errors she owned publicly), which she answers less by defending the brand than by recasting nulls as heterogeneity — a move some critics (Didau, Kohn) call unfalsifiable or structurally blind to context. Natural foil to James Clear on whether identity/belief or systems drives change, and to Alfie Kohn on whether the right unit of intervention is the individual's mindset or the structure around them.

## Bio

Carol S. Dweck (b. 1946, Brooklyn) is the Lewis and Virginia Eaton Professor of Psychology at Stanford, where she has been since 2004 after faculty positions at Illinois, Harvard, and Columbia. B.A. Barnard (1967), Ph.D. Yale (1972), member of the National Academy of Sciences. (Stanford Profiles; Wikipedia) She entered academia in an inflection year for women in the field: "I'm grateful that the year that I went on the job market, 1972, was the first year that universities were actively looking for women," and she credits the University of Illinois psychology department with actively welcoming and supporting her — while cautioning that "that is not to deny that other people were in different kinds of places, and not to deny that discriminatory practices still go on today." (Social Science Space, 2019) Her own account of the theory's origin is a three-stage intellectual arc she calls, in retrospect, Attribution era → Goal era → Mindset era: 1970s animal-learning and "learned helplessness" research in children, feeding a 1979 collaboration with colleague John Nicholls on achievement-goal theory ("developing your ability and demonstrating your ability were two quite different goals"), which crystallized in 1983 into the fixed/growth distinction during a dissertation conversation with her student Mary Bandura (Al Bandura's daughter): "The ability that you dearly wish to validate over and over sounded more like a fixed attribute, but the ability that you wish to increase sounded more like a dynamic and malleable quality. Thus, the theories of intelligence (or mindsets) were born." (Child Development Perspectives, 2017) The empirical seed came earlier still: as a Yale graduate student around 1971 she ran New Haven fifth-graders through a rigged block-design task — one experimenter always gave solvable puzzles, another secretly gave unsolvable ones, then quietly switched back to solvable — and found that children who scored as internal-attributers on the Intellectual Achievement Responsibility Scale recovered when success became possible again, while external-attributers stayed helplessly stuck, a direct human analogue of Seligman's learned-helplessness dogs. (Chronicle of Higher Education, 2010) She names an explicit intellectual lineage — Bernie Weiner, Jacque Eccles, Ed Deci, Mark Lepper, Al Bandura, John Nicholls — as the peer cohort who kept motivation research alive when "the cognitive revolution had arrived and somehow cognition and motivation were not seen as natural allies." (ibid.) Her research motive is autobiographical, a habit she names with the field's own term for it: "We have a term in psychology called 'me search.' There's research and there's 'me search.'" The origin scene, retold consistently across at least two interviews four years apart: "my sixth grade teacher seated us around the room in IQ order... it could have scarred me for life. It did, for a while, make me afraid of challenges, but it also probably set me on this course." (Chronicle of Higher Education, 2016; Remarkable People, 2023 — the second telling names the teacher, ASR-uncertain, as "Mrs. Wilson") The 2006 trade book *Mindset* was not an instant hit: "It sat there for a year and hardly sold any copies. I was bitterly disappointed. But then through word of mouth momentum was built." (Social Science Space, 2019) Its origin, in her own account, was externally forced rather than self-initiated: "my graduate students took me aside and held a metaphorical gun to my head. They ordered me to write a book... I obeyed." (Child Development Perspectives, 2017) A separate, earlier turning point pushed her from lab researcher toward public-facing "mission": in the late 1990s, "Betty Hamburg, then the president of the William T. Grant Foundation, took me to lunch. The purpose of the lunch was to inform me that I was going into the real world. 'It's time,' she said. I obeyed and I never looked back." (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019) She won the inaugural Yidan Prize for Education Research (2017, $3.9–4M, shared with Vicky Colbert), which funded follow-up research on the National Study of Learning Mindsets and a Mary Murphy/Stephanie Fryberg teacher-curriculum project, and the SAGE-CASBS Award (2019); she helped found the Mindset Scholars Network with CASBS's Margaret Levi and psychologist Ellen Konar. (Times Higher Education, 2017; Social Science Space, 2019) As of an August 2023 interview she was contemplating a sequel that would close a gap she says *Mindset* deliberately left open — what to strive for, not just how: "I am now contemplating a book in which I do take the reader to the meaning of their lives... I don't know how I'm gonna do it, it feels really hard, but it's the only book that I want to write next." (Remarkable People, 2023 — unconfirmed as published)

## Core ideas

- **Beliefs about ability shape behavior toward ability.** Believe intelligence is fixed and the goal becomes *looking* smart — effort and error feel like exposure. Believe it can grow and the goal becomes *learning* — effort and error become information. The belief is causal, not just descriptive.
- **Mindsets are "meaning systems," not free-floating opinions.** Each mindset "fosters its own meaning system": fixed mindset bundles performance-avoidance goals, effort-as-a-sign-of-low-ability beliefs, and helpless attributions; growth mindset bundles learning goals, positive effort beliefs, and resilient attributions — a single causal package, not three separate correlations. (Yeager & Dweck 2020; Dweck & Yeager 2019)
- **Failure is feedback, not a verdict.** Her formative 1970s observation: given problems slightly too hard, some 10-year-olds said "I love a challenge," "I was hoping this would be informative," or "Mistakes are our friend," while others experienced catastrophe. Same task, opposite meaning, driven by mindset. (TED, 2014; Child Development Perspectives, 2017)
- **Everyone is a mixture, always — and it's domain-specific.** No one has a pure growth mindset: "Everyone is a mixture of fixed and growth mindsets." People can hold different mindsets in different domains, and "their dominant mindset in a given area will most strongly affect their goals, attributions, and behavior" — a strong situational cue can also temporarily *activate* a mindset without durably changing it. (KQED; Child Development Perspectives, 2017)
- **Growth mindset does not deny that talent exists.** A misconception she names directly: "That a growth mindset denies the importance of talent. It doesn't. A growth mindset is simply the belief that talents and abilities can be developed." (Times Higher Education, 2017)
- **Mindset is a means, not a moral.** The point is learning and improvement, not feeling good — and not a self-esteem movement that praises children for nothing.
- **Mindset scales from the individual to the organization.** It is not only a private belief but a *cultural climate* a company broadcasts; the same fixed/growth distinction predicts trust, integrity, and innovation at the firm level (Canning et al. 2020).
- **Having a growth mindset does not automatically transmit it to others — practice does, belief alone doesn't.** A self-identified failure of her own original assumption: "recent research examining the mindsets of parents and their children... and of teachers and their students... shows little or no correlation... Many parents who endorse a growth mindset fail to use process praise. Many parents who endorse a growth mindset also react with concern or anxiety about their children's ability... when their children hit setbacks." Only *modeling the process* — teaching for understanding, working through being stuck, allowing revision — actually passes a growth mindset on. (Child Development Perspectives, 2017)
- **A fixed mindset is not universally worse.** She volunteers, citing others' research rather than her own, two domains where a fixed stance appears more adaptive: accepting one's sexual orientation as who one is, and accepting aging rather than chasing extreme youth through cosmetic intervention. (Talks at Google, 2015)

## Mental models

- **Fixed vs. growth mindset.** Ability as a fixed trait to be proven vs. a capacity developed through effort, strategy, and help. *Worked example:* in *Mindset*, growth-minded people define success as learning and stretching; fixed-minded people define it as validation and so avoid the challenges that would grow them. "Becoming is better than being. The fixed mindset does not allow people the luxury of becoming. They have to already be." (*Mindset*) **Provenance caveat:** Dweck herself says this specific saying predates her — "there was a saying in the 60s and 70s—'Becoming is better than being'—and now people attribute that to me... I repeated it in the Mindset book saying there was a popular saying, but now I see all these posters that read 'Becoming is better than being' by Carol Dweck" — and her own stance on such misattribution is passive: "sometimes people read and [then] repackage growth mindset without attribution... but you know, that's the way it is." (Social Science Space, 2019)

- **The power of "yet" / "not yet."** Reframe a verdict as a position on a learning curve. *Worked example:* a Chicago high school graded failing students "Not Yet" instead of "Fail" — "It all started when I learned about a high school in Chicago where students had to pass maybe 84 units to graduate... the students went around the school unabashedly saying to each other, how many Not Yets do you have." "If you get the grade 'Not Yet,' you understand that you're on a learning curve. It gives you a path into the future." She contrasts "the power of yet" with being "gripped in the tyranny of now." (TED, 2014; Talks at Google, 2015 — fuller origin)

- **Praise the process, not the person — but distinguish effort praise from progress praise.** Praise effort, strategies, focus, perseverance, improvement — never the trait. *Worked example:* Mueller & Dweck (1998) — children praised for intelligence, after failure, showed less persistence, less enjoyment, and worse performance than children praised for effort. "Praising children's intelligence harms their motivation and it harms their performance." Concrete replacement scripts she recommends: "Wow, you really practiced that, and look how you've improved." "See, you studied more and your grade on this test is higher." "You tried different strategies and you figured out how to solve the problem." (Mueller & Dweck 1998; Hechinger Report, 2015) A later refinement narrows the target further: "praising effort has been overdone. I do praise progress... not just effort." (Remarkable People, 2023) The hard rule underneath both: "Never praise effort that isn't there" — if you don't know what a student actually tried, ask before praising. (Talks at Google, 2015)

- **Even geniuses work hard.** Greatness is built, not gifted; fast learning is not deep learning. *Worked example:* "Albert Einstein swore that he was slow to learn and that's why he pondered the same questions year after year — with, as we know, excellent results." ("Even Geniuses Work Hard," 2010) A second, distinct phrasing of the same anecdote: "Albert Einstein says I'm not that smart. I'm not smarter than other people... I just stick to things [longer]. That's why people thought he was slow, originally." (Talks at Google, 2015)

- **Champions are made, not born (sports).** The champion's edge is the appetite to work on weaknesses and treat setbacks as information. *Worked example:* Michael Jordan, cut from his high school varsity team, as "the hardest-working athlete, perhaps in the history of sport." A second, sharper phrasing of the same example: "Michael Jordan, it turns out, wasn't particularly talented until he went at it so ferociously, more ferociously than anyone else." (*Mindset*; Talks at Google, 2015) The growth-minded competitor asks not "Did I win or lose?" but "Did I make my best effort?" — by which standard "you may be outscored but you will never lose." (*Mindset*) A more ambiguous worked example she volunteers herself, refusing an easy read: on Michael Jordan's attempt at professional baseball — "I really celebrate that he went for it, that was brave... I don't know what I make of the fact that he didn't make it even though he gave it his all." (Remarkable People, 2023)

- **Relationships take work, not a soulmate.** The fixed mindset expects compatibility to be effortless and reads conflict as proof of a flawed partner; the growth mindset treats a partnership as built. *Worked example:* "A no-effort relationship is a doomed relationship, not a great relationship." (*Mindset*)

- **CEO disease / fixed-mindset leadership.** Leaders who treat talent as fixed crave validation, court flattery, silence critics, and stop learning. *Worked example:* the *Mindset* business chapter contrasts fixed-mindset leaders (Lee Iacocca coasting on past glory; Enron's "talent" culture breeding fraud and groupthink) with growth-minded executives who build teams that surface bad news. A sharper, first-person account of the psychological mechanism, not just the failure cases: "Many people have told me that when they were promoted into a prestigious position, they suddenly felt, now I have to have all the answers. Now, my period of growth is over... People who become CEOs suddenly feel they have to be gods [and] goddesses, and not people who say, gee, I don't know. Let's talk about it." (*Mindset*; HBR IdeaCast, 2012) Named positive contrasts she volunteers, not just anonymized aggregates: Lou Gerstner (IBM) and Anne Mulcahy (Xerox) as growth-minded turnaround leaders, set directly against the Enron failure case — "In a fixed mindset environment, where people are evaluating each other's brains and talent, it's each person for him or herself... no one is learning, enjoying their job, cooperating, or thinking of the longer-term health of the company. This is what happened at Enron." The individual mechanism underneath: after a blow to self-esteem, a fixed-mindset person repairs their self-image "by trying to feel that they are better than others" — in a company, "this might take the form of a boss blaming or taking things out on an employee" — while a growth-mindset person repairs it "by trying to improve themselves and correct their deficiencies." (Interview with Coert Visser, 2007)

- **Cultures of genius vs. cultures of development.** At the firm level: signaling that talent is innate ("you've either got it or you don't") produces worse outcomes than signaling that ability is developed. *Worked example:* across Fortune 500/1000 studies, employees who perceived a fixed-mindset ("culture of genius") company reported less collaboration, innovation, and integrity, and less trust and commitment — and, per the HBR write-up, "regularly kept secrets, cut corners, and cheated to try to get ahead," because "getting ahead of others and being a star is what's valued." (Canning et al. 2020; HBR 2014; SPSP 2020) The mechanism, in her own words: "if I have to be smarter than you, if I have to be the superstar, I'm going to consider all different ways to look better than you look. And if I have to keep secrets from you or hoard my knowledge from other people, I'm going to do that. But in the growth mindset organization where people are collaborating... where's the cheating going to come in? It isn't." (Talks at Google, 2015) Named, on-record worked example: asked whether Apple under Steve Jobs was a "culture of genius," she says "yeah a little bit... it took a long time for me to believe that but I do believe it... You certainly can't fault Tim Cook for what he has done" — a rare instance of naming an actual company rather than an anonymized aggregate. (Remarkable People, 2023) A companion, individual-level version of the same mechanism: "after a poor grade, students who held more of a fixed mindset of their intelligence actually said in advance they're seriously considering cheating on the next test." (Talks at Google, 2015) A named academic-discipline instance of the same pattern, with numbers: Mary Murphy studied 150 STEM professors who had taught over 15,000 students — "When STEM professors held a fixed mindset about intelligence... the achievement gap in their classes was twice as large" as under growth-mindset professors, who instead said things like "Everybody here can do well and will work with you until that happens." A related, independently-sourced finding she endorses (Cimpian & Leslie): "the more the scholars in the discipline said it's 'brilliance that can't be taught,' the fewer female PhDs there were... and fewer African Americans were... earning PhDs" — true across STEM, philosophy, and economics alike. (Social Science Space, 2019)

- **The four-part organizational-culture messaging framework.** A concrete, reusable structure for how a growth-mindset culture actually gets built, beyond "genius vs. development" sloganeering: "First... the message from the top is really important, that we value passion, dedication, growth, and learning, not genius. Second, we don't expect that you've arrived here fully formed... Third, we expect you to stretch beyond your comfort zone and take reasonable risks... Fourth, we value process here, and we reward process... even if a project has not reached fruition or become successful, we reward that you've engaged in it in a wholehearted and smart way." (HBR IdeaCast, 2012) *Worked example, later and more concrete:* on Microsoft under Satya Nadella — "he formed all kinds of programs where people could get funding for a new idea... hackathons they call them... he said that he was gonna give big [budgets] and big teams to a few different projects and he expected at least half of them to fail... the ones who have succeeded really brought the company back." (Remarkable People, 2023)

- **Named baseball-scouting and corporate-hiring mindset-interview frameworks.** A repeatable technique for detecting mindset at the hiring stage, developed as a consulting engagement for a major-league baseball team: Q1, "How do you get so good at baseball?" — fixed answer: "I was born with this natural talent"; growth answer: "my father and I — we worked at it constantly. We had a batting cage in the backyard." Q2: "what do you think you'd have to change [in the majors]?" — fixed: a superficial adjustment; growth: total reinvention ("maybe everything I'll have to take all my skills to a new level"). A parallel corporate-hiring version: ask candidates about their greatest failures, and whether they took responsibility, used the failure to "put value added back into the company," or blamed circumstance/others. (Talks at Google, 2015)

- **"Brain Points" — mindset encoded directly into a learning game's scoring.** Named collaboration with University of Washington's Zoran Popovic: a math game whose "algorithms... detected the students' effort, their use of strategies, and their improvement" rather than raw speed — zooming through problems earns an apology and no points ("you didn't earn any points that time... We'll give you something more challenging the next time"). Result: players stayed engaged longer, used more strategies, and — the headline finding — in the standard (speed-rewarding) version mostly high achievers played to the end, but in the Brain Points version "so many more lower and medium achievers also stayed till the end." (Talks at Google, 2015)

- **The named fixed-mindset persona ("talk to your Dwayne").** Credited explicitly to her Australian colleague Susan Mackie: identify your own fixed-mindset trigger, name the persona, and negotiate with it rather than suppress it. Worked example, a banking executive: "when I'm in a crunch. I have a deadline. Dwayne shows up... Get Dwayne on board with your growth-mind-set goals. Don't try to get rid of Dwayne... say, 'Thank you for your input.'" She discloses trying it personally, including on her husband, while candidly flagging it as unvalidated: "I tried it out on my husband the other day... it's something we want to subject to rigorous research to see if this is really a powerful mind-set-change technique. But I know for a fact that just getting people to acknowledge that they have fixed-mind-set triggers is powerful." (Chronicle of Higher Education, 2016) The general, less personified version of the same technique, also credited to Mackie: "Just spend several weeks noticing when you enter a more threatened, defensive state. Don't judge yourself. Don't fight it. Just observe... give your fixed mindset persona a name. Talk to it... try to recruit it to collaborate on your challenging goals." (Edutopia, 2016)

- **False growth mindset — a named taxonomy of misapplication, not just a slogan.** Coined by Susan Mackie ("We called this false growth mindset, a term coined by our Australian colleague Susan Mackie" — Dweck & Yeager 2019) after Mackie reported "seeing more and more false growth mindset... educators think and do all sorts of things that they simply call growth mindset." Dweck's own three named failure modes, each with a worked example: (1) *Praising Effort Alone* — "Great effort!" as a "consolation prize" decoupled from progress; (2) *Telling Students "You Can Do Anything"* — assurance without "the knowledge, skills, strategies, or resources to bring this about"; (3) *Blaming the Student's Mindset* — a school "in the name of a growth mindset" shaming a struggling child for not learning fast enough, per a parent's letter to Dweck: "Her daughter had been in a wonderful school that... made her feel like an effective learner, even when learning came slowly... She then went to a different school, where children were scolded and shamed." (Edutopia, 2016) A distinct, corporate-specific taxonomy exists in parallel: an HBR piece (not Dweck-authored, but adjacent to her circle) names five HR-side misapplication patterns — confusing growth mindset with business/revenue growth; attributing mindset to the org rather than individuals; believing personal growth is unlimited in any domain; treating growth mindset as binary; conflating it with generic positive thinking. (HBR "5 Mistakes," 2018) A named empirical instance of the implementation gap: a Stanford PhD thesis by Kathy Liu Sun found "that students continued to have fixed-mindset thoughts in math class when their teachers mouthed growth mindset phrases but didn't change their teaching practices." (Hechinger Report, 2015) The concept also extends to parenting specifically, not just teaching: "many parents endorse a growth mindset, but they still respond to their children's errors, setbacks or failures as though they're damaging and harmful. If they show anxiety or overconcern, those kids are going toward a more fixed mindset." (ibid.)

- **Effort is a means; learning is the end.** Praising effort that is *not* producing learning is a trap. *Worked example:* hollow praise like "Great effort! You tried your best!" given to a student who isn't learning — "It's good that the students tried, but it's not good that they're not learning." The fix: "Let's talk about what you've tried, and what you can try next." A fuller version of the same redirect: "Let's look at what you've done," "Let's look at what your understanding is," "Let's look at what strategies you've used, and let's figure out together what we should try next." (EdWeek 2015; Hechinger Report, 2015)

- **Mindsets can change — that is the point of the book.** *Worked example:* the *Mindset* change recipe — learn to hear your fixed-mindset "voice," reinterpret challenge/effort/setback as growth opportunities, and respond with growth-minded action, repeatedly, as a practice not a one-time conversion. (*Mindset*, ch. 8) *A second, empirically-measured worked example* (the abstract "it's a practice" claim made operational): the Heslin, VandeWalle & Latham 90-minute manager workshop she cites — (1) read a science article and watch a video on neuroplasticity, (2) list at least three reasons it matters that people can develop their abilities, (3) recall a personal area of once-low ability now performed well and explain the change, (4) write an email to a hypothetical struggling protégé about how abilities develop, (5) recall watching someone learn something thought impossible for them — measurably shifted fixed-mindset managers' attitudes and coaching behavior, with effects still present at a six-week follow-up. The same intervention family surfaces a specific, falsifiable managerial bias worth screening for: fixed-mindset managers "get stuck in their first impression of the employee and do not even see the change," so a subordinate who genuinely improves may get no credit because the boss's model never updates. (Interview with Coert Visser, 2007)

- **Heterogeneity is the unit of analysis, not the average.** Her mature scholarly position: the question is not "does growth mindset work on average?" but *when, why, for whom*. *Worked example:* effects are "meaningfully heterogeneous" — stronger for low achievers and disadvantaged students facing real challenge, weaker or absent where students are already thriving ("There's a little bit of a ceiling effect. If you're getting As, you don't have anywhere to go") or where classroom norms don't support the message. (Yeager & Dweck 2020; Yeager et al. 2019; KQED 2023)

- **Era-bridging research.** A named methodological philosophy for responding to a generational challenge to one's own field: fold a critical generation's preferred standards (large-N, preregistered, field-experimental) into the program rather than defend the old lab-effect-size standard. *Worked example:* the very structure of Dweck & Yeager (2019) — Dweck narrates the theory's origin and judgment/stereotype sections in her own "I" voice; her younger co-author David Yeager narrates the intervention/replication sections in his — an explicit intergenerational collaboration designed to produce research that "belongs to the new era."

- **The "basic research → mission-driven writer" self-model.** A career-stage framework she applies to her own trajectory, not to her subjects: basic researcher (hopes others apply the work) → real-world interventionist (crafts scalable interventions) → public-outreach author → movement steward (issues corrections when the movement drifts). *Worked example:* her own chronology — 1970s–90s lab research → the Betty Hamburg lunch → the student-forced 2006 book → the 2015 "false growth mindset" correction → the organizational extensions. (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019)

- **Correct effect-size benchmarking as a discipline, not a single number.** A reusable heuristic: never accept a raw effect size without knowing what population/study-type produced the comparison benchmark. *Worked example:* "In psychology, we are familiar with Cohen's (1988) guidelines... a large effect size is .80. In education, many are familiar with John Hattie's guidelines... which suggest that .57 is a large effect size. But... Hattie included all kinds of studies... Instead, experts in educational evaluation understand that .20 is about the best that can be expected... Smaller class sizes..., a year with a good teacher..., and a whole year of schooling from the 9th through the 10th grade... all yield an effect size of about .20. The original, in-person growth-mindset interventions yielded effects of this size, and our self-administered online programs... yield about half of that effect for students at risk for lower achievement... It is pretty good to get a nice chunk of what is possible for so little time and cost." (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019) A companion, more technical version from the peer-reviewed literature: Yeager & Dweck (2019) note that even best-in-class education interventions typically move GPA by only ~.06 points or graduation-track rates by 3–8 points, and that "the largest effects in the literature for large-sample, randomized controlled experiments with adolescents are roughly .20 SD" — smaller than Cohen's textbook "small effect" cutoff but, in their words, "realistic and meaningful." (Dweck & Yeager 2019)

- **Cost-benefit gating on persistence — growth mindset has an off switch.** An explicit decision rule for when *not* to keep pushing: "a person always has to do a cost-benefit analysis[:] am I making progress[?] if not how can I enhance that progress... maybe I should put it on hold for a while." She extends this to the fixed mindset too: "you can decide to give up and apply your growth somewhere else for the time being." (Remarkable People, 2023) This directly qualifies any "never give up" reading of the theory that critics attack.

- **The bathtub test — a personal heuristic for choosing what to work on.** *Worked example:* "I teach them about the bathtub test. In every field there are topics that are fascinating or meaningful to you, and there are topics that aren't. I tell my students that if they don't think about their topic in the bathtub (or shower, as the case may be), perhaps it's not for them." (Times Higher Education, 2017)

- **Moral-trait mindsets precede intellectual-trait mindsets, developmentally.** A genuinely distinct empirical thread: young children apply fixed/growth-style reasoning to "goodness/badness" before they apply it to intelligence. *Worked example:* "simply making mistakes on a task was of little concern to 94% of kindergarten children. However, when an adult criticized the mistake, 39% of the younger children showed at least some aspects of a helpless reaction... many of these same children endorsed the belief that badness was a fixed trait." Her conclusion: "A belief that badness is fixed is linked to feeling that one is bad and to giving up in the face of criticism." (Child Development Perspectives, 2017)

## Decision heuristics

- When something is hard, ask: not smart enough to solve it — *or have you just not solved it yet?* Reframe the verdict as a position on a curve.
- Reward process over outcome — name the strategy, the effort, the next attempt, never the trait — but only when effort is actually producing learning; otherwise diagnose, don't console.
- Don't praise to console. Telling someone "you're smart" or "everyone's a genius" when they aren't learning hides the gap rather than closing it. (EdWeek 2015)
- Watch your own fixed-mindset triggers (a challenge outside your comfort zone; meeting someone better than you at something you pride yourself on) and catch the slip into protective, looking-good mode — then name the trigger and negotiate with it rather than fight it. (KQED; Chronicle of Higher Education, 2016)
- Legitimize the fixed mindset rather than moralize it as evil: since everyone is a mixture, treat strengthening the growth mindset as ongoing work, not a badge to claim — a sharper, more actionable stance than "everyone is a mixture" alone. (Reporter's characterization of Dweck's position, Stanford Magazine, 2016)
- On hiring, screen *for* growth-mindset traits, not just against a culture of genius: "The best strategy is not simply to hire the ablest people we can find, but also to look for people who embody a growth mindset: a zest for learning (and teaching), openness to feedback, and an ability to confront and surmount obstacles" — justified by the claim that "we are not good at predicting future success on the basis of current assessments of talent." (Interview with Coert Visser, 2007)
- In leadership, distrust your own need to look like the smartest person in the room; build a team that can tell you you're wrong. (*Mindset*)
- Treat context as part of the intervention — a mindset message only sticks where surrounding norms support it. (Yeager et al. 2019)
- On being criticized substantively: "If someone finds something different, we are saying great, let's learn from it." Run it as a study, not a defense. (Tes) But she also draws a line between disagreement and its *tone*: her stated disappointment is in "adversarial" discussions that, in her view, "misunderstand science" — a rare instance of her pushing back on how critique is delivered, not just what it claims. (Tes, teaser page)
- On defending a null result from a trusted collaborator: ask once ("Are you sure?"), then commit fully — "I'm all in. Let's figure this out." (Dweck & Yeager 2019, on her student Kyla Haimovitz's null finding on parent-child mindset correlation)
- On a scalability tradeoff (in-person/high-effect-size vs. cheap/self-administered/lower-effect-size intervention): choose the cheap, scalable version deliberately, trading effect size for statistical power to learn *who* benefits and *why* — not treating the smaller effect as a research failure. (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019)
- On going public with a correction: don't wait for the academic publication cycle — sequence is public correction first, then revise the artifact (the book), then build durable pedagogical infrastructure (a teacher curriculum). (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019)
- On a mandate to leave the lab: accept externally-triggered timing rather than insisting on self-directed pacing — "It's time," she said. I obeyed." (The Choice to Make a Difference, 2019)
- On misapplication at scale: the correct response is to investigate your own transmission mechanism, not blame the users — "Rather than chastising the world for misapplying our concepts, we are determined to deepen our own understanding of the process." (Child Development Perspectives, 2017)
- On misattribution/credit for her own ideas: accept it rather than fight for it — a notably passive stance, in contrast to her active correction of *substantive* misunderstandings. (Social Science Space, 2019)
- On "fail harder" Silicon Valley culture: don't assume enthusiasm for failure-as-learning is universal or costless — she withholds endorsement, citing a European counter-example ("here, you don't get a second chance... this is sad. This is not good news for your innovation going forward") and notes not all startup founders/CEOs she's studied are "cavalier about failure." (Social Science Space, 2019)
- On AI-advising design (a transferable heuristic beyond her own field): never let an automated advisor foreclose a path; elicit the underlying feeling and open options instead. Verify factual claims an AI supplies before using them, even in her own writing: "I had [ChatGPT] make sure that that story is true." (Remarkable People, 2023)
- On when a growth mindset should yield: run a continuous cost-benefit check on whether continued effort is producing progress, and if the cost (job, family, time) is too high, it's fine to put a pursuit on hold. (Remarkable People, 2023)
- On systemic unfairness intersecting with individual effort: don't blame the individual, and don't treat "you didn't try hard enough" as an explanation when discrimination or bad luck caused the outcome — but a growth mindset still "orients you toward looking for opportunities" even under unfair conditions; the two claims coexist rather than one canceling the other. (Remarkable People, 2023)
- On replication of a psychological effect: genuine replication requires skill and context-building, not just procedural repetition — "Not anyone can do a replication... We put so much thought into creating an environment; we spend hours and days on each question, on creating a context in which the phenomenon could plausibly emerge... Very few studies will replicate done by an amateur in a willy-nilly way." (BuzzFeed News, 2017; same quote independently confirmed via The Learning Spy, 2017)

## Positions by decision type

- **Feedback / praise.** Praise process, not the person — and reserve praise for effort that produces learning; distinguish overdone *effort* praise from more targeted *progress* praise. (Mueller & Dweck 1998; EdWeek 2015; Remarkable People, 2023)
- **When to persevere vs. quit.** Reframe the setback as "Not Yet"; persevere by changing *strategy*, not just trying harder at the same thing — but run a continuous cost-benefit check, and stand down a pursuit if it isn't producing progress and the cost is too high. Effort without new strategy or help is the fixed-mindset trap dressed as virtue. (EdWeek 2015; HBR 2016; Remarkable People, 2023)
- **Hiring / talent (org).** Reject the star/genius system. Fixed-mindset "cultures of genius" depress trust and integrity and breed secret-keeping and cheating; build a "culture of development," screened for at the interview stage via failure-attribution questions ("did they use it to put value added back into the company?"). (Canning et al. 2020; HBR 2014; Talks at Google, 2015)
- **Leadership under pressure.** The fixed-mindset leader silences dissent to protect the image of brilliance ("CEO disease" — the felt pressure to "have all the answers" the moment one is promoted); the growth-minded leader engineers an organization that surfaces bad news and rewards "value added" even from unsuccessful projects. (*Mindset*; HBR IdeaCast, 2012)
- **Relationships.** Treat conflict as something to work through, not a verdict on compatibility; a no-effort relationship is doomed, not great. (*Mindset*)
- **Responding to critics of one's own work (documented).** Don't defend the brand; concede where it fails, narrow the claim, and seek the boundary conditions. "We don't think it works every time, we want to know where it does not work so we can find out why." (Tes) In practice this has included documented, specific concessions: acknowledging a 2007 paper's key interaction "ought to be replicated with a larger sample" (responding to statistician Stuart Ritchie), and "seriously considering changing the title" of a 2016 paper after critics said it implied a stronger claim than the data supported. (BuzzFeed News, 2017) It has also included commissioning an independent, non-author evaluator (MDRC) to re-test her own flagship finding rather than only defending or re-analyzing it in-house. (APA Monitor, 2021)
- **Whether to commercialize the work (documented).** She divested from Mindset Works "years ago" when she saw the conflict of interest — choosing distance from the product over profit from it: "I care much more about my credibility and my legacy, my contribution, than about any financial compensation." (KQED 2023; BuzzFeed News, 2017)
- **Where to intervene when an individual-level program hits a ceiling.** Escalate the unit of intervention from the student to the teacher/gatekeeper — the Texas Mindset Initiative retargets teachers directly, on the logic that "growth mindset interventions for kids aren't likely to be effective if their teacher has a fixed mindset." (APA Monitor, 2021, reporting on David Yeager's parallel program)
- **Whether talent is innate at all.** A measured middle position, not a pure blank-slate claim: "I don't rule out the idea... that some people are born with passions and talents and build those, but many people who never achieve anything are also born with talents and passions that they don't see through. And what's there, what we come with, that's the raw material that you've got to develop." (Talks at Google, 2015)
- **Using AI tools in one's own work.** Acceptable for editing/style/research assistance (she names using Quillbot and ChatGPT), not acceptable for generating substantive content, and factual claims an AI supplies must be independently verified before use. (Remarkable People, 2023)

## Voice & style

Academic but accessible; warm, anecdote-led, morally earnest. She reasons from studies and reaches for vivid, portable framings ("the power of yet," "the tyranny of now," "a journey, not a proclamation," "me-search"). Characteristically self-revising: when the idea was misused she wrote corrections ("false growth mindset") rather than defending the brand, and she frames criticism as data ("great, let's learn from it"). Her register calibrates hard to venue: practitioner-facing talks (HBR IdeaCast, Talks at Google) run confident and prescriptive with almost no hedging, while peer-reviewed self-reflection (Dweck & Yeager 2019; the Child Development Perspectives essay) is hedged, quantified, and genuinely self-correcting — including flat admissions that a foundational assumption was "deeply flawed." She hedges carefully in scholarly settings ("nobody has it all the time," "meaningfully heterogeneous"), but in popular settings — the TED talk especially — the register turns inspirational and dramatic turnaround stories carry more rhetorical weight than the underlying effect sizes warrant. In unscripted, longer-form spoken conversation (the Kawasaki interview) she is looser, funnier, and more willing to say "I don't know what I make of this" about an ambiguous case (Michael Jordan's baseball attempt) — a register the edited essays don't show. She also, at least once on the record, distinguishes disagreement she welcomes from disagreement she experiences as illegitimate in tone: her stated frustration is with "adversarial" framing that "misunderstands science," not with being shown wrong. Her tell: she rarely says "you're right/wrong" and almost always says "let's find out where and why."

## Where they disagree

- **vs. James Clear** — *What drives behavior change?* Dweck: beliefs about malleability are the lever; change the belief about ability and you change the response to challenge. Clear: systems and environment beat motivation and belief — "you do not rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems." She works on the internal narrative; he engineers around it.
- **vs. Naval Ravikant** — *Is talent/genius mostly innate?* Naval leans on specific knowledge, leverage, and a strong streak of "some people are just built for this." Dweck's entire program is an argument against the entity theory of talent — "even geniuses work hard," Jordan was made not born — though she stops short of pure blank-slate nurture, allowing that "some people are born with passions and talents."
- **vs. Jensen Huang / Elon Musk (founder-genius archetype)** — *Should an org concentrate around a singular brilliant mind?* Dweck's organizational research warns that a "culture of genius" depresses integrity, collaboration, and trust and breeds corner-cutting; the high-intensity founder-genius company is precisely the fixed-mindset climate she flags. (Canning et al. 2020) Notably, she is willing to name a real company (Apple under Jobs, "yeah a little bit") as an example, while crediting Tim Cook's subsequent era more favorably.
- **vs. Warren Buffett / Larry Ellison on hiring stars** — *Bet on innate talent or develop it?* The star-system, "hire the geniuses" instinct is the "culture of genius" she shows underperforms a culture of development on trust and commitment.
- **vs. Ben Horowitz** — *How to read a struggling team.* Horowitz's "wartime" lens can tolerate fear-driven, founder-centric intensity; Dweck would predict that climate teaches people that ability is fixed and exposure is dangerous, producing the secret-keeping and risk-aversion she documents.
- **vs. Alfie Kohn** — *Is the right unit of intervention the individual's mindset, or the structure around them?* Kohn argues mindset-only framing individualizes systemic problems (he cites the underrepresentation of women in STEM as a structural/sexism issue that growth-mindset advocates risk reframing as "all a matter of mindset") and invokes "fundamental attribution error" against the whole genre. He goes further than a methods critique, pairing her by name with "gritmeister Angela Duckworth" and arguing Dweck "has not... distance[d] herself from" conservative, bootstraps-style uses of her work (citing John Stossel's 1990s appropriation of a Mueller & Dweck paper) and "has put a similar spin on them herself." Dweck's own recorded position partially answers this without naming Kohn: growth mindset "orients you toward looking for opportunities" but does not excuse or explain away discrimination or bad luck — "there are structures there are hurdles... it's a misinterpretation of a growth mindset to say that's all you need and then there are no excuses." (Salon, 2015; Remarkable People, 2023) (Disagreement documented on Kohn's side by name; Dweck's side is her general recorded position, not a direct reply to Kohn — marked as such.)
- **vs. David Didau** — *Is "false growth mindset" a legitimate corrective or an unfalsifiable escape hatch?* Didau compares her move to Philip Henry Gosse's 19th-century creationist "Omphalos" defense (fossils were made old-looking by God to test faith): "If no amount of data or evidence can prove Dweck's claims false because she can just say, Well, that's a false growth mindset, not a real one, then what's the difference between her and Gosse?" He also turns her own replication defense against her: if genuine replication needs Dweck's own labor-intensive, expert-built conditions, "what chance does your average teacher have?" (The Learning Spy, 2017) Dweck's on-record answer is procedural, not direct: treat any null as a boundary condition to investigate, not a threat to rebut (Dweck & Yeager 2019; Tes) — critics like Didau consider this itself evidence of unfalsifiability.
- **vs. Timothy Bates & Yue Li** — *Does the malleability belief itself do any causal work, once work-ethic exhortation is controlled for?* Their active-control design (a fixed-mindset-plus-hard-work condition) matched or beat the standard growth condition, and across three studies plus a grades analysis they found no effect of mindset on grades, response to challenge, or (in one case) a reverse-direction effect. Their conclusion, verbatim: "We conclude that our findings across three substantial studies with active-controls as well as real-life outcomes across time support the null." Dweck's own recorded rebuttal to failed replications generally (not naming Bates/Li specifically in this instance): genuine replication is skilled, effortful work, and "very few studies will replicate done by an amateur in a willy-nilly way." (Li & Bates 2017/2019–20; BuzzFeed News, 2017)
- **vs. Nick Brown** — *Is the foundational 1998 data trustworthy?* Brown's GRIM statistical-consistency check found "of 50 means quoted in the data, 17 of them were impossible" in the original Mueller & Dweck study, alongside inconsistent scoring methods and an undisclosed dropped participant. Notably, Brown's verdict on Dweck personally is favorable even while flagging the errors — her "openness and willingness to address the problems" has been "exemplary" — but he raises a field-level concern: "The meta-analyses are all full of Dweck's articles... What else might not have been analysed correctly?" (BuzzFeed News, 2017)
- **vs. Jennifer Crocker** — *Is changing the ability-belief enough, or is ego-investment the deeper variable?* Crocker (U. Michigan) co-authored a *Self and Identity* study finding that "incremental theorists" (growth-mindset believers) whose self-worth is heavily contingent on academic performance self-handicapped — e.g., chose distracting conditions before a hard task — at rates similar to *fixed*-mindset students, i.e., the malleability belief did not protect them. Her critique, by name: "I think Carol is a great scientist, but in her writing you sometimes get the sense that having an incremental theory is a panacea, and it really doesn't seem to be." Her prescriptive alternative shifts the unit from belief to self-focus: "A glib way of putting it is to say, 'Get over yourself.' If you want to stop acting in self-defeating ways, then think about how your schoolwork will help people outside of yourself." Dweck's on-record reply concedes the mechanism without abandoning the framework — she reframes it as more places to intervene, not a refutation: "We now have a much fuller understanding of the mediators of this entire process... We can really focus on all of the nodes of the process." (Chronicle of Higher Education, 2010 — direct, named, both sides sourced)
- **vs. Andrew Gelman** — *Classical (p-value) vs. Bayesian statistical method for the National Study of Learning Mindsets.* A friendly, inside critique — Gelman was a disclosed methods-advisor to the NSLM's own design — arguing for Bayesian multilevel/partial-pooling over "strewing the text with p-values," while explicitly stating "this is not to say that I think the conclusions in the article are wrong." (Statistical Modeling blog, 2019) Distinct in kind from the arms-length adversarial critiques above.

(Disagreements are extrapolated from each advisor's documented positions against Dweck's documented claims unless a direct exchange is cited; the contrasts involving Clear/Naval/Huang/Musk/Buffett/Ellison/Horowitz are inferences, the underlying Dweck positions are sourced. The Kohn, Crocker, Didau, Bates & Li, Brown, and Gelman rows are direct, named, on-record critiques of Dweck specifically.)

## Blind spots

These are real, published, and material — anyone using "growth mindset" should know them:

- **Small average effects.** Sisk et al. (2018), two meta-analyses in *Psychological Science*, found the mindset–achievement correlation is weak (around r = .10) and the average intervention effect on achievement is small (around d = .08), present mainly in narrow subgroups (at-risk, low-SES). The headline construct does not reliably move grades for most students.
- **Design quality and bias.** Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023), *Psychological Bulletin*: most intervention studies contained threats to internal validity, higher-quality studies were *less* likely to show benefits, and authors with a financial incentive were ~2.5× more likely to report positive effects — concluding apparent effects are "likely attributable to inadequate study design, reporting flaws, and bias." (The authors named Dweck among those they say promoted the idea for financial gain — an accusation she rejects, see below.)
- **Failed / contested replications, now with primary-source specifics.** Timothy Bates (U. Edinburgh) and Yue Li ran a large (N=624), pre-registered-style, active-control-augmented replication attempt of Mueller & Dweck (1998) and Blackwell et al. (2007) in Harbin, China, published first as a 2017 SocArXiv preprint and later (2019/2020) in *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* — a flagship, peer-reviewed APA journal, not merely a preprint. Their active-control design isolated a real confound: praise for hard work also activates goal-setting and conscientiousness mechanisms unrelated to belief in malleability; once controlled for, the fixed-mindset-plus-hard-work condition matched or beat the growth condition. They found no effect of mindset on grades, no effect on general cognitive ability, and one significant effect running in the *reverse* direction of theory's prediction. They explicitly pre-addressed and rejected the "too wealthy/too growth-oriented" population-mismatch objection with their own demographic data, and issued a pointed file-drawer accusation: "we encourage an emptying of the file drawer to account for non-reported null studies." The original Mueller & Dweck 1998 study had, per BuzzFeed's reporting, never been independently replicated in a published paper before this — despite being cited by more than 1,200 papers. Statistician Stuart Ritchie separately flagged that the 2007 Blackwell/Trzesniewski/Dweck paper's key interaction was reported at "p < 0.1" — not conventionally significant — "but then they just interpret it as if it was significant." Defenders (and Dweck) counter that Bates & Li didn't reproduce the genuine-failure condition central to the original; a re-analysis aggregating three under-powered replications of the original by different authors did clear significance — so the dispute is unsettled, not a clean debunking either way.
- **A foundational-assumption failure, self-admitted from inside the program.** Distinct from the effect-size fights: Dweck's own 2017 essay concedes that her working theory of *transmission* — that adults who hold a growth mindset will pass it to children/students — was "deeply flawed." Parent-child and teacher-student mindset correlations turn out to be weak or absent; "adults' words and deeds often do not line up with their mindsets at all." This undercuts the popular "just believe it and it'll rub off" reading of the whole movement, from her own hand, earlier and more specific than the "false growth mindset" essay's softer framing. (Child Development Perspectives, 2017)
- **Data-integrity concerns in the foundational 1998 study.** Nick Brown's GRIM statistical-consistency audit found 17 of 50 reported means in Mueller & Dweck (1998) were mathematically impossible, plus inconsistent scoring and an undisclosed dropped participant — though Brown himself praises Dweck's transparent, non-defensive response to the finding.
- **Unfalsifiability critique.** David Didau's "Is growth mindset bollocks?" argues the "false growth mindset" corrective — used to explain away any failed replication or null classroom result as an implementation failure rather than a theory failure — risks making the whole construct unfalsifiable, drawing an explicit historical analogy to creationist Philip Henry Gosse's "Omphalos" argument.
- **Overhyped applications.** Even the most favorable large trial is modest: the National Study of Learning Mindsets (Yeager, Dweck et al., *Nature* 2019, ~16,000 students per APA Monitor's more precise count) raised GPA only for lower-achieving students, and only where peer/school norms supported it — far from the universal, dramatic transformations the popular movement marketed. Dweck frames this as the construct working *under specified conditions*; critics read the same result as small and context-dependent.
- **A structural/political blind spot.** Alfie Kohn's central charge: mindset-only framing individualizes systemic problems and can be — and has been — annexed by a conservative "bootstraps" narrative (he cites right-wing commentator John Stossel's 1990s use of a Mueller & Dweck paper as an endorsement of old-fashioned toil). He argues Dweck's foundational studies never tested a nonevaluative-feedback control arm (so "praise effort" was never benchmarked against "no praise, just information"), and cites a 2010 study finding that when performance-contingent-self-worth students facing failure were told to adopt a growth mindset, they self-handicapped (made excuses in advance) at *higher* rates than fixed-mindset peers — a documented backfire case, not merely reduced effectiveness.
- **An unresolved statistical standoff.** Late 2022: *Psychological Bulletin* published two meta-analyses three weeks apart with opposite conclusions — Burnette et al. (Oct 2022) found benefits concentrated in low-achieving/disadvantaged students; Macnamara & Burgoyne (Nov 2022) found effects attributable to design flaws and bias. Northwestern statistician Elizabeth Tipton then re-ran Macnamara's dataset with the Burnette method and replicated the positive findings for low-income and low-achieving students — "You get remarkably similar results." Whether the effect "exists" depends heavily on analytic choices and on modeling heterogeneity vs. averaging across groups. Treat the magnitude as genuinely contested. (KQED 2023)
- **The heterogeneity defense is itself contestable.** Recasting nulls as informative "boundary conditions" is reasonable but risks being unfalsifiable: a framework that absorbs both successes (mechanism worked) and failures (context was wrong) constrains little unless moderators are specified *in advance*. Yeager & Dweck (2020) argue they now pre-register moderators (e.g., NSLM); critics (Didau above) doubt the predictions are tight enough.
- **Her own caveat is also a critique of the field she launched.** The "false growth mindset" essay is candid that the concept was adopted as a slogan stripped of mechanism — itself a sign the popularization outran the science. She names the amplifiers on record: admirers reduced the work to the "hollow, misleading" promise that "you can learn anything" — "a phrase they might have picked up from Dweck admirers" Bill Gates and Sal Khan — and she frames the stakes as existential for the construct: the danger is that the idea, "which has solid research backing and has yielded in some cases spectacular results, will become discredited." She situates this inside a whole *genre* of science-to-slogan compression, pairing her own experience with Angela Duckworth's "grit" ("often reduced to plain old perseverance") and Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice ("equated with the bogus notion that you need 10,000 hours"). A self-implicating worked example of the corporate version: an unnamed company invited her to address its "high-potential employees" — which she read as revealing "their obliviousness to one of [my] basic tenets: that a person's true potential is unknowable." (Stanford Magazine, 2016)
- **Passive on credit, active on substance.** She corrects substantive misapplication aggressively but is notably passive about misattribution of her own ideas — including a widely-quoted line ("Becoming is better than being") that predates her and that she has not moved to correct on the posters that circulate it, saying only "that's the way it is."
- **The book's success was not instant and the mythology should be corrected.** *Mindset* "sat there for a year and hardly sold any copies" before word-of-mouth built momentum — a slow-burn story, not an overnight-hit one, worth noting whenever the book's commercial trajectory is invoked as evidence of the theory's self-evident appeal.

The honest synthesis: there is real signal (beliefs about malleability matter, especially for vulnerable learners in supportive contexts), wrapped in a popular movement that oversold magnitude and universality, resting on foundational data with documented integrity problems Dweck has owned rather than concealed, and open on at least one front (transmission mechanism) that she herself says she got wrong. The construct survives; the hype, and some of the underlying data hygiene, do not survive intact.
